Shekha Jheel Bird Sanctuary as India’s 99th Ramsar Site

25-04-2026

Source: PIB 

The Union Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change has designated Shekha Jheel Bird Sanctuary in Aligarh, Uttar Pradesh, as a Ramsar site (Wetland of International Importance), taking India’s total Ramsar sites to 99 and the State’s tally to 12. 

  • Origin: It is a 25-hectare freshwater perennial wetland that came into existence in 1852 following the construction of the Upper Ganga Canal, which divides the lake into two parts. 
  • Ecological Significance: The wetland acts as a crucial wintering habitat for over 166 water bird species, including important species like the painted stork and bar-headed goose, alongside mammalian fauna such as the blackbuck (a Schedule I species) and blue bull. 
  • Flora & Vegetation: The dominant tree species include Terminalia arjuna and Syzygium cumini, but the native ecosystem is increasingly threatened by invasive alien species such as Lantana camara, Parthenium hysterophorus, and Water Hyacinth. 
  • Anthropogenic Threats: The wetland is degrading due to siltation (caused by unscientific mound construction in 1991), loss of forest cover due to land distribution schemes, and increased poaching vulnerabilities facilitated by nearby road construction.  
    • Additionally, aggressive water chestnut farming has reduced the foraging surface area for migratory birds. 
  • Eutrophication: The profuse and uncontrolled growth of water hyacinth is causing severe eutrophication, deteriorating the water body's health and reducing habitats for waterfowl.

World Elephant Day 2026

14-08-2026

Source: PIB

Why in News?

India reaffirmed its commitment to elephant conservation during the national World Elephant Day 2026 celebrations in Visakhapatnam, highlighting technology-driven habitat protection, community participation and coordinated measures to address human-elephant conflict.

What are the Key Facts about World Elephant Day? 

  • About: World Elephant Day is observed annually on 12 August to raise awareness about the conservation and protection of Asian and African elephants.
  • Origin: Patricia Sims of Canada and the Elephant Reintroduction Foundation of Thailand jointly established World Elephant Day on 12th August 2012. Since then, Patricia Sims continues to lead the initiative.
  • Theme 2026: “Bringing the world together to help elephants.”
  • Objective: The observance seeks to highlight threats such as habitat loss and fragmentation, poaching and illegal ivory trade, human-elephant conflict, loss of migration routes and mistreatment in captivity, while promoting conservation, better habitat protection and peaceful coexistence between people and elephants.

Key Initiatives Announced on World Elephant Day 2026

  • Regional Action Plan for Southern India: A ₹500-crore plan covering Karnataka, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu was released to strengthen corridor conservation, habitat restoration, early-warning systems, conflict mitigation and community participation.
  • Model Elephant Conservation Plan: A Model Elephant Conservation Plan for the Nilgiri Elephant Reserve was released to guide scientific conservation and management.
  • Captive Elephant Welfare Guidelines: The publication “Captive Elephant Husbandry: Science, Welfare and Practice” was released to strengthen standards for captive elephant care.
  • Gaj Gaurav Awards 2026: Conferred on individuals for exemplary contributions to elephant conservation and management.
  • “30 Giant Steps” Publication: Released to document 30 major milestones, achievements and conservation initiatives under Project Elephant between 2020 and 2026.
  • HEC Research Expansion: Phase II of the human-elephant conflict study covering Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Odisha and West Bengal was launched.

Key Facts About Elephant

  • Three species: There are three different species of elephant: the African Savannah elephant, the African Forest elephant and the Asian elephant. 
    • African elephants have larger ears shaped like Africa; Asian elephants’ ears resemble the Indian subcontinent. African elephants have two trunk “fingers”; Asian elephants have one.
  • World’s Largest Land Animal: The African Savanna (Bush) elephant is the world's largest land animal.
  • Elephant Lifespan and Reproduction: Elephants can live up to 65 years. Females reach puberty at 11, have a 22-month pregnancy, and stay fertile into their late 40s. Under ideal conditions, populations can grow by 7% annually.
  • Social Structure: A matriarch, usually the oldest and most respected female, leads the family.
  • Tusks: Enlarged incisor teeth that grow for life. They are used for feeding, digging, and defence; targeted for ivory, making elephants vulnerable to poaching.
  • Communication: Use sound, body language, touch, scent, and seismic vibrations detected through bones.
  • India’s Population: India is home to nearly 60% of the world’s wild Asian elephant population, giving it a major responsibility in global elephant conservation.
  • SAIEE 2021–25: The Synchronous All India Population Estimation of Elephants (SAIEE) 2021–25 estimated India’s wild elephant population at 22,446, protected across 33 Elephant Reserves and 150 identified corridors.
  • Population Decline: 90% of African elephants lost in the past century. Asian elephant populations are down by at least 50%. Habitat loss disrupts migration routes and increases human-elephant conflict.

What Steps Has India Taken to Strengthen Elephant Conservation?

Project Elephant

  • About: The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change implements Project Elephant as India’s flagship elephant-conservation programme, providing financial and technical assistance to States for habitat protection, captive-elephant conservation and mitigation of human-elephant conflict, while promoting AI-enabled monitoring, radio-collaring, genetic databases and early-warning systems.
  • DNA-Based Population Assessment: The Synchronous All India Population Estimation of Elephants (SAIEE) 2021–25 used India’s first DNA-based synchronised elephant census, analysing dung samples through genetic fingerprinting to identify individual elephants, detect repeat samples and improve population estimates alongside block counts and line-transect surveys.

Elephant Corridor Network

  • Ecological Connectivity: Elephant corridors connect natural habitats that have become fragmented due to human development and other factors, enabling elephant movement between fragmented habitats.
  • Population Viability: By maintaining connectivity between habitats, corridors support healthy and genetically diverse elephant populations and improve their long-term survival prospects.
  • Conflict Reduction: Securing traditional movement routes helps reduce human-elephant conflict by allowing elephants to move through connected landscapes with fewer interactions with settlements and infrastructure.
  • National Corridor Network: Under Project Elephant, the Government has identified and validated 150 elephant corridors across 14 elephant-range States.
  • Regional Distribution: According to Elephant Corridors of India, 2023, prepared with technical support from the Wildlife Institute of India, the East-Central region has 52 corridors, the North-East 48, the Southern region 32 and the Northern region 18.
    • West Bengal has the highest number of elephant corridors, with 26 corridors, accounting for more than 17% of the national total.
  • Inter-State and Transboundary Connectivity: The network includes 19 inter-State corridors and six transboundary corridors connecting India and Nepal.
  • Status of Corridor Use: Monitoring recorded increased elephant use in 59 corridors (40%), stable use in 29 (19%), declining use in 29 (19%), while 15 corridors were reported as impaired.
  • Conservation Measures: The Government is strengthening corridor conservation through scientific monitoring, boundary delineation and integration of corridors into State working and management plans.

Growing the Reserve Network

  • Expansion of Reserve Network: Elephant Reserves have expanded steadily since 2014 and now cover around 80,777 km² across 14 major elephant-range States.
  • Additional Area under Protection: The expansion has brought an additional 8,610 km² under the Elephant Reserve network, thereby securing critical habitats used by wild elephant populations.
  • Improved Habitat Connectivity: The enlarged reserve network strengthens ecological connectivity between important elephant landscapes and supports safer movement across fragmented habitats.
  • Scientific Management: Expansion of Elephant Reserves has also strengthened scientific habitat management, monitoring and long-term conservation planning.
  • Landscape-Level Conservation: Project Elephant, scientific population estimation, protected reserves and connected elephant corridors together have transformed elephant conservation into an integrated landscape-level approach.

Management Effectiveness Evaluation

  • Management Effectiveness Evaluation: The Management Effectiveness Evaluation framework for Elephant Reserves was launched in 2023 to assess conservation performance through transparent and measurable indicators.
  • Pilot Assessment of Reserves: The framework was initially tested in four representative Elephant Reserves—Shivalik, Kaziranga–Karbi Anglong, Mayurbhanj and Nilgiri—to evaluate its effectiveness across different elephant landscapes.
  • Nationwide Expansion: Building on the pilot experience, the Government is extending the Management Effectiveness Evaluation framework to Elephant Reserves across the country with support from CAMPA funding.
  • Adaptive Management: The framework seeks to institutionalise transparent assessment and adaptive management by linking conservation interventions with measurable outcomes.
  • Regional Action Plans: The Ministry has developed Regional Action Plans for all elephant-bearing regions of the country to strengthen coordinated conservation at the landscape level.
    • Inter-State Coordination: The plans recognise that elephants move across State boundaries and that habitat connectivity, human-elephant conflict and landscape management require coordinated action across jurisdictions.
    • Common Conservation Framework: The Regional Action Plans provide States with a common framework for undertaking priority conservation and conflict-mitigation interventions at the landscape scale.

Railway Collision Mitigation

  • Identification of Vulnerable Stretches: Joint surveys have covered 127 sensitive railway stretches spanning 3,452.4 km to identify areas prone to elephant-train collisions.
  • Targeted Mitigation Measures: Of these, 77 railway stretches across 14 States, covering 1,965.2 km, have been recommended for specific mitigation interventions.
  • Wildlife-Friendly Infrastructure: The proposed measures involve 705 structures, including underpasses, overpasses, ramps, level crossings and bridge modifications to facilitate safer elephant movement across railway lines.
  • Technology-Based Monitoring: Structural interventions are being complemented by optical-fibre-based intrusion detection systems, seismic sensors and thermal cameras to detect elephant movement near railway tracks.
  • AI-Enabled Early Warning: Distributed Acoustic Sensing systems use AI-enabled monitoring to provide real-time alerts to loco pilots, station masters and railway control rooms, allowing timely preventive action.
  • Joint Investigation Mechanism: Every train collision involving an elephant is jointly investigated with the concerned State Forest Department to identify causes and improve future mitigation measures.
  • Decline in Elephant Mortality: These sustained interventions have helped reduce train-hit elephant deaths from 26 in 2013 to 12 in 2024, representing a decline of more than 50%.

Genetic Monitoring of Captive Elephants

  • Science-Based Monitoring: The Government has strengthened safeguards against the illegal trade and movement of captive elephants through genetic identification, digital records and regulated transfer mechanisms.
  • Legal Protection: Under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, cases relating to the sale, transport and procurement of captive elephants are referred to the concerned State Chief Wildlife Wardens for appropriate action.
  • Gajah Suchana Application: The Gajah Suchana mobile application has created a national genetic database containing the DNA profiles, morphological characteristics and ownership details of every registered captive elephant.
    • Identity Verification: The database helps verify the identity of captive elephants before movement and provides reliable forensic evidence during legal proceedings.
  • Captive Elephant Rules, 2024: The Captive Elephant (Transfer or Transport) Rules, 2024 regulate the transfer and transportation of captive elephants and establish safeguards for their movement.
    • A High-Powered Committee, constituted pursuant to the Supreme Court’s order, examines inter-State transfers of captive elephants.

Community Compensation

  • Ex-Gratia Assistance: The Government provides financial assistance under the Centrally Sponsored Schemes Project Tiger and Elephant and Development of Wildlife Habitats to people affected by human-wildlife conflict.
  • Compensation for Human Casualties: Assistance includes ₹10 lakh for death or permanent incapacitation, ₹2 lakh for grievous injury, and treatment costs of up to ₹25,000 for minor injuries.
  • Compensation for Crop and Property Loss: Financial assistance is also provided for crop and property losses as per the norms prescribed by the respective States and Union Territories.
  • Human-Wildlife Conflict Advisory: The Government has strengthened conflict management through the Human-Wildlife Conflict Advisory, which recommends hotspot mapping, rapid-response teams, State and district-level review committees, and faster compensation.
  • Elephant-Specific Guidelines: Elephant-specific conflict-mitigation guidelines issued in 2023 seek to improve coordination among stakeholders and strengthen human-elephant conflict management.

Conclusion

As India observes World Elephant Day 2026, its elephant conservation approach reflects a blend of civilisational values, modern science, technology and community participation. Habitat protection, corridor restoration, safer infrastructure and support for conflict-affected communities are strengthening coexistence. Under the vision of Viksit Bharat, sustained conservation efforts will be vital to securing the future of the Asian elephant while preserving India’s natural heritage and civilisational legacy for future generations.

Arctic Warming Amplifies Invasive Species Threat

13-02-2026

Source: TH 

Why in News? 

Recent scientific studies warn that rapid Arctic warming is increasing the risk of invasive alien plant species establishing and transforming fragile Arctic ecosystems. 

How is Arctic Warming Increasing the Threat of Invasive Plants? 

  • Changing Climate Conditions: Climate change and rising human activity are weakening the Arctic’s natural barriers, short growing seasons, harsh climate, and limited disturbance, making previously inhospitable regions suitable for non-native species. 
  • Scientific Findings: A recent study estimates that 2,554 vascular plant species from across the world could find suitable climatic niches in a warming Arctic. 
  • Existing Evidence of Spread: An earlier Arctic inventory documented 341 alien taxa, of which 188 have naturalised, and researchers have already observed unexpected non-native species in Svalbard, including common meadow rue. 
  • Vulnerable Hotspots Identified: Six major potential invasion hotspots were identified: western Alaska, southwestern and southeastern Greenland, northern Iceland, Fennoscandia, and the Kanin–Pechora region, where warming and human connectivity intersect. 
  • Pathways of Introduction: Invasive plant introductions occur mainly through escape from confinement, transport-stowaway, seed contamination, transport via vehicles, etc. 
  • Ecological Implications: Alien species that displace native flora threaten the fragile Arctic tundra ecosystem, comprising mosses, lichens, shrubs like bearberry, and unique fauna such as snowy owl, Arctic fox, grizzly bear, seals, walruses, and belugas, and are considered one of the greatest drivers of global biodiversity loss. 

What are Invasive Alien Plant Species? 

  • About: Invasive plant species are non-native plants introduced intentionally or unintentionally into an ecosystem, where they outcompete native species and cause economic, environmental, or human health impacts. 
    • Invasions are being accelerated by climate change, land-use change, altered fire regimes, soil moisture changes, livestock grazing patterns, and biodiversity loss. 
  • Invasive Plant Species in India: Key invaders include Lantana camara, Chromolaena odorata and Prosopis juliflora. 
    • These invasions have spread across 266,954 sq km of natural habitats in India. 
  • High-risk Regions: Include the Shivalik–Terai belt, the Duar region of the Northeast, the Aravallis, the Dandakaranya forests, and the Nilgiris in the Western Ghats.  
    • Open ecosystems such as dry grasslands, savannas, shola grasslands, and the wet plains along the Ganga–Brahmaputra are among the most vulnerable to rapid invasive plant expansion. 

Frequently Asked Question (FAQ) 

1. What are invasive alien species? 
Invasive alien species are plants or other organisms introduced outside their natural habitat that establish, spread rapidly, and cause ecological harm by outcompeting native species and altering ecosystems. 

2. Why is the Arctic more vulnerable to invasions now?                                      Rapid warming and increasing human activity are reducing natural climatic barriers that earlier prevented non-native species from surviving. 

3. Why are invasive alien species harmful?                                                               They reduce native biodiversity, disrupt ecosystem balance, and can permanently change fragile habitats such as the Arctic tundra.

New Dragonfly Species Discovered in Kerala

11-02-2026

Source: TH 

Researchers have identified a new dragonfly species, Lyriothemis keralensis, in Kerala, extending its known range beyond northeast India and highlighting the State’s rich biodiversity. 

  • About: Although present in Kerala since 2013, it was misidentified for over a decade as Lyriothemis acigastra, until detailed microscopic and museum specimen comparisons confirmed its distinct identity. 
  • Features: The species exhibits distinct sexual dimorphism, with males being bright blood-red with black markings and females yellow with black markings. 
  • Seasonality: It is seasonally visible during the Southwest Monsoon (late May to August) and persists as aquatic larvae during the rest of the year. 
  • Concerns: It thrives in human-modified irrigation landscapes such as pineapple and rubber plantations and shaded canals, with most populations occurring outside protected areas, underscoring the need for biodiversity-sensitive land-use practices in plantation-dominated regions.

Rusty-Spotted Cat Sighting in the Aravallis

08-05-2026

Source: TH 

Researchers have documented the first photographic evidence of a rusty-spotted cat (Prionailurus rubiginosus) breeding in the Aravalli landscape of Delhi-NCR and Haryana, confirming active reproduction outside protected areas. 

  • About: The rusty-spotted cat is often referred to as the "hummingbird of the cat family" and is recognised as the world's smallest wild cat species, weighing barely 1 to 1.5 kg.  
    • It is distinguished by its fawn-grey coat with characteristic rust-coloured spots on its back and flanks. 
  • Distribution: It is endemic to the Indian subcontinent, with populations primarily found in India, Sri Lanka, and Nepal.  
    • Notably, India harbours approximately 80% of its global population. 
    • In India, its range extends from Tamil Nadu to Jammu and Kashmir, including Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, and Odisha. 
  • Preferred Habitat: They typically inhabit moist and dry deciduous forests, scrublands, grasslands, and rocky terrains, and are increasingly found adapting to agricultural fringes and human-modified landscapes. 
  • Behaviour: They are primarily nocturnal, highly agile, and arboreal, feeding mainly on insects, rodents, frogs, and small birds.  
    • The sighting in Haryana’s Aravalli scrublands challenges the belief that rusty-spotted cats avoid human presence and highlights the Aravallis’ continued role as a vital biodiversity corridor despite rapid urbanisation.  
  • Conservation Status:  
    • IUCN Red List: Near Threatened 
    • Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972: Schedule I 
    • CITES,: Appendix I (Indian population); Appendix II (Sri Lankan population) 
  • Major Threats: The primary threats include habitat loss due to real estate development, agricultural expansion, and deforestation. They also face high risks from roadkill mortality and retaliatory killings by farmers protecting poultry.
