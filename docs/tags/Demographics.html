Youth Unrest under Neoliberalism

31-10-2026

For Prelims: G7 nations, Neoliberal capitalism, Article 41, Gig economy, Right to Life, Demographic Dividend 

For Mains: Globalisation, social empowerment, demographic changes and social inequalities.

Source:TH 

Why in News?  

Across the globe from G7 nations like France and Spain to countries in the Global South like India, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh a massive wave of youth-led protests is unfolding. These mobilizations are a collective reaction to the systemic failures of neoliberal capitalism, which has weakened public welfare, skyrocketed the cost of living, and worsened youth unemployment.

Summary

  • Youth unrest under neoliberalism reflects growing unemployment, insecure gig work, rising living costs, wealth concentration and intergenerational inequality, threatening social cohesion and democratic stability. 
  • Addressing these challenges requires stronger welfare systems, labour protections, progressive wealth redistribution, future-oriented skill development and green jobs to ensure inclusive growth and intergenerational justice. 

What is Neoliberalism? 

  • About: Neoliberalism is a political and economic ideology that gained global dominance in the late 20th century (post-1970s) as a counter-revolution to the state-led "Golden Age of Capitalism" (1945-1973).  
    • It fundamentally advocates for free-market capitalism, minimal government intervention, and comprehensive economic liberalization. 
  • Key Pillars: 
    • Free-Market Capitalism: The belief that markets operate most efficiently without government intervention. 
    • Withdrawal of the Welfare State: Aggressive reduction in public expenditure, subsidies, and social safety nets. 
    • Deregulation & Privatization: Removing state controls over the financial sector and transferring public utilities to private corporations. 
    • Labor Market "Flexibility": Dismantling labor unions and workers' protections to lower wage costs, effectively using the threat of unemployment (the 'sack') as a disciplinary measure against the working class. 

What are the Youth Concerns Regarding Neoliberalism? 

  • Crisis of Unemployment and Underconsumption: As neoliberalism suppressed wages to maximize corporate profits, the purchasing power of the working class declined.  
    • According to heterodox economists (like Karl Marx's theory of underconsumption), when workers cannot afford goods, capitalists stop investing, leading to a massive lack of labor demand.  
    • Consequently, youth unemployment rates in G7 countries are now almost twice as high as overall unemployment. 
    • According to the India Employment Report 2024, youth account for nearly 83% of the unemployed workforce in India.  
    • This systemic failure contradicts the Directive Principle (Article 41), which mandates the State to secure the Right to Work and public assistance in cases of unemployment. 
  • Commodification of Education: Under neoliberalism, higher education transitioned from a public good to a profit-driven industry.  
    • The youth face skyrocketing tuition fees and massive student debt, only to graduate into a market experiencing "credential inflation" with few remunerative jobs. 
  • Casualization of Labor and Dilution of Rights: Neoliberalism prioritized "labor market flexibility," replacing secure, pensionable, and unionized jobs with contract work, zero-hour contracts, and gig economy roles.  
    • This has stripped the youth of job security, medical benefits, and collective bargaining rights.  
    • This increasing precarity raises serious questions about the state's obligation to protect the Right to Livelihood, which the Supreme Court explicitly read as a fundamental part of the Right to Life (Article 21) in Olga Tellis vs. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985). 
    • While the Code on Social Security, 2020 explicitly recognizes "gig workers" and "platform workers", it falls short of granting them traditional "employee" status, thereby denying them minimum wage guarantees and collective bargaining rights. 
  • Feminization of Precarity: Young women face a double burden disproportionately pushed into unpaid care work as state welfare shrinks, or relegated to the lowest, most insecure tiers of the gig economy. 
  • The AI and Automation:  The youth face general economic stagnation compounded by capital-intensive growth.  
    • Artificial Intelligence (AI) is eroding entry-level white-collar jobs - the traditional first rung of the social mobility ladder for educated youth. 
  • Intergenerational Environmental Injustice: The neoliberal pursuit of endless, resource-intensive growth has externalized environmental costs.  
    • Older generations contributed the most to global warming, but the catastrophic economic and ecological consequences of the climate crisis are being entirely inherited by the youth. 
    • A 2021 study based on IPCC data reveals a child born in 2020 will face 7x more heatwaves and 2.8x more floods than someone born in 1960. 
    • A World Bank report warns that India could account for 34 million of the projected 80 million global job losses from heat stress-related productivity declines by 2030, an economic burden falling directly on the incoming youth workforce. 
  • Asset Price Inflation: Prolonged pro-capital policies have inflated the costs of assets like real estate. The youth are priced out of homeownership and trapped in an economy of "rentier capitalism."  
    • This massive concentration of wealth directly violates Article 38(2) (striving to minimize income inequalities) and Article 39(b) & (c) (mandating that material resources subserve the common good and the economic system does not result in the concentration of wealth). 
  • Dismantling of Social Safety Nets: There has been a systemic transfer of economic risk from the state and corporations onto the individual.  
    • For instance, the shift from state-backed pensions to market-linked retirement funds forces the youth to navigate volatile financial markets for their future security. 
  • Political Alienation: Perceived corruption, nepotism, unequal opportunities and weak political responsiveness erode youth trust in democratic institutions and meritocracy, fuelling protests across Albania and Europe.

How Current Youth Unrest against Neoliberalism Differs from Historical Movements? 

  • Economic Protection vs. Cultural Freedom: In 1968, youth operating within a robust welfare state protested for greater social and cultural freedom. Today, facing neoliberal state withdrawal, youth demand basic economic protection. 
  • The 'Precariat' vs. The Proletariat: Historical movements relied on strong, unionized industrial workers. Today's unrest is driven by the precariat (a scattered, highly indebted class of gig workers and desperate graduates). 
    • The massive 2022 RRB-NTPC railway protests in UP and Bihar perfectly illustrate this, highlighting the extreme desperation of millions of educated youth for a shrinking pool of secure formal jobs. 
  • Leaderless Networks vs. Vanguard Leadership: Past protests were steered by charismatic leaders (like Jayaprakash Narayan in India) and vanguard unions with clear ideological frameworks.  
    • Today’s digital-age mobilizations are decentralized, leaderless, and organized via social media, making them resilient to state crackdowns but often ideologically fragmented. 
  • "Polycrisis" vs. Utopian Optimism: 1960s/70s youth possessed revolutionary optimism to build a utopian society (e.g., the demand for "Total Revolution").  
    • Today’s Gen Z is driven by eco-anxiety and a "polycrisis" mindset (enduring systemic shocks from the 2008 crash, Covid-19, and climate change), protesting out of a desperate need to survive a bleak, insecure future. 

What are the Implications of Rising Youth Unrest? 

  • Economic Disruption and Investment Uncertainty: Prolonged social unrest can disrupt economic activity, undermine business confidence and discourage investment. 
    • According to IMF research (2021), major unrest events were associated, on average, with GDP being approximately 1% below baseline six quarters after the event. 
  • Political Polarisation and Democratic Instability: Persistent economic grievances may intensify political polarisation and create opportunities for extremist or populist political mobilisation.  
    • France's 2026 protests illustrate how socioeconomic discontent can become intertwined with electoral competition and law-and-order politics. 
  • Erosion of Institutional Trust: Unaddressed youth grievances, perceived corruption and inadequate public services can weaken confidence in representative institutions, undermining democratic legitimacy and social cohesion. 
  • Potential for Democratic Renewal: Peaceful youth mobilisation can also promote accountability, institutional reforms and greater political participation by bringing neglected issues into public debate.

What Measures are Needed to Address Intergenerational Inequality under Neoliberalism? 

  • Re-calibrating the Labor Market for the 'Precariat': State intervention is crucial to protect vulnerable workers in the new digital and gig economy.  
    • The government must enforce the 2020 recommendations of the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Labour to clearly define employer-employee relationships.  
    • The Code on Social Security must be amended to guarantee minimum living wages, algorithmic transparency, and mandatory health insurance for all gig and platform workers. 
  • Pivoting to Universal Basic Services (UBS): The State must prioritize Universal Basic Services (ensuring high-quality public healthcare, education, and affordable social housing) to de-commodify fundamental human needs. 
    • Existing targeted schemes like Ayushman Bharat (PM-JAY) for healthcare and Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY) for housing must be progressively expanded toward universal entitlements, ensuring the youth are not trapped in rent-heavy, debt-ridden living.  
  • Progressive Wealth Redistribution: Adopting the World Inequality Lab’s recommendations on progressive taxation (such as taxing extreme wealth, corporate monopolies, and inheritances) will generate the fiscal space needed to fund youth welfare, student debt relief, and the green energy transition. 
  • Aligning Education with the Future of Work: To combat "credential inflation," the education system must transition from traditional degree-centric models to agile skilling ecosystems. 
    • The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 must aggressively integrate vocational training. 
    • Initiatives like FutureSkills Prime and PM Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY 4.0) must be scaled up to upskill youth in Industry 4.0 technologies (Artificial Intelligence, IoT, 3D printing, and drones) to bridge the real-world employability gap. 
  • Ensuring a 'Just Transition' for Climate Action: Economic planning must centre on a 'Just Transition' framework.  
    • The Skill Council for Green Jobs (SCGJ) should be heavily funded to align workforce training with the employment demands of the National Green Hydrogen Mission and the International Solar Alliance (ISA).  
    • Massive state investments in climate adaptation and renewable energy infrastructure will create millions of sustainable "green jobs," effectively turning the climate crisis into an employment opportunity for the youth. 
  • Intergenerationally Equitable Fiscal Policy: Governments must balance spending on the elderly (pensions and social security) with investments in the youth (education, housing, and jobs) to ensure an aging demographic doesn't drain resources and opportunities from future generations.  

Conclusion 

Global youth unrest reflects the structural inequalities of neoliberal capitalism. Addressing it requires a shift towards inclusive growth, equitable wealth distribution, secure employment and intergenerational justice, with the State playing a stronger role in ensuring social and economic equity.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What is Neoliberalism?
Neoliberalism is an economic and political ideology advocating free-market capitalism, deregulation, privatization and reduced government intervention in the economy. 

2. What is meant by the Precariat?
The precariat refers to a class of workers facing insecure employment, irregular incomes and limited social protection, particularly in the gig and informal economies. 

3. Which constitutional provisions support economic and social justice?
Articles 38 and 39 promote distributive justice and prevent wealth concentration, while Article 41 directs the State to secure the right to work and public assistance. 

4. How does the Code on Social Security, 2020 address gig workers?
It formally recognizes gig workers and platform workers and provides a framework for social security measures, but does not grant them conventional employee status.

State Finances: A Study of Budgets of 2025–26

30-01-2026

For Prelims: Demographic Transition, Fiscal Deficit, GST, Capital Expenditure, Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment (SASCI), Consolidated Sinking Fund, Guarantee Redemption Fund, Total Fertility Rate (TFR), Demographic Winter, Tax Buoyancy, Silver Economy, ,National Pension System (NPS), Public Private Partnership, Green Hydrogen.

For Mains: Key findings of the RBI’s State Finances: A Study of Budgets of 2025–26, fiscal implications of India's demographic transition for state governments and way forward for harnessing the demographic dividend and mitigate the risks of ageing population.

Source: ET 

Why in News? 

The RBI’s State Finances: A Study of Budgets of 2025–26 report notes that India is at a critical demographic inflection point, with the need to harness the youth bulge while preparing for an ageing population.  

  • It stresses that tailored state-level fiscal strategies, aligned with different stages of demographic transition, are essential for sustainable economic growth. 

Summary 

  • India’s States face varied demographic stages, demanding differentiated fiscal strategies. 
  • Youthful States require investment in education, skills, and job creation; Ageing States must prioritise healthcare and pensions. 
  • Capital expenditure, revenue mobilisation, and fiscal buffers are crucial for sustainable growth and debt management.

What are the Key Findings of the RBI's State Finances Report 2025-26? 

  • Demographic Diversity Across States: The median age varies from 23 years in Bihar to 37 years in Kerala, while the old-age dependency ratio ranges from 14.0 to 30.1, leading to the classification of states into Youthful, Intermediate, and Ageing categories, each requiring distinct fiscal approaches. It calls for differentiated fiscal strategies.  
    • Youthful States need higher spending on education, skilling, and job creation; Intermediate States should focus on infrastructure, urban reforms, and female workforce participation; Ageing States must prepare for higher healthcare, pension, and social welfare costs. 
  • Widened Fiscal Deficit: The states’ Gross Fiscal Deficit (GFD) widened to 3.3% in 2024-25 (from lower levels post-pandemic), primarily due to lower grants from the Centre (decline in GST compensation & revenue deficit grants). 
    • 16 States have budgeted a GFD exceeding 3% of GSDP for 2025-26, with 13 States surpassing 3.5%. 
  • State-Level Reforms for Revenue Growth: States' own tax base is highly concentrated, with State GST, sales tax, excise duties, and stamp duties constituting ~90% of collections. States are exploring non-tax revenue sources like mineral taxation and asset monetisation. 
  • Capital Push and Social Sector Focus: Capital expenditure has firmed up steadily, supported by the Centre's Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment (SASCI). It is expected to increase up to 3.2% of GDP in 2025-26. Social sector expenditure is the major driver of revenue expenditure in 2025-26 (8.2% of GDP). 
  • Debt and Borrowings: Consolidated debt of States declined to 28.1% of GDP by March 2024 from a peak of 31% (March 2021), but is budgeted to rise to 29.2% by March 2026. 
    • Market borrowings now finance about 76% of the GFD (2025-26), indicating increased reliance. 
    • Interest payment burden remains manageable (Interest Payments-to-GDP stable at 1.5–1.9%), aided by concessional loans from the Centre. 
    • The maturity profile of State Government Securities (SGS) is elongating, with an increase in issuances beyond 10–15 years. 
  • Other Key Observations: Research & Development (R&D) expenditure by States is low (around 0.2–0.3% of GSDP) and dominated by medical and agricultural research. 
    • States are building fiscal buffers via the Consolidated Sinking Fund (CSF, Rs 2.4 lakh crore) and Guarantee Redemption Fund (GRF, Rs 16,019 crore). 
    • SASCI has been highly effective, with nearly full disbursement (Rs 1.49 lakh crore in 2024-25) and driving reforms. 

Demographic Transition 

  • About: The demographic transition describes the historical shift from high to low birth and death rates as a country develops. It typically involves several stages. 
    • Stage 1: High birth and high death rates lead to a stable, low population. 
    • Stage 2: Death rates fall due to better healthcare and food security, while birth rates remain high, causing rapid population growth. 
    • Stage 3: Birth rates begin to decline due to urbanization, education, and family planning, slowing population growth. 
    • Stage 4: Both birth and death rates are low, leading to a stable or aging population. 

  • India's Current Demographic Status: As per the 2011 Census, India is in Stage 3 of the demographic transition model. 
    • The Total Fertility Rate (TFR) has fallen to 2.0 (as per NFHS-5), which is below the replacement rate of 2.1. This means, on average, women are having fewer children than needed to maintain the current population size without migration. 
  • Future Projection: According to the UN's World Population Prospects 2024, India's population is expected to peak at around 1.7 billion in the early 2060s and then gradually decline, though it will remain the world's most populous country. 
  • Demographic Disaster: A demographic disaster occurs when a rapidly growing working-age population (15–64 years) fails to boost growth, creating unemployment, underemployment, social unrest, higher dependency, and long-term economic stagnation. 
  • Demographic Winter: The Demographic winter refers to a long-term population decline from low birth rates (below ~2.1 children per woman), causing a shrinking workforce, higher elder dependency, and strained social systems. China’s population fell for the fourth consecutive year in 2025, highlighting this trend.

What are the Fiscal Implications of India's Demographic Transition for State Governments?

Revenue Potential 

High potential tax buoyancy due to expanding working-age population, rising incomes, consumption, and labour participation. Requires productivity-enhancing investments to realise demographic dividend (e.g., Bihar, Uttar Pradesh). 

Stable but slowing revenue growth as workforce share peaks. Scope to tap skilled labour, services, and silver economy (senior citizens centered) (e.g., Telangana). 

Shrinking or stagnant tax bases as the working-age population declines. Lower income and consumption growth caps own-source revenues (e.g., Kerala, Punjab). 

Expenditure Composition 

High developmental spending needs on education, skilling, healthcare, and job creation to absorb youth bulge; lower old-age welfare burden initially. 

Dual expenditure pressure—continued investment in human capital alongside rising healthcare and social security demands. 

Rising committed expenditure on pensions, geriatric healthcare, and social security, crowding out infrastructure and education spending. 

Debt Sustainability & Fiscal Space 

Manageable debt outlook if growth-enhancing spending raises future revenues; risk of stress if youth dividend is missed. 

Tightening fiscal space as welfare obligations rise while revenue growth moderates; careful debt management required. 

High stress on debt sustainability due to rising welfare spending, and high interest payments; fiscal deficits risk exceeding 3% of GSDP. 

Fiscal Buffers, Transfers & Productivity 

Need to build early fiscal buffers and invest in productive capex; benefit relatively from current Finance Commission devolution norms based on population size. 

Strategic focus on expenditure quality, R&D, and technology to offset future ageing effects; gradual buffer creation. 

Strong need for fiscal buffers, prudent contingent liability management (pensions), higher R&D spending, and reform of devolution criteria to reflect higher old-age dependency ratios. 

How can States Harness the Demographic Dividend and Mitigate the Risks of Ageing Population? 

Harnessing the Demographic Dividend 

  • Hyper-Local Skill Matching: Use AI-driven platforms to map local industry demand in real-time with the skill profiles of youth, creating dynamic, district-level apprenticeship and job-matching ecosystems. 
  • Create ‘Opportunity Corridors’ in Emerging Sectors: Identify and develop specialized industrial/technology corridors focused on future sectors (e.g., green hydrogen, semiconductors, space technology) with pre-approved land, plug-and-play infrastructure, and fast-tracked clearances to attract investment and high-quality jobs. 
  • Implement a ‘Learn-Earn-Pension’ Continuum: Bundle student loans with guaranteed internship stipends and an option to auto-enroll a micro-contribution into the National Pension System (NPS) upon first employment, fostering long-term financial security from the start of a career. 
  • Foster District as an Export Hub (DEH) Model: Empower districts with high youth populations to specialize in niche products (handicrafts, textiles, food processing). Provide integrated support for branding, e-commerce onboarding, logistics, and meeting global quality standards to turn local talent into global entrepreneurs. 

Mitigating Risks of Population Ageing 

  • Develop Silver Economy Clusters: Incentivize the private sector to develop integrated townships or clusters with age-friendly housing, accessible healthcare facilities, recreation centers, and geriatric care services, creating new economic ecosystems and reducing public infrastructure burden. 
  • Elderpreneurship Schemes: Partner with industry to design flexible, part-time, and consultancy-based roles for experienced retirees. Provide seed grants and incubation support for seniors to start social enterprises or knowledge-based consultancies. 
  • Sub-National Longevity Funds: Create dedicated state-level funds, potentially through public-private partnerships, to invest in and subsidize preventive healthcare, telemedicine, assistive technologies, and drug research for age-related diseases. 

Conclusion 

India’s demographic transition presents both opportunities and challenges for State finances. Harnessing the youth bulge through skill development, employment, and innovation can boost revenue, while ageing populations require fiscal buffers, social security, and healthcare preparedness. Tailored, stage-specific fiscal strategies are essential for sustainable growth and intergenerational equity.

Major Announcements on India’s 79th Independence Day

18-08-2025

Source: HT  

On India’s 79th Independence Day, Prime Minister announced key initiatives to drive the nation toward developed status by 2047. 

Major Announcements 

  • PM Viksit Bharat Rozgar Yojana: It aims to create 3.5 crore jobs in two years. Newly employed youth will receive Rs 15,000 as support, benefiting around 3 crore individuals. It marks a key step from Swatantra Bharat to Samriddha Bharat. 
  • Mission Sudarshan Chakra: Aims to develop an indigenous Iron Dome-like air defence system by 2035 to protect strategic and civilian sites, with capabilities to intercept and retaliate against enemy attacks. 
  • Semiconductor: India to roll out its first Made-in-India semiconductor chip by the end of 2025. 
  • National Deep Water Exploration Mission: The planned mission termed as “Samudra Manthan’ aims to explore offshore oil, gas, and mineral resources in mission mode. 
  • GST and Task Force Reforms: Goods and Services Tax (GST) reforms to be launched by October 2025, with reduced taxes on essentials and relief for MSMEs and consumers. 
    • A dedicated Reform Task Force will drive economic growth, cut red tape, and modernise governance for a USD 10 trillion economy by 2047. 
  • High-Powered Demography Mission:  Aims to tackle demographic imbalance from illegal migration in border areas, ensuring national security, unity, and citizens' rights. 
  • Nuclear Energy Expansion: India plans to increase nuclear power capacity tenfold by 2047, with 10 new reactors currently under development. 
  • Clean Energy Target Achieved: India has met its goal of 50% electricity capacity from non-fossil sources, five years ahead of the 2030 deadline.
