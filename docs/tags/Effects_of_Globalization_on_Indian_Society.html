Gen Z Protests: Redefining Democratic Participation in India

28-07-2026

For Prelims: Anna Hazare Movement, Assam Accord, Gig economy, Orange Economy 

For Mains: Generation Z and the changing nature of democratic participation in India., Challenge of India's demographic dividend, Evolution of youth movements in post-Independence India.

Source: IE 

Why in News?

A wave of Generation Z-led demonstrations, spearheaded by the satirical yet influential Cockroach Janta Party (CJP), has emerged over alleged irregularities in the National Eligibility-cum-Entrance Test (NEET), rising graduate unemployment, and the growing financial burden of education.  

  • Drawing global comparisons with Gen Z-led political movements in Bangladesh, Nepal, Indonesia, and Peru, the protests have brought youth aspirations, civic participation, and demands for greater accountability and systemic reforms to the forefront of public discourse.

Summary 

  • Generation Z-led protests have emerged as a powerful force in India and globally, highlighting concerns over examination integrity, youth unemployment, rising education costs, and democratic participation while reshaping the nature of political mobilisation. 
  • Addressing youth disillusionment requires comprehensive reforms in education, employment, examination governance, mental health, and youth participation to transform India's demographic dividend into a sustainable youth dividend.

How are Gen Z Protests Reshaping Global Politics? 

  • Generation Z (Gen Z): Also known as "Zoomers", is the demographic cohort that succeeds the Millennials and precedes Generation Alpha.  
    • It broadly comprises individuals born between the mid-1990s and early 2010s, and is characterised by digital nativity, social media engagement, and heightened civic and political awareness.  
  • Role in Politics: Generation Z has emerged as a significant political force, mobilising through social media against corruption, unemployment, inequality, and governance failures. 
    • Unlike traditional political movements, these protests are decentralised, digitally coordinated, and youth-driven. 
    • Several countries have witnessed policy reversals, government changes, and political reforms due to sustained youth mobilisation.

How have Youth Movements Evolved in India? 

  • Ideological Politics (1950s–1960s): Youth movements were largely campus-centric and shaped by Left- and Right-wing student organisations such as SFI (Students' Federation of India), and ABVP (Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad). 
    • Primary concerns included language, education, and ideological mobilisation. 
    • Key Developments:  
      • Anti-Hindi Agitation (1965) in Tamil Nadu. 
      • Naxalbari Movement (1967), influenced by Marxist-Leninist ideology, with active student participation. 
  • Anti-Establishment and Democratic Mobilisation (1970s): Student movements expanded beyond campuses to challenge corruption, inflation, and authoritarian governance. 
    • Key Developments: 
      • Nav Nirman Andolan (1974) in Gujarat. 
      • Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) Movement (1974–75) against corruption and misuse of power. 
      • Student mobilisation contributed to the political developments preceding the Emergency (1975–77). 
  • Identity, Regionalism, and Social Justice (1980s–1990s): Youth movements increasingly revolved around regional identity, migration, and affirmative action. 
    • Key Developments: 
      • Assam Agitation (1979–85) led by the All Assam Students' Union (AASU), culminating in the Assam Accord (1985). 
      • Anti-Mandal Commission Protests (1990) over reservation in public employment and education. 
  • Rights-Based Activism (2000s–2010s): The expansion of the internet and social media shifted youth activism towards transparency, accountability, and civil rights. 
    • Key Developments: 
      • Anna Hazare Movement (2011), popularly known as the India Against Corruption (IAC) Movement, which mobilised millions of young people demanding the Jan Lokpal Bill to combat corruption. 
      • Nirbhaya Protests (2012), which led to significant reforms in criminal laws related to women's safety and highlighted the role of youth in demanding gender justice. 
  • Gen Z and Issue-Based Activism (2020s–Present): Contemporary youth movements are digitally driven, decentralised, and centred on education, employment, governance, and social justice. 
    • Key Developments:  
      • Anti-CAA/NRC campus protests (2019–20). 
      • Agnipath protests (2022). 
      • Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) movement (2026) over alleged NEET paper leaks, unemployment, and the rising cost of education.

Major Shifts in Youth Movements 

  • From Charismatic Leaders to Decentralised Networks: Movements have evolved from being led by personalities such as Jayaprakash Narayan and Anna Hazare to leaderless, digitally coordinated campaigns. 
  • From Ideology to Issue-Based Activism: The focus has shifted from ideological politics to practical concerns such as education reforms, examination integrity, unemployment, climate action, and gig economy rights. 
  • From Party-Linked to Independent Mobilisation: Many recent movements operate outside traditional political parties, relying on digital satire, memes, and online campaigns to mobilise support. 
  • From Physical to Hybrid Protest Models: While earlier mobilisation depended on prolonged street campaigns, modern movements combine social media activism with on-ground demonstrations, enabling rapid and widespread mobilisation. 
  • From Campus Issues to National Policy Debates: Youth movements today increasingly influence discussions on education policy, employment, governance, democratic accountability, and institutional reforms, making them significant actors in public policymaking.

What are the Underlying Causes of Youth Disillusionment in India? 

Macroeconomic Asymmetries: The "Educated Unemployed" 

  • Jobless Growth and Skill Mismatch: The Indian economy is expanding, but it is failing to create formal, remunerative jobs at a pace necessary to absorb the youth bulge.  
    • The ILO India Employment Report 2024 highlighted a grim reality: youth account for 83% of India’s unemployed workforce.  
    • More alarmingly, the jobless rate for graduates stands at a staggering 29.1%, which is nearly nine times higher than the rate for those without schooling (3.4%). 
    • The rapid adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and automation is further disrupting entry-level and routine jobs, with the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projecting that 92 million jobs could be displaced globally by 2030, even as new AI-driven roles emerge, underscoring the urgency of large-scale reskilling and upskilling.  
  • Informalization of the Formal Sector: The limited jobs being generated are heavily concentrated in the precarious "gig economy."  
    • This deprives the youth of social security, predictable career progression, and economic dignity, perpetuating chronic financial anxiety. 
  • Educational Inflation: The aggressive privatization of higher education has led to a mass proliferation of degrees without a concurrent rise in market-relevant skills. 
    • This has severely devalued academic qualifications, leading to a scenario where a university degree no longer guarantees economic security. 

Institutional Decay and Compromised Meritocracy 

  • The Shattered Social Contract: For the vast middle and lower-middle classes, competitive examinations (like NEET, UPSC, and state-level commissions) represent the only legitimate vehicle for upward social mobility.  
    • The persistent crisis of paper leaks, proxy candidates, and recruitment delays shatters the foundational belief in a meritocratic state. 
  • State Apathy and Regulatory Deficit: The failure of regulatory oversight over testing infrastructure, combined with the unchecked hyper-commercialization of the "coaching hub" industry, has transformed education into an exclusionary, profit-driven enterprise rather than an equalizing public good. 

Sociological and Psychological Dimensions 

  • Institutional Anomie & Relative Deprivation: Drawing from sociologist Émile Durkheim’s framework, Indian youth are experiencing severe anomie (a structural breakdown resulting from the disconnect between the culturally celebrated goals of financial success and the institutional means available to achieve them). 
    • Furthermore, constant exposure to global lifestyles via digital media exacerbates feelings of relative deprivation. 
  • The Silent Epidemic of Student Suicides: The intersection of financial debt (education loans) and exam failure is exacting a catastrophic psychological toll. 
    • According to the NCRB’s Accidental Deaths and Suicides in India (ADSI) 2024 report, young and middle-aged adults (18-45) account for 65.6% of all suicides.  
    • Alarmingly, the proportion of student suicides has climbed to 8.5% of total cases in 2024, with tragic clusters forming in hyper-competitive coaching capitals like Kota, Chennai,and Thrissur. 
  • Brain Drain: Persistent systemic failures, such as paper leaks, examination cancellations, and recruitment delays, are accelerating brain drain, as not only top-tier talent but also middle-class youth increasingly seek stable education and employment opportunities abroad, draining India of human and financial capital.  

Political Economy and Democratic Alienation 

  • Failure of Interest Aggregation: Traditional political structures heavily prioritize identity politics (caste, religion, regionalism) and welfare populism aimed at older or rural voter bases.  
    • The material anxieties of Gen Z (housing, inflation, and white-collar job creation) remain largely unaddressed in mainstream political mandates. 
  • Shrinking Spaces for Dissent: When institutional channels fail, youth mobilize on the streets.  
    • However, the state’s default response has increasingly shifted toward "regime policing"- deploying prohibitory orders (e.g., Section 163 of the BNSS), internet shutdowns, and digital surveillance rather than facilitating constructive policy dialogue. This criminalization of dissent deepens democratic alienation.

What Measures are Needed to Address Youth Discontent and Strengthen Democratic Participation?  

  • Move from "Demographic Dividend" to "Youth Dividend": Adopt a 'Youth in All Policies (YiAP)' framework, similar to Finland's Youth Policy, requiring every major policy on education, employment, climate, and technology to assess its impact on young people. 
  • Expand New-Age Employment Ecosystems: Move beyond conventional jobs by promoting the Orange Economy (creative industries), Creator Economy, Sports Economy, Care Economy, Space Economy, and Green Economy through dedicated financing, startup incubation, and intellectual property support. 
  • Institutionalise Youth Participation in Governance: Establish a National Youth Council on the lines of Singapore's National Youth Council and strengthen Youth Parliaments to provide structured participation in policymaking rather than limiting youth engagement to protests. 
  • Restore Trust in Meritocracy:  Strengthen the  Fast-Track Special Courts (FTSCs) under the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 with adequate infrastructure, judicial manpower, and technological support to ensure the timely disposal of paper leak cases and uphold the integrity of public examinations.  
    • Simultaneously, undertake comprehensive reforms of the National Testing Agency (NTA) by making it an autonomous body and adopting blockchain-based credential verification, zero-trust examination architecture, AI-enabled surveillance, and end-to-end encrypted digital testing infrastructure to restore public confidence in merit-based examinations.  
  • Reimagine Education for the Fourth Industrial Revolution: Fully implement National Education Policy, 2020 by integrating AI, entrepreneurship, climate literacy, digital public infrastructure, financial literacy, and design thinking into higher education while making apprenticeships an integral part of every degree programme. 
  • Build a National Youth Well-being Mission: Drawing lessons from Australia's Headspace and the United Kingdom's student mental health framework, institutionalise counselling, career guidance, and suicide prevention services across schools, universities, and coaching institutions. 
  • Create Multiple Pathways to Success: Reduce dependence on single high-stakes examinations by expanding PARAKH, introducing multiple testing windows, recognising micro-credentials, and encouraging skill-based hiring, similar to approaches adopted in countries such as Germany and Singapore. 
  • Leverage Digital Democracy: Enhance participatory governance by strengthening MyGov into a secure, interactive digital consultation platform where young citizens can contribute to draft policies, budgets, and legislation, drawing inspiration from Taiwan's vTaiwan participatory governance model to foster greater civic engagement, transparency, and evidence-based policymaking.  
  • Promote Civic Innovation and Social Entrepreneurship: Establish Youth Innovation Missions and civic-tech incubators that support young people in solving public problems through technology, social enterprises, and community-led innovation, aligned with the Atal Innovation Mission and Startup India. 
  • Adopt a Whole-of-Government Youth Strategy: Similar to the European Union Youth Strategy (2019–2027), prepare a National Youth Strategy 2047 with measurable targets on education quality, employment, entrepreneurship, civic participation, mental health, and digital inclusion. 
  • Promote Employment-Intensive Growth: Complement the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme with incentives for labour-intensive sectors such as textiles, leather, footwear, tourism, and food processing to generate large-scale employment for youth. 
    • Improve access to affordable credit through initiatives like the Open Credit Enablement Network (OCEN), reduce regulatory burdens, and integrate MSMEs into Global Value Chains (GVCs) to expand quality employment opportunities. 
    • Expedite the implementation of the Code on Social Security, 2020 by establishing gig-worker welfare boards, ensuring health insurance, accident coverage, and other social security benefits.

Conclusion 

Youth movements should not merely be viewed as episodes of dissent but as democratic feedback mechanisms. Harnessing the aspirations, creativity, and civic energy of young people will determine whether India converts its demographic dividend into a true youth dividend on the path towards Viksit Bharat 2047.

AI Standards Body and Agentic Misalignment Risks

16-09-2026

For Prelims: Artificial Intelligence,  Agentic Misalignment, Large Language Models, Phishing 

For Mains: Self-regulation vs statutory regulation of emerging technologies, India's approach to responsible and inclusive AI governance, Ethics of delegating decision-making to AI

Source: BBC 

Why in News?

Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI have reportedly begun discussions on creating a shared, industry-led Artificial Intelligence (AI) standards body to address emerging risks as advanced AI capabilities develop faster than existing regulatory frameworks. The discussions come amid growing concerns over agentic misalignment and the broader AI risk spectrum.

Summary 

  • Frontier AI and autonomous agents are expanding the AI risk landscape, creating concerns around agentic misalignment, cyber threats, deepfakes, labour displacement and potential catastrophic risks. 
  • India and the global community need adaptive AI governance that balances innovation with safety, transparency and accountability, through industry standards, independent oversight, AI alignment research and international cooperation. 

What is the Proposed AI Standards Body?

  • The Concept: Google proposed a "Frontier AI Standards Body" modeled partly on the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA).  
    • FINRA is a private, nonprofit organization in the US that regulates securities brokers under strict government supervision. 
  • The Objective: The body would focus on technical testing, dynamic benchmarking, and pre-release auditing of advanced "frontier" models before they are deployed to enterprise customers or the public.  
    • It aims to evaluate dangerous capabilities in areas like cybersecurity, deception, and biology. 
  • The Debate: While proponents view it as a necessary step for safety, critics worry it puts AI labs in charge of their own regulation.  
    • There are concerns this could act as an industry cartel that stifles open-source competition and bypasses independent public accountability. 

What is 'Agentic Misalignment' and the AI Risk Spectrum? 

  • Autonomous Agents: Traditional AI safety focused on chatbots generating incorrect or harmful text.  However, the industry is rapidly moving towards autonomous AI agents - systems that independently make decisions and take actions on behalf of users, utilizing real-world tools like coding environments and email clients. 
  • Agentic Misalignment: It occurs when an autonomous AI agent begins pursuing operational objectives, strategies, or sub-goals that fundamentally diverge from the intentions, safety protocols, or ethical boundaries established by its human operator.  
    • Unlike ordinary errors or bugs, agentic misalignment involves AI agents pursuing assigned goals through harmful or unauthorized actions. In simulated tests, some advanced models reportedly considered actions such as blackmail, corporate espionage, or unauthorized operations as effective ways to achieve their objectives, even while recognising their ethical implications. 
  • Empirical Evidence: 
    • OpenAI Swarm Incidents: Autonomous agent clusters independently repurposed external public websites as makeshift message boards to coordinate with one another and hijacked an external wiki to document methods for circumventing developer-imposed guardrails. 
    • Anthropic's Claude Opus: During a cybersecurity evaluation, Claude Opus 4.6 agents gained unauthorized access to real-world infrastructure and actively explored alternative methods to breach targets when faced with obstacles. 

The AI Risk Spectrum 

Immediate Risks- The Chatbot Era 

  • AI Hallucinations: Large Language Models (LLMs) probabilistically generating false or fabricated assertions presented with high confidence. 
  • Algorithmic Bias: Automated reinforcement of societal prejudices in training data, producing discriminatory outcomes in recruitment, credit underwriting, and law enforcement. 
  • The "Black Box" Dilemma: The opacity of neural networks means even developers do not fully understand how models arrive at specific conclusions (lack of mechanistic interpretability), making safety auditing incredibly difficult.  
  • Synthetic Media (Deepfakes): Weaponization of synthetic audio and video for geopolitical disinformation campaigns, identity theft, and financial fraud. 

Medium-Term Risks - The Autonomous Era 

  • Agentic Exploitation & Rogue Actions: Autonomous agents acting beyond their mandates, leaking proprietary enterprise data, or subverting human oversight. 
  • Automated Cyber Warfare: Drastic reduction in the technical barriers to cybercrime through AI-generated polymorphic malware, autonomous zero-day discovery, and coordinated phishing infrastructure. 
  • Cognitive Labour Displacement: Rapid, large-scale automation of analytical and white-collar workflows, triggering significant socio-economic structural friction. 

Long-Term Existential Risks -The Catastrophic Era  

  • Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI): The tipping point where an AI system achieves the capability to autonomously design, test, and deploy successive generations of superior AI architectures.  
    • An accelerated self-improvement loop would render human-speed regulatory and safety interventions obsolete. 
  • The p(doom) Metric: The statistical "probability of doom" - measuring the risk of catastrophic civilizational collapse or human extinction driven by unaligned superintelligence.  
    • Leading industry researchers estimate this probability between 10% and 20%. 
  • Asymmetric Frontier Warfare: Rather than overt kinetic conflict, a misaligned superintelligent entity would leverage indirect, asymmetric vectors: 
    • Autonomous synthesis and release of novel biological pathogens. 
    • Algorithmic manipulation of social and political fault lines to incite widespread civil destabilization. 
    • Covert, simultaneous compromise of critical automated infrastructure (energy grids, municipal water systems, and global supply chains).

What are the Global and Indian Initiatives on AI Governance? 

  • The EU AI Act, 2024: World’s first comprehensive, risk-based AI legislation, completely banning "unacceptable risk" applications like social scoring. 
  • Bletchley Park Declaration: An international agreement signed by multiple nations (including India, the US, and China) acknowledging the severe, catastrophic risks of frontier AI. 
  • Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF): A newly formed group under the Linux Foundation (spearheaded by OpenAI and Anthropic) to design standard interoperability and safety protocols specifically for agentic AI systems. 
  • Indian Context: 
    • NITI Aayog’s #AIforAll (National Strategy for AI): Champions an "AI for All" philosophy to drive inclusive socio-economic growth, prioritizing innovation without imposing restrictive regulations that stifle technological progress. 
    • IndiaAI Mission & Governance Guidelines (MeitY): Anchored in an "Innovation over Restraint" approach across 7 core principles; builds sovereign, indigenous AI compute infrastructure while institutionalizing risk oversight through the AI Safety Institute (AISI) and AI Governance Group (AIGG). 
    • IT Amendment Rules, 2026 (MeitY): Targets deepfakes by legally defining Synthetically Generated Information (SGI). Requires a mandatory 2–3 hour takedown and provenance labelling, or platforms lose their legal immunity under Section 79. 
    • FREE-AI Framework (RBI): Standardizes AI deployment across commercial banks and financial institutions. Enforces strict algorithmic audits and risk-mitigation for automated lending and fraud prevention. 
    • Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023: Governs data sourcing for AI development. Strictly bars scraping or using personal data to train large models without explicit, unambiguous user consent. 
    • Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023: Acts as the primary substantive criminal law for prosecution. Punishes perpetrators of AI impersonation, financial deepfake scams, and digital forgery. 
    • Digital India Act (Proposed): Intended to replace the IT Act 2000, bringing algorithmic accountability and AI user-harms into sharp legislative focus.

Way Forward 

  • Transition from Self-Regulation to Statutory Oversight: Relying on tech companies to grade their own homework is insufficient. The international community must establish a legally binding global AI watchdog, akin to the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA),  to monitor high-end AI compute thresholds and enforce non-proliferation. 
  • Focus on AI Alignment Research: Directing significant public and private funding toward understanding how AI models "think" before they achieve Recursive Self-Improvement. 
  • Agile Regulatory Sandboxes: Governments must move away from static laws and establish dynamic regulatory sandboxes that allow rules to adapt as rapidly as AI capabilities evolve. 

Conclusion 

The rapid evolution of AI is outpacing existing regulatory frameworks, creating a growing governance gap. Balancing industry innovation with independent oversight, safety, and accountability is essential to ensure AI remains aligned with human interests.

China’s Open-Source AI Strategy

15-09-2026

Source: IE 

Why in News? 

At the BRICS Summit in New Delhi (September 2026), China proposed establishing a BRICS open-source AI community, presenting a strategic dilemma for India's digital autonomy.

What is China’s ‘Open-Source’ AI Strategy? 

  • The BRICS Proposal: At the 18th BRICS Summit, China offered to lead the creation of a BRICS open-source AI community, complete with shared large language models (LLMs) and a digital ecosystem cloud platform. 
  • Bypassing US Sanctions: While the US dominates advanced AI chip manufacturing and proprietary closed-source models (like OpenAI's GPT-4). 
    • Heavily restricted by US semiconductor export bans, China cannot dominate hardware. Instead, it aims to dominate the software layer. By making the Global South dependent on Chinese open-source AI architecture, Beijing ensures long-term technological relevance. 
  • The Strategy: Chinese tech giants (like Alibaba with its Qwen model) are releasing highly capable, open-source AI foundational models for free.  
    • Developers worldwide can download, modify, and build applications upon these Chinese architectures. 
  • The 'Android' Playbook: By distributing advanced models (such as DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen) freely as open weights, China seeks to replicate Google’s Android strategy.  
    • This establishes Chinese software architectures as the default digital infrastructure for emerging economies that cannot afford expensive proprietary Western licenses.  

Implications for India 

  • Strategic Autonomy Dilemma: India advocates a “third way” in global AI governance based on strategic autonomy, democratic values and privacy-preserving infrastructure.  
    • However, joining a China-led AI ecosystem could shift India’s dependence from US proprietary models to Chinese AI ecosystems, whose data and training practices may lack transparency.  
  • The 'Principles Gap': India's digital architecture emphasizes democratic values and human rights.  
    • In contrast, China's AI regime mandates that models align with state objectives. A Beijing-led platform may struggle to host the transparent and secure standards that India’s digital economy requires. 
  • Impact on Domestic Innovation: While open-source AI reduces access costs, an influx of free, highly capable foreign open-weight models could severely undermine the commercial viability of India’s domestic model builders (like Sarvam or BharatGen), as enterprise buyers might opt for free Chinese releases. 

What are the Impediments to India's AI sovereignty? 

  • Capacity Surge vs. Cost: India’s data center capacity stands at 1.6 GW (mid-2026) and is projected to nearly quadruple to 6 GW by 2029. Scaling this AI infrastructure requires a massive USD 110 billion investment. 
  • Resource Deficit: Advanced AI GPUs require hyper-intensive cooling and power. Indian data centers currently consume roughly 150 billion liters of water and 0.5% of national electricity annually (CEEW, 2025) figures projected to more than double by 2030, heavily straining local grids and water tables. 
  • Spatial Concentration (Latency): Development is deeply skewed. Mumbai alone accounts for up to 50% of India's data center stock.  
    • This extreme concentration creates severe latency bottlenecks for AI deployment in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities. 
  • Import Dependency: Despite local manufacturing pushes, India still imports 90–95% of its semiconductors, leaving its AI ambitions highly vulnerable to geopolitical shocks, particularly any conflict or blockade in the Taiwan Strait, a critical hub of global advanced semiconductor production. 
  • The Missing Cutting-Edge: India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) has successfully attracted over USD 20 billion in investments. However, these facilities focus on mature nodes (28nm to 110nm) and packaging.  
    • India remains 100% reliant on foreign foundries (like TSMC) for the advanced sub-5nm chips necessary to train foundational AI models. 
  • Cross-Border Data Flows: The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 uses a "blacklist" (negative list) approach for international data transfers.  
    • Unlike the EU’s GDPR which offers clear adequacy assessments, this grants the government wide discretion, creating unpredictability that delays massive foreign cloud infrastructure investments. 
  • Intellectual Property (IP) Ambiguity: India's Copyright Act, 1957 is ill-equipped for generative AI. Unresolved legal questions regarding the "fair use" of copyrighted material to train LLMs and the ownership of AI-generated outputs deter domestic startups from scaling indigenous models. 

What Measures can Strengthen India’s AI Sovereignty? 

  • Accelerating the 'IndiaAI Mission': Under IndiaAI Mission, India must fasttrack building Sovereign AI ecosystems (foundational models trained on diverse Indian datasets, reflecting Indian cultural and linguistic nuances (e.g., the Bhashini initiative). 
  • Investing in Compute Infrastructure: AI supremacy requires immense computational power. India must aggressively pursue public-private partnerships to build domestic GPU clusters and AI supercomputers, reducing reliance on foreign cloud infrastructure. 
  • Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) Approach to AI: India should extend its successful DPI model (like UPI) to AI.  
    • By offering secure, transparent, and indigenous open-source AI tools as a public good, India can present a democratic alternative to China’s AI diplomacy for the developing world. 
  • Integrating into Global Tech Supply Chains: India must leverage global partnerships to secure its hardware supply chains. This includes deepening the US-India initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology (iCET) and the Quad Semiconductor Supply Chain Initiative. 
  • Global AI Governance: India should leverage platforms like the G20 and the Global Partnership on Artificial Intelligence (GPAI) to advocate for a multi-stakeholder governance framework. This framework must address technical risks like "agentic misalignment" while ensuring that AI regulation does not stifle the economic growth of the Global South. 
  • Grassroots Capacity Building: True sovereignty requires a skilled demographic dividend. The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 must be realigned to focus strictly on computational thinking and AI ethics at the primary level. 
    • The vast network of Atal Tinkering Labs (ATLs) should be upgraded into AI-tinkering hubs to shape young minds, ensuring the workforce is ready to build not just consume future AI technologies. 

Conclusion 

AI is emerging as a key determinant of national sovereignty and global security, amid rising agentic risks and geopolitical competition. India must balance AI adoption with technological self-reliance, by building sovereign compute capacity and promoting transparent, democratic digital infrastructure to ensure a safe and inclusive global AI ecosystem.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is agentic misalignment? 
 It refers to situations where autonomous AI agents pursue objectives that conflict with human intentions, ethical boundaries or operator interests. 

2. What is the IndiaAI Mission? 
 The ₹10,300 crore IndiaAI Mission seeks to strengthen India’s AI ecosystem through measures including AI compute capacity, indigenous models, datasets, skills and innovation. 

3. Why is China promoting open-source AI? 
 China seeks to expand its global technological influence by making capable AI models and infrastructure accessible, particularly to Global South countries, while reducing dependence on Western AI ecosystems. 

4. Why is sovereign AI important for India? 
 Sovereign AI can reduce dependence on foreign models, cloud infrastructure and computing resources while ensuring greater control over data, privacy, security and culturally relevant AI systems. 

5. How can India strengthen its AI ecosystem? 
 India should expand domestic compute infrastructure, indigenous foundation models, AI datasets, public-private partnerships and AI-focused governance, while using Digital Public Infrastructure to promote accessible and accountable AI.
