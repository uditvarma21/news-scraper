Fall in Gold Prices

28-03-2026

Source: TH 

Why in News?  

Despite the escalation of the West Asian conflict, global and domestic gold prices have witnessed a sharp and anomalous decline. Traditionally, gold acts as a safe-haven asset during geopolitical or financial crises, but current macroeconomic dynamics have forced a deviation from this historical trend.

What are the Key Factors Driving the Fall in Gold Prices? 

  • Oil Price Shock & Inflationary Pressures: The West Asian conflict has severely disrupted global oil supply chains, pushing crude oil prices significantly higher (crossing USD 120/barrel in recent trends). 
    • Higher energy costs translate to broader macroeconomic inflation. To combat oil-driven inflation, major central banks (like the US Federal Reserve) have shifted away from expected rate cuts. 
    • The market now anticipates a hawkish monetary policy, where interest rates will remain elevated for a prolonged period to cool down demand. 
  • Surging Bond Yields: With the expectation of sustained high interest rates, U.S. Treasury bonds (which offer guaranteed, fixed returns) have become highly attractive. 
    • Investors are pivoting capital away from zero-yield gold and into high-yielding government bonds. 
  • Strengthening of the US Dollar: As global capital flows into US debt markets to capture high yields, the US Dollar index strengthens. 
    • A stronger dollar makes gold fundamentally more expensive for foreign buyers, dampening global physical and investment demand. 
  • Liquidity Crunch and Profit Booking: Prior to the current crash, gold was trading at record highs. As the geopolitical conflict triggered a massive sell-off in global stock markets, investors faced a liquidity crunch. 
    • To cover margin calls and equity portfolio losses, investors resorted to profit booking by selling their highly profitable gold holdings, flooding the market with excess supply and driving prices down further. 

Gold as a Traditional Safe Haven 

  • Safe-Haven Asset: Historically (e.g., during the 2008 financial crisis, the Covid-19 pandemic, and the 2022 Russia-Ukraine war), investors flock to gold to preserve wealth when equity markets and fiat currencies become volatile. 
  • Inverse Relationship with Interest Rates: Gold is a non-yielding asset (it does not pay interest or dividends).  
    • It typically performs best when interest rates are low, as the opportunity cost of holding gold decreases compared to interest-bearing assets like government bonds. 
  • Inverse Relationship with the US Dollar: Gold is globally priced in US Dollars (USD).  A weaker dollar makes gold cheaper for buyers holding other currencies, thereby driving up demand and prices.

US Dollar the New Safe Haven

  • Short-Term Dollar Dominance: In the immediate term, the USD is acting as the primary safe haven. Because global oil trade is primarily dollar-denominated (Petrodollar), rising oil prices force countries to acquire more dollars to pay for their energy imports, structurally boosting dollar demand over gold. 
  • Long-Term De-dollarization: Despite the current price correction, central banks across the globe (especially in emerging markets) continue to aggressively accumulate physical gold.  
    • The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has aggressively increased its gold holdings as a strategic component of its foreign exchange (forex) reserves, with total gold holdings surpassing 880 metric tonnes by late 2025, accounting for 15.17% of total reserves. 
    • This is driven by a long-term strategic shift toward De-dollarization—diversifying foreign exchange reserves to protect against Western financial sanctions and weaponization of the dollar. 

Gold 

  • Elemental Basics: Gold’s chemical symbol is Au (derived from the Latin word aurum, meaning "shining dawn"). Its atomic number is 79, making it one of the naturally occurring elements with the highest atomic numbers. 
    • Gold is extremely rare and largely originated from meteorite impacts. 
  • Malleability and Ductility: Gold is the most malleable and ductile of all known metals.  
  • The Noble Metal: It is chemically unreactive. Gold does not tarnish, rust, or corrode. It remains unaffected by air, moisture, and most acids, dissolving only in a specific, highly corrosive mixture of nitric and hydrochloric acids known as aqua regia. 
  • Conductivity: It is an outstanding conductor of both thermal and electrical energy. 
  • Economic & Financial Significance: Gold’s purity is measured in karats (24K = 99.9% pure), and it is often alloyed for durability. 
    • It serves as a safe-haven asset, historically linked to the Gold Standard, and is widely held by central banks as a strategic reserve. 
  • Technological & Industrial Applications: Due to its conductivity and resistance to corrosion, gold is widely used in electronics, aerospace applications for heat reflection, and in medicine and dentistry, including advanced uses like gold nanoparticles. 

Gold Reserves in India 

  • Gold in India is a vital mineral resource with economic and strategic importance, found mainly in auriferous rocks and alluvial deposits, and concentrated in the Peninsular Plateau.  
  • Karnataka is the leading producer with major fields like Kolar and Hutti, while Andhra Pradesh follows with Ramagiri. 
    •  The Kolar Gold Fields (KGF) in the Kolar district is one of the world's oldеst and deepest gold minеs. 
  • In terms of reserves, Bihar holds the largest share (~45%), followed by Rajasthan (~23%), with smaller contributions from Jharkhand, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, and Kerala, often in alluvial forms.

India's Tea Industry

25-09-2025

Source: TH

Why in News?

According to the Executive Director of the International Tea Committee, India has the potential to become a global tea superpower, supported by its strong production capacity, large consumption base, and growing export opportunities. 

Current Status of India’s Tea Market

  • Major Producers: India ranks as the 2nd largest producer and consumer of tea and the 3rd largest exporter. Kenya, the top exporter, ships nearly all its output, while China is the 2nd largest exporter.
  • Tea Producing Regions: In India, major tea growing states are Assam (Assam valley and Cachar), West Bengal (Dooars, Terai and Darjeeling), Tamil Nadu and Kerala that account for about 96% of the total tea production.
  • Consumption: India is a top tea-consuming country, using 80% of its production domestically, with a per capita consumption of 840 gm/year (Highest: Turkey, 3 kg/year).
  • Exports: India exports tea to over 25 countries, with major importers including Russia, Iran, UAE, USA, UK, Germany, and China.
    • About 96% of India’s tea exports is black tea, with other exported types including regular, green, herbal, masala, and lemon tea.

What are the Key Facts Regarding Tea?

  • About: Tea is a beverage made from the Camellia sinensis plant and is the world’s most consumed drink after water.
  • Tea Board of India: Established under the Tea Act of 1953, it is a statutory body under the Ministry of Commerce with its head office in Kolkata and overseas offices in London, Dubai, and Moscow.
    • Provides financial and technical support for the cultivation, production, and marketing of tea.
  • Growth Conditions: 
    • Climate: Tea is a tropical and sub-tropical crop that thrives in hot, humid conditions.
    • Temperature: It grows best between 20°–30°C, while temperatures above 35°C or below 10°C can damage the plant.
    • Rainfall: Requires 150–300 cm of well-distributed rainfall annually.
    • Soil: Prefers slightly acidic, calcium-free soil with a porous sub-soil to allow free water percolation.
  •  Factors Contributing to the Crisis in Industry:
    • Weather-Driven Decline: Extreme heat in May 2024 followed by flooding in Assam cut India’s tea production by 30% to 90.92 million kg, its lowest in over a decade.
    • Supply-Demand Imbalance: There is a growing gap between the demand for tea and its supply, creating further pressure on the industry.
    • Stagnant Prices: Assam, which accounts for 55% of India’s tea production, has been particularly affected by stagnant prices.
      Intense Competition: India is facing stiff competition from other major tea-producing countries like Kenya.
    • Rising Input Costs: Over the past decade, the costs of essential inputs have increased by 9-15%, while tea prices have only risen by 4%. 
      • The ban on 20 pesticides raised tea prices due to costly alternatives further straining the profitability of the sector.

How can India Ensure the Sustainability of its Tea Industry?

  • Shift to Quality & Value Addition: Promote GI-tagged, specialty, and branded teas for better export prices.
  • Diversify Export Markets: Target South America, Middle East, and Africa to reduce reliance on volatile markets.
  • Boost Domestic Consumption: Increase per capita intake through marketing, ready-to-drink (RTD), flavored, and wellness teas.
  • Improve Farm-Gate Value: Ensure small growers receive a fairer share via direct markets, Farmer Producer Organizations, (FPOs), and transparent auctions.
  • Learning from Other Countries: Equip farmers to produce high-quality, sustainable tea; Kenya’s Farmer Field Schools (FFSs) provide hands-on training in planting, fine-plucking, and certification preparation.

Conclusion

The Indian tea industry stands at a crossroads, balancing its global potential against severe challenges like climate vulnerability and low value realization. Achieving sustainable profitability requires a strategic shift from volume to quality, empowering small growers, and innovating for both export and domestic markets.

Critical Minerals in India: From Mineral Security to Material Security

20-11-2026

For Prelims: Critical minerals, National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM),  Rare Earth Elements, Semiconductors, National Mineral Exploration and Development Trust, Extended Producer Responsibility 

For Mains: Critical minerals and India's manufacturing ecosystem, Government policies and interventions for development, Resource security and strategic autonomy.

Source: TH 

Why in News?  

India has accelerated its critical minerals policy through the National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM) and amended mining laws. However, auctioning mineral blocks alone cannot guarantee industrial readiness; experts emphasize that India must now shift from mineral extraction to material security by building domestic processing, refining, and recycling capabilities. 

Summary 

  • India has undertaken major reforms through the NCMM, CMRIS and MMDR amendments to boost domestic exploration, reduce import dependence and secure overseas critical mineral supplies. 
  • India must move beyond mineral extraction by strengthening domestic processing, refining, recycling, urban mining and strategic stockpiling to build resilient supply chains and achieve long-term industrial self-reliance.

What are Critical Minerals?

  • About: Critical minerals are metallic or non-metallic elements essential for advanced technologies. There is no universally accepted global definition; countries identify them based on their specific economic importance, substitutability, and supply risks.  
    • They are called critical because their supply disruption can significantly impact a country's economy, national security, and strategic industries due to limited availability and high supply risks. 
  • Prerequisite for Viksit Bharat: Without an uninterrupted, secure supply of these processed inputs, India cannot realise its Net Zero 2070 commitment, energy transition targets, or ambitious domestic manufacturing plans (Make in India).

Current Policy Push for Critical Minerals in India 

  • National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM) (2025): Approved by the Union Cabinet in January 2025 for a 7-year period (FY 2024–25 to FY 2030–31). 
    • NCMM covers the full value chain: 1,200 domestic exploration projects, domestic beneficiation and mineral processing parks, 9 industry-linked Centres of Excellence for R&D, skilling, and a dedicated fast-track clearance window on the PARIVESH portal. 

  • Critical Mineral Recycling Incentive Scheme (CMRIS) (2025): Launched under the NCMM with an outlay of RS 1,500 crore (FY 2025–26 to FY 2030–31) to build recycling capacity for e-waste, lithium-ion battery scrap, and catalytic converters. 
    • CMRIS targets 270–300 kilotonnes of annual recycling capacity (~40 kilotonnes of annual critical mineral output) and overall recovery of 400 kilotonnes from tailings, fly ash, red mud, and end-of-life products by 2031.  
    • Notably, one-third of the outlay is earmarked for startups and smaller recyclers, and incentives are restricted to actual mineral extraction/purification (excluding units producing only black mass). 
  • CAPEX Support Scheme for Critical Mineral Processing (2026): Approved in November 2026 with an outlay of Rs 2,814 crore for FY 2026–27 to FY 2030–31, providing a 30% capital subsidy on eligible investments to promote domestic processing of critical minerals, particularly lithium and nickel. 
  • Lithium-Ion Battery Black Mass Export Ban (2026): The Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT), under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, prohibited exports of specified lithium-ion battery black mass to retain critical minerals for domestic recycling and refining. 
  • Legislative Reforms: 
    • MMDR Amendment Act, 2023: Placed 24 critical and strategic minerals in Part D of the First Schedule of the MMDR Act, 1957, empowering the Central Government to directly auction mineral blocks (with 34 blocks already auctioned and more slated before 2031). 
    • MMDR Amendment Act, 2025: Expanded the mandate of the National Mineral Exploration and Development Trust (NMEDT) to fund overseas exploration and mining, raised lessee contributions from 2% to 3% of royalty, enabled mineral exchanges (regulated by the Indian Bureau of Mines), and introduced intermediary timelines in the Mineral (Auction) Rules,2015 to fast-track mine operationalisation. 
    • MMDR Amendment Act, 2026: Introduced Section 9D into the MMDR Act, 1957, restricting State governments from imposing taxes, cesses or other levies on mineral rights and mineral-bearing lands except under conditions prescribed by the Central Government, with the objective of improving fiscal uniformity and investment predictability. 
  • Trade Support: Basic customs duty has been exempted on critical minerals, lithium-ion battery scrap, and processing capital goods across India's Union Budgets from FY 2024–25 through FY 2026–27. 
  • Rare Earth Corridors: Union Budget 2026–27 announced Dedicated Rare Earth Corridors in Odisha, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu to integrate mining, processing, research and manufacturing. 
  • Rare Earth Permanent Magnet (REPM) Manufacturing Scheme: Approved in November 2025, aiming to develop 6,000 tonnes per annum of integrated domestic manufacturing capacity for magnets used in EVs, wind turbines, electronics and defence. 
  • Resource Diplomacy & Overseas Acquisition: Khanij Bidesh India Limited (KABIL) is actively acquiring overseas lithium and cobalt assets, complemented by India’s participation in global supply-chain coalitions like the Minerals Security Partnership (MSP) and the Quad Critical Minerals Initiative.

What are the Challenges to India’s Critical Minerals Sector?

  • Mineral Security vs. Material Security: India’s current critical minerals policy is heavily skewed toward mineral security (an upstream focus on discovering reserves and measuring success by the number of exploration projects and mineral blocks auctioned) while overlooking the reality that possessing mines does not guarantee usable industrial inputs.  
    • True material security, by contrast, requires shifting focus to the midstream and downstream value chain (beneficiation, chemical refining, recycling, and component manufacturing) and benchmarking success by the actual tonnage of refined and recycled materials delivered to domestic factories. 
    • As China’s global stranglehold demonstrates, supply-chain resilience stems not merely from mining, but from controlling midstream processing and refining at scale; without building domestic refining capacity alongside mining, India risks remaining import-dependent even with vast untapped domestic reserves.  
  • Acute Import Dependence and Forex Pressure: Domestic production lags far behind clean-energy demand.  
    • According to NITI Aayog’s Trade Watch report (2026), India’s metals and ores imports nearly doubled from USD 32.2 billion (2015) to USD 60.5 billion (2025), alongside near-total import reliance for lithium, cobalt, and nickel. 
  • Geopolitical Concentration and Export Restrictions: Global supply chains face severe single-country exposure. UNCTAD (2026) reported that the Democratic Republic of the Congo accounts for 74% of global cobalt and Indonesia supplies 67% of mined nickel.  
    • Furthermore, the OECD found that nearly 70% of global cobalt and manganese exports faced export restrictions between 2022 and 2024, leaving Indian manufacturers vulnerable to abrupt supply shocks. 
  • Long Gestation Periods: Turning an auctioned block into an operational mine takes years due to delayed environmental clearances, land acquisition hurdles, and inadequate mining, power, and logistics infrastructure. 
  • Fragmented Recycling and Urban Mining Ecosystem: Although India generated 3.8 million metric tonnes of e-waste in 2024 (Redseer), a volume set to surge with EV and battery adoption, the scrap economy remains largely informal.  
    • Collection and Traceability: Recyclers lack predictable, traceable access to steady waste streams. 
    • Quality Assurance: Domestic manufacturers remain hesitant to adopt recovered minerals without assured, consistent quality and purity. 
  • Strategic Stockpiling Dilemmas: While a national mineral reserve can buffer short-term supply disruptions, stockpiling is not a standalone long-term strategy. 
    • Rapid technological shifts (such as evolving battery chemistries) continually alter which minerals are strategically vital, complicating decisions on what to stock and at what inventory levels. 
    • Although NCMM envisages the establishment of five strategic mineral stockpiles, their effectiveness will depend on appropriate mineral selection, inventory levels, timely replenishment and mechanisms for responding to supply disruptions. 
  • Low Private and Foreign Investment: Deep-seated mineral exploration is high-risk and capital-intensive.  
    • India has attracted only USD 3.5 billion in cumulative FDI equity inflows into mining between 2000 and 2025, accounting for less than 0.5% of the country’s overall long-term cumulative foreign equity inflows, reflecting regulatory friction and weak global investor appetite. 
  • Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) Constraints: Critical mineral mining carries high ecological costs; a 2026 UN University assessment noted that ~16% of global critical-mineral extraction occurs in areas of high water stress. 
    • Balancing rapid extraction with water security, community livelihoods, and low-carbon trade norms (such as the EU’s CBAM) remains a major hurdle. 
  • On-Ground Implementation Gap: Having rapidly built legal, financial, and institutional frameworks under the NCMM and amended MMDR Acts, India’s toughest challenge now is execution - moving beyond easily measurable exploration targets to capital-intensive, technologically complex refining and recycling.

What Measures are Needed to Shift from Mineral Security to Material Security? 

  • Synchronise Mining with Midstream Processing: Domestic beneficiation and mineral processing parks must be planned and developed simultaneously alongside every mineral auction round. 
    • Integrate the Critical Mineral Processing Scheme and Dedicated Rare Earth Corridors with mining projects to ensure that extracted minerals are converted into refined, industry-grade materials and finished components within India. 
  • Redefine NCMM Success Metrics: Shift policy tracking away from blocks auctioned toward supply-based outcomes (measured in tonnes of refined, industry-grade, and recycled materials) actually delivered to Indian manufacturers. 
  • Formalise and Scale Urban Mining: Leverage the CMRIS and Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) frameworks to build traceable, formal waste-collection networks. 
    • Establish strict certification and purity standards for secondary recovery (from e-waste, tailings, fly ash, and red mud) so domestic battery and electronics makers can confidently integrate recycled inputs. 
    • Leverage the 2026 lithium-ion battery black mass export prohibition and the recycling incentive scheme to secure domestic recycling feedstock, promote mineral recovery and develop an integrated circular economy. 
  • Design Agile, Risk-Based Strategic Stockpiles: Rather than hoarding unprocessed ore, build dynamic, industry-managed, rotating reserves of usable metals, chemicals, and components backed by real-time supply-chain risk intelligence and clear release triggers. 
  • Accelerate Indigenous R&D and Fiscal Incentives: Fast-track proposed fiscal measures such as tax holidays, accelerated depreciation, and R&D deductions and leverage institutional financing (NIIF, EXIM Bank) to crowd in private investment into high-risk refining technologies. 
  • Inclusive Mining Governance: Implement the National Mineral Policy, 2019 to promote environmentally sustainable mining, transparent allocation and equitable benefit-sharing, while leveraging Pradhan Mantri Khanij Kshetra Kalyan Yojana (PMKKKY) and District Mineral Foundations (DMFs) to improve the welfare of mining-affected communities. 

Conclusion 

India must shift from merely auctioning mineral blocks to converting domestic ores and recycled waste into industry-grade materials through domestic processing, formalised recycling and strategic stockpiling, ensuring long-term material security.

Pax Silica & India’s Inclusion

13-01-2026

Source: IE 

Why in News? 

The United States’ newly appointed Ambassador to India, Sergio Gor, stated that India will be invited to join “Pax Silica,” a US-led coalition aimed at securing and strengthening the critical minerals supply chain. 

What is Pax Silica? 

  • About: It is a US led coalition aimed at building a secure, resilient, and innovation-driven silicon and Artificial Intelligence (AI) supply chain ecosystem through deep cooperation with trusted global partners. 
    • The inaugural Pax Silica Summit was held in Washington, D.C. in December, 2025. 
  • Objective: It aims to reduce coercive dependencies on a single country, protect AI-critical materials and enable aligned nations to develop and deploy transformative technologies at scale. 
    • China dominates the critical minerals supply chain needed for the silicon and AI supply chain ecosystem , refining over 60% of lithium, cobalt, and rare earths. Global diversification efforts are accelerating after China’s restrictions on rare earth magnets disrupted supply chains. 
  • Participating Nations: Japan, Republic of Korea, Singapore, the Netherlands, United Kingdom, Israel, United Arab Emirates, and Australia. 
    • Partner countries host key firms such as Sony, Hitachi, Fujitsu, Samsung, SK Hynix, Temasek, DeepMind, MGX, Rio Tinto, and ASML, which power the global AI supply chain. 
  • Core Commitments: Joint projects to address AI supply chain vulnerabilities across critical minerals, semiconductor design, fabrication and packaging, compute infrastructure, and energy grids, along with protecting sensitive technologies from countries of concern. 

Pax Silica and India 

  • India’s Earlier Exclusion: India currently lacks the critical edge technologies that Pax Silica prioritizes and is not a major repository of critical minerals, which limited its inclusion in the grouping. 
    • However, just as India joined the US-led Minerals Security Partnership (MSP) (2022), a year after its launch in 2023, becoming its 14th member, India could similarly be inducted into Pax Silica at a later stage. 

What are India's Initiatives to Support Silicon and AI Supply Chain? 

  • India’s Semiconductor Push: Under the USD 10 billion India Semiconductor Mission (ISM, 2021), India aims to build an indigenous semiconductor ecosystem. 
    • 10 projects approved, involving Rs 1.6 trillion investment, covering fabrication and packaging. 
  • India’s AI Strategy: Rs 10,372 crore IndiaAI Mission (2024) focuses on indigenous Large Language Models (LLMs) and domestic AI capacity. 
    • Graphics processing units (GPUs) capacity expanded to 34,333 GPUs, nearly doubling earlier levels. 
    • Supports a shared cloud-based compute platform for AI training and inference, critical for foundational models tailored to Indian data and context. 
  • National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM): It aims to secure India's self-reliance in critical minerals for high-tech, clean energy, and defense.  
    • The mission covers the full chain—from exploration and mining to processing and recycling—encouraging overseas asset acquisition and international trade ties.  
    • It will establish mineral parks, promote recycling, and support research, including a Centre of Excellence. 
  • Minerals Security Partnership (MSP): The MSP, a US initiative, aims to strengthen critical mineral supply chains by ensuring minerals are produced, processed, and recycled to maximize their economic development benefits. 
    • It directly supports India's mineral self-reliance strategy, complementing domestic actions like the National Critical Minerals Mission and overseas acquisitions by KABIL. It bolsters India’s position in the global race for critical minerals vital for future industries and the energy transition.

Capex Support Scheme to Promote Critical Mineral Processing

06-11-2026

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

The Union Cabinet approved the CAPEX Support Scheme to Promote Critical Mineral Processing to strengthen India's domestic processing capabilities, reduce import dependence and enhance critical mineral supply-chain security.   

What are the Key Features of the CAPEX Support Scheme? 

  • About: The scheme, implemented by the Ministry of Mines, provides targeted capital expenditure (CAPEX) assistance to establish domestic high-purity lithium and nickel processing facilities, addressing a critical gap in India's mineral value chain. 
  • Financial Outlay and Capital Subsidy: The scheme has a total outlay of ₹2,814 crore for five years (FY 2026–27 to FY 2030–31), fully funded through Central Government budgetary support, including ₹2,800 crore for capital subsidies and ₹14 crore for administrative expenses. 
    • It provides a 30% subsidy on eligible investment for establishing four processing plants: 
      • Lithium: Two plants of at least 30 kilotonnes (kt) capacity each, with a maximum subsidy of ₹500 crore per beneficiary. 
      • Nickel: Two plants of at least 50 kt capacity each, with a maximum subsidy of ₹900 crore per beneficiary. 
  • Processing Focus: Lithium plants will convert ores and concentrates (excluding brines) into battery-grade products, while nickel plants will produce Class-1 nickel or battery-grade nickel sulphate, with provision for cobalt recovery. 
  • Beneficiary Selection: Four beneficiaries (two each for lithium and nickel) will be selected through a two-stage competitive process, involving technical qualification followed by financial bidding based on the least-cost criterion (lowest CAPEX support sought). 
  • Milestone-Based Disbursement: Subsidies will be released in three tranches based on investment, equity contribution and commissioning milestones: 
    • 25%: Upon expenditure of 25% of committed CAPEX and contribution of at least 15% of the minimum required equity. 
    • 50% (cumulative): Upon expenditure of 50% of committed CAPEX and contribution of at least 50% of the minimum required equity. 
    • 100% (cumulative): Upon plant commissioning and contribution of 100% of the minimum required equity. 
  • Commissioning Timeline: Plants must be commissioned within three years of the Letter of Award (LoA), with an outer limit of five years. Delays beyond three years will attract progressive reduction (tapering) of the remaining undisbursed subsidy. 
  • Governance Framework: A three-tier institutional mechanism will oversee implementation: 
    • Governing Council (GC): Chaired by the Secretary, Ministry of Mines, it provides strategic guidance, selects beneficiaries, sanctions subsidies and monitors progress. 
    • Techno-Financial Committee (TFC): Evaluates technical and financial proposals and recommends beneficiaries. 
    • Project Management Agency (PMA): Provides administrative and implementation support. 

Why is Strengthening India's Critical Mineral Processing Capacity Important? 

  • India's Critical Minerals: A Ministry of Mines committee identified 30 critical minerals in 2023, while 24 critical and strategic minerals are listed under Part D of the First Schedule of the MMDR Act, 1957. India remains heavily dependent on imported refined mineral products and high-purity intermediates, highlighting gaps in domestic processing capabilities. 
  • Applications: Critical minerals are essential for economic development, national security and the clean energy transition, with applications in electric vehicles, electronics, telecommunications, aerospace and defence. 
  • Strategic Security: Global processing of critical minerals is concentrated in a few countries, exposing India to geopolitical disruptions, export restrictions and price volatility, thereby highlighting the need for diversified and resilient supply chains. 
  • Rising Domestic Demand: Expanding electric mobility, battery storage and advanced manufacturing, alongside India's net-zero target of 2070, is increasing demand for high-purity critical mineral products, necessitating domestic processing capacity. 
  • High Investment and Technological Barriers: Critical mineral processing requires advanced equipment, multi-stage conversion technologies and stringent environmental safeguards, resulting in high capital costs. 
    • Limited access to raw materials, cost-effective technologies and technical expertise further constrains project viability, necessitating targeted government support. 
    • Even with domestic processing facilities, dependence on imported ores and concentrates may persist, highlighting the need for diversified sourcing and overseas mineral partnerships. 
  • Government Initiatives to Strengthen the Critical Mineral Value Chain: 
    • National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM): Launched in January 2025, it aims to secure long-term critical mineral supplies and strengthen the entire value chain, covering exploration, mining, processing and recycling. 
      • A ₹1,500 crore recycling scheme, approved in September 2025, promotes recovery of critical minerals from e-waste, spent lithium-ion batteries and other secondary sources, supporting a circular mineral economy. 
    • Exploration and Mining: The Central Government has auctioned 64 critical and strategic mineral blocks and 11 Exploration Licence blocks, strengthening domestic resource development and supporting downstream processing.     

What are the Expected Outcomes of the CAPEX Support Scheme?  

  • Investment and Capacity Creation: The scheme is expected to attract approximately ₹10,000 crore in investment and establish 60 kilotonnes (kt) of lithium and 100 kt of nickel processing capacity to meet projected demand by 2030. 
  • Domestic Value Addition and Import Substitution: Processing ores, concentrates and intermediates into high-purity products domestically will strengthen mineral value chains, reduce dependence on imported refined products and potentially lower foreign exchange outgo. 
  • Supply Chain Resilience: Domestic processing capacity will reduce exposure to geographically concentrated global refining hubs, export restrictions and supply disruptions. 
  • Strengthening Downstream Industries: Availability of domestically processed minerals will support battery materials, battery cells, electric vehicles, energy storage systems and speciality steel manufacturing. 
  • Employment and Industrial Development: Establishment of processing facilities and expansion of downstream manufacturing will generate direct and indirect employment and promote industrial growth. 

Conclusion 

The CAPEX Support Scheme strengthens India's critical mineral value chain by promoting domestic lithium and nickel processing, reducing import dependence and enhancing supply-chain resilience. Its success will depend on raw material security, technological capabilities and environmental sustainability, advancing India's strategic autonomy and the vision of Atmanirbhar Bharat.
