Source: PIB

Why in News?

World Food India 2025, India’s flagship food processing event, aims to position India as a ‘Global Food Hub’. The event concluded with the signing of Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) worth over Rs 1 lakh crore, highlighting investments, innovations, and employment generation across multiple states.

World Food India

  • WFI, conceptualized by the Ministry of Food Processing Industries (MoFPI), is India’s flagship food processing event. Since its first edition in 2017, followed by the 2nd in 2023, 3rd in 2024, and now the 4th edition in 2025.
  • It has grown to showcase India as the “Food Basket of the World,” promoting investment, enhancing farm-to-fork linkages, encouraging sustainable food systems, and highlighting India’s diverse food culture.

 

What is the Landscape of the Food Processing Sector in India?

  • Food Processing: It involves methods to transform raw agricultural, animal, or fish products into edible, commercially valuable finished or semi-finished products, altering their original physical properties.
  • Levels of Processing:
    • Primary processing: Basic cleaning, grading, and packaging of agricultural products.
    • Secondary processing: Converting ingredients into edible products (e.g., milling wheat into flour).
    • Tertiary processing: Creating ready-to-eat foods (e.g., baking bread from flour).
  • Growth of India’s Food Processing Industry:  India is largest producer of milk, onions and pulses, Second largest producer of rice, wheat, sugarcane, tea, fruits & vegetables and eggs. 
    • India’s agricultural and processed food exports reached USD 49.4 billion in 2024–25, with processed foods contributing 20.4% (up from 13.7% in 2014–15). The sector employs 2.23 million in registered units and 4.68 million in unregistered ones.
    • Registered food business operators rose from 25 lakh to 64 lakh, supported by 24 mega food parks. 
    • Operation Greens and 225 Research and Development projects added 20 patents and 52 commercialized technologies.
  • India’s Initiatives:
    • Production Linked Incentive Scheme for Food Processing Industry (PLISFPI) aims to boost Indian brands internationally.
    • Production Linked Incentive Scheme for Millet-based Products (PLISMBP)  encourages Ready-to-Eat (RTE) and Ready-to-Cook (RTC) millet products, promoting value addition and millet consumption.
    • Pradhan Mantri Kisan Sampada Yojana (PMKSY) creates modern infrastructure, efficient supply chains, and reduces post-harvest losses.
    • PM Formalisation of Micro Food Processing Enterprises Scheme (PMFME)  supports micro food processing units with technical, financial, and business assistance.
    • Under the Make in India campaign, the government allows 100% Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)  in food processing.
    • The Mega Food Park Scheme provides integrated infrastructure for food processing, offering up to Rs 50 crore per project.

What Measures can Strengthen India’s Food Processing Sector?

  • Cluster Development: Set up integrated food processing zones near farm hubs with shared infrastructure (cold storage, labs, effluent plants), ancillary industries (packaging, logistics), and seamless transport links to cut costs and boost rural–urban balance.
  • Tech-Driven Supply Chain: Use blockchain for traceability, IoT for real-time monitoring, AI for demand forecasting, and drones/satellite imaging for crop assessment to cut wastage and improve efficiency.
  • Financial Reforms: Create sector-specific credit schemes aligned with crop cycles, a credit guarantee fund for SMEs, and tax incentives to attract private equity and venture capital in food-tech.
  • Quality Standards: Harmonize Indian norms with global benchmarks (Codex), introduce tiered certifications with market benefits, deploy mobile testing labs, and link quality-based pricing to National Agriculture Market (eNAM).
  • Regulatory Simplification: Establish a single-window clearance and unified digital platform  to cut delays and reduce compliance costs.
  • Export Ecosystem: Develop export-specific zones with plug-and-play facilities, country-focused strategies, and a real-time market intelligence system; fully utilize PLISFPI funds to attract global majors.
  • R&D Boost: Set up Food Innovation Labs, give weighted tax breaks for R&D, and build a national database of traditional food processing techniques for scaling up.

India’s Coconut Sector

27-08-2026

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

Coconut, often referred to as the “Tree of Life”, occupies a unique position in India’s agricultural economy, rural livelihoods, and cultural heritage. With policy interventions like the Coconut Promotion Scheme (Budget 2026–27), the sector is moving towards greater sustainability, value addition, and income enhancement for farmers.

What is India's Contribution in the Global Coconut Economy? 

  • Global Position: India ranks 1st in coconut production and 3rd in cultivation area. India contributes 31.24% of global coconut production and accounts for 17.90% of the global cultivation area. 
  • Production and Productivity:  India produced around 20,301.22 million coconut nuts during 2024–25 from 2.19 million hectares of cultivation area. The average productivity of coconut in India stood at 9,249 nuts per hectare during the period. 
  • State-wise Contribution: Keralam has the largest area under coconut cultivation.  Tamil Nadu leads the country in coconut production, while Andhra Pradesh records the highest productivity, followed by West Bengal and Tamil Nadu. 

  • Growing Role in Agricultural Exports: The coconut sector has become an important contributor to India’s agricultural exports and supports the country’s broader export objectives. 
    • Coconut product exports reached ₹7,038.35 crore (USD 794.70 million) in 2025–26, registering a 62% increase compared to ₹4,349.03 crore in the previous year. 
  • Transformation of Export Basket: India’s coconut exports have evolved significantly from traditional products such as coconut oil, copra, desiccated coconut, and coconut shell charcoal. The sector has diversified into value-added products including virgin coconut oil, coconut milk, coconut water, and activated carbon. 
  • Expansion of Global Markets: Indian coconut products have expanded beyond traditional markets in the Middle East and are now exported to Europe, the Americas, and Australia. Major export destinations include the USA, UAE, Sri Lanka, Germany, Russia, Turkey, Belgium, the UK, and the Netherlands.  

What are the Government Support Measures for the Coconut Sector? 

Coconut Development Board (CDB) 

  • About: The Coconut Development Board (CDB) was established on 12th January 1981 and functions under the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare. 
    • Headquartered in Kochi, the Board works towards the growth and transformation of the coconut sector across 22 States/Union Territories. 
  • Integrated Development: The CDB is responsible for the integrated development of coconut cultivation and industry in India by promoting production, productivity, processing, and market development.  
  • Enhancing Production and Sustainability: The Coconut Development Board (CDB) focuses on improving coconut production through the availability of quality planting material, expansion of cultivation areas, productivity enhancement of existing plantations, and management of major pests and diseases. 
  • Diversification: The CDB promotes product diversification, utilisation of coconut by-products, and technology adoption to strengthen value addition, exports, and income opportunities for coconut farmers.   

MSP for Copra 

  • Coverage: Copra, the dried kernel of coconut, is included among the 22 mandated agricultural crops covered under the Minimum Support Price (MSP) regime. The Union Cabinet has also approved an enhanced MSP for copra for the 2026 season to ensure remunerative returns for coconut growers. 
  • Types of Copra Produced in India: India primarily produces two types of copra: 
    • Milling Copra: It is mainly used for oil extraction and is prepared by drying the coconut kernel after removing it from the shell. 
    • Ball Copra (Edible Copra): It is consumed as a dry fruit and used for religious purposes. It is prepared by drying whole mature coconuts until the kernel hardens inside the shell. 
  • Procurement: The National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation of India Ltd. (NAFED) and the National Cooperative Consumers’ Federation (NCCF) act as the Central Nodal Agencies for copra procurement. State-level agencies are also engaged to support procurement operations.

What are the Government Schemes and Initiatives Related to the Coconut Sector? 

The Government, primarily through the Coconut Development Board, implements a range of schemes to strengthen the coconut ecosystem. These improve access to quality planting material, expand cultivation, enhance productivity, promote technology adoption, and support market development.

Production of Quality Planting Material

  • The scheme strengthens the coconut value chain at the foundation stage by ensuring the availability of quality planting material. It focuses on establishing seed production farms, seed gardens, nurseries, and quality certification systems to improve the supply of healthy coconut seedlings. 

Demonstration Cum Seed Production Farms (DSPFs) 

  • Establishment of DSPFs: The Coconut Development Board (CDB) establishes Demonstration cum Seed Production Farms (DSPFs) across various States to demonstrate scientific coconut cultivation practices and produce commercially viable quality coconut planting materials. 
  • Financial Assistance for New DSPFs: For newly established DSPFs, financial assistance of ₹30 lakh is provided from the budget for the first two years to support farm establishment and development. 
  • Maintenance Support for Existing DSPFs: From the third year onwards, financial assistance of up to ₹1 lakh per hectare per year is provided for maintenance of DSPFs until yield stabilization, which generally takes 15–20 years.  

Other Initiatives Supporting Quality Planting Material Production 

  • Establishment of Nucleus Coconut Seed Garden: The scheme supports the establishment of coconut seed gardens for producing high-quality coconut seedlings and improved varieties.   
  • Establishment of Small Coconut Nursery: The initiative promotes production of quality coconut seedlings through public and private nurseries.  
  • Accreditation and Rating of Coconut Nurseries: The scheme ensures the availability of genuine and high-quality planting material through nursery accreditation, quality assessment, and certification mechanisms. 
  • Assistance for Quality Planting Material Production in Public Sector Nurseries: The scheme provides financial assistance to eligible public sector nurseries, including State Departments, State Agricultural Universities (SAUs), Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs), Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs), and DSP Farms of the Coconut Development Board, for strengthening seedling production.

Expansion of the Area Under Coconut Cultivation 

  • Objective: The programme aims to expand coconut cultivation in suitable new areas by providing financial assistance, incentive-based subsidies, and technical support for scientific cultivation practices. It also focuses on stabilising existing coconut-growing areas to enhance overall production. 
  • Financial Assistance for New Plantations: Under the scheme, financial assistance of ₹56,000 per hectare is provided for planting coconut seedlings in new areas. The assistance is released in two equal annual instalments. 
  • Eligibility under the Scheme: The subsidy is available for a maximum area of 2 hectares and a minimum area of 0.08 hectares (20 cents) per beneficiary to encourage wider participation among farmers.  
  • Expansion of Coconut Area During 2025-26: During 2025-26, major emphasis was placed on expanding coconut cultivation, with around 2,175 hectares of new area brought under cultivation across several States. 
  • States Covered Under Expansion Programme: The expansion programme covered major coconut-growing and potential States, including Keralam, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, West Bengal, Gujarat, Assam, and Arunachal Pradesh.  

Comprehensive Programme for Sustainable Productivity Improvement in Existing Coconut Holdings 

  • Objective: The scheme aims to improve the production and productivity of existing coconut holdings through the adoption of integrated farming practices, including coconut-based cropping systems. 
  • Productivity Improvement Through Coconut-Based Cropping System (PICCS): The Productivity Improvement Through Coconut-Based Cropping System (PICCS) is a major component of the programme that promotes sustainable productivity enhancement in coconut plantations. 
  • Implementation of PICCS: The programme is implemented on a cluster basis through the Departments of Agriculture/Horticulture of State Governments and directly by the Coconut Development Board (CDB). 
  • Financial Assistance under PICCS: The scheme provides 100% subsidy of the cost, amounting to ₹42,000 per hectare, with a limit of 2 hectares per beneficiary. The assistance is released in two equal annual instalments. 
  • State Government Convergence Support: Major regions covered under PICCS included Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Keralam, Assam, Goa, and Puducherry, with Keralam accounting for a significant share during 2025–26. 
  • Other Components under the Programme: 
    • Water Resource Creation: The scheme supports the development of community tanks, farm ponds, and reservoirs to improve irrigation facilities and ensure better water availability in coconut gardens. 
    • Organic Farming Promotion: The programme encourages farmers to adopt organic farming practices by providing support for production, certification, processing, and marketing of organic products. 
    • Farm Mechanisation: The scheme promotes the adoption of agricultural machinery and modern technologies through training, demonstrations, and financial assistance for equipment purchase, post-harvest management, and processing activities. 

Replanting and Rejuvenation of Coconut Gardens 

  • Objective: The scheme aims to enhance coconut production and productivity by replacing disease-affected, unproductive, old, and senile coconut palms with quality seedlings. 
  • Rejuvenation of Existing Plantations: The scheme focuses on rejuvenating remaining coconut palms through an integrated package of practices to restore productivity and improve the health of coconut gardens. 
  • Project-Based Implementation: The scheme is implemented on a project-by-project basis to address state-specific challenges and plantation-related issues. 
  • Financial Assistance under the Scheme: Financial assistance of up to ₹54,000 per hectare is provided over a period of two years to support replanting and rejuvenation activities. 
  • Major Activities Undertaken: The key activities under the scheme include: 
    • Removal of diseased and unproductive coconut palms. 
    • Replanting of quality coconut seedlings. 
    • Rejuvenation and improvement of existing coconut plantations through scientific practices. 

Complementary Initiatives Strengthening the Coconut Ecosystem 

  • Technology Mission on Coconut (TMOC): The initiative supports the development and adoption of technologies for pest and disease management, processing, product diversification, and other technology-based interventions. 
  • Kera Suraksha Insurance Scheme: The scheme provides accident insurance coverage to coconut tree climbers, Neera technicians, hybridization workers, and other labourers engaged in the coconut sector. 

Coconut Promotion Scheme 

  • Objective: The Coconut Promotion Scheme was announced in the Union Budget 2026-27 to address challenges such as pest outbreaks, root wilt disease, and aging coconut plantations, while improving the sustainability of the sector. 
  • Enhancing Production and Productivity: The scheme focuses on quality planting material production, expansion of coconut cultivation in suitable areas, and replanting/rejuvenation of old and unproductive plantations to increase productivity. 
  • Crop Health Management: It promotes integrated crop health management practices for controlling pests and diseases and improving the resilience of coconut plantations. 
  • Value Addition and Financial Support: The scheme supports integrated coconut processing units for value addition and exports, under the broader ₹350 crore allocation for high-value agriculture covering coconut, cashew, and cocoa. 
  • Long-Term Vision: The initiative aims to strengthen the resilience of the coconut sector, improve farmer livelihoods, and position India as a global leader in coconut and coir exports by the 2030s. 

Skill Development and Enterprise Creation Initiatives  

  • Skill Development Programmes by CDB: The Coconut Development Board (CDB) implements flagship skill development programmes to create a trained, self-reliant, and enterprise-ready workforce across activities such as coconut harvesting, processing, and value addition. 
  • Promotion of Coconut-Based Micro Enterprises: Through initiatives like Kera Kaushal Kendra, the CDB promotes coconut-based micro-enterprises, rural entrepreneurship, sustainable livelihoods, and local value addition. 
  • Awareness and Capacity Building: During 2025-26, the Coconut Development Board conducted 414 awareness training programmes covering CDB schemes, scientific coconut cultivation practices, and coconut value addition techniques. 

Conclusion 

India’s coconut sector represents the convergence of agriculture, culture, livelihood security and export potential. The transformation from a traditional plantation crop to a modern value-added industry demonstrates the importance of integrated policy support. With continued innovation and institutional support, coconut can become a key contributor to sustainable agriculture, rural prosperity and the vision of Viksit Bharat@2047.

India’s Makhana Sector

21-08-2026

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

The Government has highlighted India’s growing makhana sector, following the launch of the National Makhana Board in Bihar on 15th September 2025 and approval of a Central Sector Scheme for the Development of Makhana with an outlay of ₹476.03 crore for 2025–26 to 2030–31. 

What is the Current Status of India’s Makhana Sector? 

  • Global Leadership: India is the world’s largest producer of makhana, with Bihar accounting for nearly three-fourths of India’s production and around 80–85% of global supply. 
  • Production Hub: Cultivation is concentrated mainly in North Bihar, particularly the Kosi basin districts of Supaul, Saharsa and Madhepura, along with the Mithila and Seemanchal regions. 
  • Rising Production and Productivity: India’s production increased from 63,910 MT in 2024–25 to an estimated 80,590 MT in 2025–26, while average productivity rose from 2.03 MT/ha to 2.34 MT/ha. 
    • Bihar alone produced around 60,000 MT in 2025–26, with productivity of about 2.00 MT/ha. 
  • Domestic Market Growth and Potential: The domestic market grew by around 17–18% annually between 2021–22 and 2024–25, driven by health awareness, premiumisation and growth of branded products, and is projected to reach ₹11,000–12,000 crore by 2029–30.  
  • Rising Prices: Average prices increased from about ₹500/kg during 2020–22 to nearly ₹1,250/kg in 2025, reflecting strong demand and relatively slower supply growth. 
  • Domestic Consumption: Monthly consumption of popped makhana is estimated at 3,000–3,500 MT, rising to around 5,000 MT during festive periods. 
  • Export Growth: After the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) introduced separate HSN codes for makhana products in 2025, India exported 7,264.89 MT worth ₹19,296.08 lakh in 2025–26. 
  • Export Markets and Diversification: The US (40%), Canada (20%) and UAE (17%) account for around 77% of exports, while markets such as Germany, Nepal and Australia offer higher unit-price realisations, indicating scope for diversification towards premium markets.  

Key Facts about Makhana 

  • About Makhana: Makhana (Euryale ferox), or fox nut, is an aquatic crop traditionally grown in shallow ponds, lakes and wetlands. Its edible seeds are processed and popped for consumption. 
  • Major Growing States: It is cultivated mainly in Bihar, West Bengal and Assam, supported by favourable agro-climatic conditions and traditional expertise. 
  • GI Tag: Mithila Makhana has received a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, recognising the unique quality, reputation and traditional knowledge associated with makhana from Bihar’s Mithila region. 
  • Changing Cultivation Practices: Alongside traditional pond cultivation, field-system farming is increasingly being adopted as a modern cultivation practice.  
  • Improved Varieties: Improved varieties such as Swarna Vaidehi and Sabour Makhana-1 have contributed to higher productivity. 
  • Nutritional Value: Makhana is rich in carbohydrates, protein, dietary fibre and minerals such as magnesium, potassium, phosphorus and iron, while being low in fat and sodium and cholesterol-free. 
  • Health-Food Appeal: Its nutritional profile, low glycaemic load and growing preference for healthy, plant-based foods have transformed makhana from a traditional food into a widely consumed health and premium snack. 

How is the Government Strengthening the Makhana Sector? 

  • National Makhana Board: Announced in Union Budget 2025–26 and launched in Bihar on 15 September 2025. 
  • Central Sector Scheme for the Development of Makhana: ₹476.03 crore for 2025–26 to 2030–31. 
  • Focus Areas: Research, quality seeds, farmer training, harvesting, post-harvest management, value addition, branding, marketing, exports and quality control. 
  • Allocation: ₹30 crore in 2025–26 and ₹90 crore in 2026–27. 
  • Around 8,159 farmers benefited in 2025–26. 
  • 1,010 additional hectares brought under cultivation and 73 hectares under seed production. 
  • 89 Front Line Demonstrations conducted through State Agriculture Universities (SAUs), Central Agricultural Universities (CAUs) and Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) institutions.

How are Processing, Research and Technology Strengthening the Makhana Value Chain? 

  • Farm-Level Production: Makhana cultivation involves pond preparation, seed broadcasting, crop management and labour-intensive manual harvesting, including underwater seed collection. 
  • Primary Processing: Seeds undergo drying, cleaning, roasting and popping, followed by polishing, sorting and grading according to size and quality. 
  • Secondary Processing and Value Addition: The sector is expanding into flavoured makhana, ready-to-eat snacks, makhana flour and other processed products, creating opportunities for higher value realisation and employment. 
  • Role of Start-ups and FPOs: Start-ups and Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) are strengthening processing, branding, value addition and market access. 
  • Role of NRCM: The National Research Centre for Makhana (NRCM) promotes improved cultivation and processing practices, including water-efficient and integrated farming systems and makhana-cum-fish farming. 
  • Seed and Capacity Support: It has distributed around 15,824.1 kg of high-yielding makhana seeds and trained more than 3,000 farmers between 2012 and 2023. 
  • Mechanisation and Enterprise Support: NRCM has provided technical support to 24 enterprises and developed technologies such as seed washers, graders, roasting and popping machines, which have been licensed for commercial use. 
  • Overall Potential: Strong demand, processing, research and technological improvements can help raise farmer incomes, rural employment, value addition and India’s agri-food exports. 

Conclusion 

India’s makhana sector has transformed from a traditional regional crop into a fast-growing superfood economy. With Bihar at the centre of global production, rising domestic demand, export opportunities, value-added products and institutional support, makhana can become a major driver of farmer income, rural employment and agricultural exports.

Pradhan Mantri Formalisation of Micro Food Processing Enterprises Scheme

18-10-2025

Source: ET

Why in News? 

The Union Finance Minister of India announced the release of ₹3,791.1 crore to States and Union Territories under the Pradhan Mantri Formalisation of Micro Food Processing Enterprises (PM-FME) Scheme. 

What is the PM-FME Scheme? 

  • About: PM-FME aims to strengthen micro food enterprises, promote ‘Vocal for Local’ products, and integrate rural entrepreneurs into the formal economy. 
    • Launched on 29 June 2020, the PM-FME Scheme is a Centrally Sponsored Scheme under the Atmanirbhar Bharat Abhiyan. 
    • It was launched for a duration of 2020–21 to 2025–26 with a total outlay  of ₹10,000 crore. 
  • Salient Features of the Scheme:  
    • Common Infrastructure: FPOs, Cooperatives, SHGs, and Government agencies setting up food processing units with shared facilities are eligible for a 35% credit-linked subsidy, up to ₹3 crore. 
    • Credit-Linked Subsidy: Individuals, FPOs, NGOs, Cooperatives, SHGs, and Pvt. Ltd. firms can avail a 35% subsidy, up to ₹10 lakh per unit, for upgrading or setting up new units. 
    • One District One Product (ODOP): Implements an ODOP approach to promote scale, value chain development, and marketing support- covering 713 districts in 35 States/UTs with 137 unique products. 
    • Seed Capital for Self Help Groups: Provides ₹40,000 per member, up to ₹4 lakh per SHG, as seed capital for working capital and small tools, disbursed through SHG federations. 
    • Marketing & Branding: Offers a 50% grant for branding and marketing initiatives by FPOs, SHGs, Cooperatives, or SPVs to promote processed food products. 
    • Capacity Building: Provides training under the Food Processing Entrepreneurship Development Programme to enhance technical and business skills of beneficiaries. 
  • Cost Sharing Pattern: 60:40 between Centre and States, 90:10 for North Eastern and Himalayan States, 60:40 for UTs with legislature, and 100% Central funding for other UTs. 

What are the Other Key Government Initiatives Related to the Food Processing Sector? 

  • Pradhan Mantri Kisan Sampada Yojana 
  • Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme for the food processing industry. 
  • Priority Sector Lending (PSL): Food and agro-processing units, along with cold chain infrastructure, are now recognized as priority sectors. 
  • 100% FDI under Automatic Route: The food processing sector can receive full foreign direct investment through the automatic approval route. 
  • Special Food Processing Fund: Establishment of a ₹2,000 crore fund with the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD). 
  • The Mega Food Park Scheme provides integrated infrastructure for food processing, offering up to Rs 50 crore per project. 

Frequently Asked Questions

A scheme launched in 2020 to formalize micro food enterprises, promote local products, enhance rural income, and integrate entrepreneurs into the formal economy.

2. What support does PM-FME provide?
Provides capital subsidies, grants, seed funding, training, branding, marketing, common infrastructure, and R&D support to individual units, FPOs, cooperatives, and SHGs.

3. What is the ODOP approach?
Each district focuses on one key product, promoting cluster-based processing, storage, branding, market linkages, and aligning with India’s Agriculture Export Policy.

4. What challenges does the sector face?
Infrastructure gaps, limited finance access, and weak quality standards hinder growth, export potential, and competitiveness of India’s food processing industry.

China’s Entry In Spice Market

17-01-2026

Source: ET 

China has begun cultivating and exporting chillies and cumin, two of India’s most critical spice exports, at lower and more competitive prices, posing a potential challenge to India’s long-standing dominance as the world’s largest spice supplier. 

  • Chillies form the backbone of India’s spice exports, accounting for over 25% of total export volume and value. Cumin, meanwhile, is among the most in-demand high-value spices, particularly in West Asia, Europe, and the Americas, where it is widely used in food processing and culinary applications. 
  • Export Performance: In 2024–25, India recorded strong growth in spice export volumes, with chilli powder exports rising 35% to 80.6 million kg and total chilli exports increasing 19% to over 700,000 tonnes, compared to 15% growth in 2023–24. 
  • Price Pressure: Despite higher volumes, chilli export earnings declined 11% due to severe global price pressure, even as cumin exports surged 39% to 2.29 lakh tonnes from 1.65 lakh tonnes in 2023–24. 
  • China’s Strategy: China is strengthening its presence in the global spice market by focusing on high-demand chilli varieties such as paprika (for colour and mild flavour) and Teja chilli (for high pungency and pharmaceutical use), while importing raw Indian chillies, processing them domestically, and re-exporting finished products to third-country markets at competitive prices. 
  • Impact on Indian Agriculture: India is simultaneously facing supply-side challenges: 
    • Chilli acreage has declined by nearly 35% in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Karnataka, the country’s key chilli-producing states. Cumin cultivation area has fallen by 7–8%. 
    • These declines are driven by weather-related crop losses and persistently low export prices, which have discouraged farmers from sowing chillies and cumin, particularly during the kharif season.

Empowering Farmers for Sustainable and Inclusive Agriculture

15-06-2026

For Prelims: PM-KISAN, PMFBY, MSP, e-NAM, Kisan Credit Card, PMKSY, PM KUSUM, Pradhan Mantri Dhan-Dhaanya Krishi Yojana, Agricultural Infrastructure Fund (AIF), National Mission on Edible Oils, Crop Insurance, Soil Health Card, Digital Agriculture, Mega Food Parks 

For Mains: Agricultural Policy Reforms, Financial Inclusion and Social Security of Farmers, Market Access and Infrastructure Development, Technology-Driven and Sustainable Agriculture

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

India’s agricultural sector has transformed significantly over the past 12 years, moving beyond welfare support toward productivity, income security, market access and resilient infrastructure. Schemes such as PM-KISAN, PMFBY, e-NAM and expanded MSP operations have strengthened income stability and market assurance for farmers. 

Summary 

  • Initiatives like PM-KISAN, PMFBY and MSP reforms have strengthened income stability, risk mitigation, and direct financial assistance for over 9 crore farmers. 
  • Investments in AIF, e-NAM, Mega Food Parks, and expanded Kisan Credit Card & Ground Level Credit have improved farm-to-market linkages, digital integration, and timely access to affordable credit. 
  • Farmer empowerment in India requires addressing income, climate, credit, infrastructure and technology gaps through stronger market access, risk mitigation, digital tools, sustainable practices, social security and value-added diversification.  

What is the Current Status of Agriculture in India? 

Growth and Economic Contribution 

  • The agriculture and allied sectors contribute around 18% of India’s Gross Value Added (GVA), making them a critical part of the economy. 
  • Over the past decade, the sector’s GVA increased from ₹20.9 lakh crore (2014–15) to ₹48.7 lakh crore (2023–24), reflecting a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 8.83% at current prices. 
  • Within agriculture, the crop segment GVA nearly doubled, rising from ₹12.92 lakh crore to ₹26.52 lakh crore, indicating sustained growth in primary crop production. 
  • Policy focus and public investment have been central drivers, with budgetary allocations rising from ₹27,663 crore in 2013–14 to ₹1,40,528.78 crore for 2026–27, reflecting significant investment in agricultural infrastructure, input support, and development programs. 

Crop Production and Food Security 

  • India’s total foodgrain production rose from 265.05 million tonnes in 2013–14 to 357.73 million tonnes in 2024–25, reflecting a significant increase in agricultural output. The growth was driven by higher production of rice, wheat, maize and coarse cereals, including millets, which are now promoted as Shree Anna. 
    • The National Food Security and Nutrition Mission supported this expansion by promoting improved seeds, better agronomic practices and technology adoption for rice, wheat, pulses and coarse cereals.  
  • India became the world’s largest rice producer, with rice production up 42.38% since 2014–15. Wheat production reached a record high of 117.94 million tonnes in 2024–25, while maize production nearly doubled, registering about a 79% increase during the same period.  
  • Oilseeds registered the highest production of 42.99 Mt (2024-25), which is 56% higher than 2014-15 (27.51 Mt), contributing to self-reliance and reducing edible oil import dependence from 63.2% (2015–16) to 56.25% (2023–24). 
  • Edible oil import dependence declined from 63.2 percent in 2015-16 to 56.25 percent in 2023-24, indicating gradual progress.  
  • The horticulture sector has become a major driver of agricultural growth, contributing nearly 37% of the crop sector’s Gross Value Output and reflecting a shift towards high-value crops driven by better farming practices and rising market demand.   

Farmer Welfare and Social Security 

  • Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN) provides ₹6,000 per year per eligible farmer family through Direct Benefit Transfer(DBT), reaching over 9.44 crore farmers. Over 25% of benefits have accrued to women beneficiaries, reflecting the scheme’s inclusive outreach across rural households.   
  • Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) provides farmers with a simple, affordable, and comprehensive crop insurance system. PMFBY covers the entire crop cycle, including pre-sowing and post-harvest losses.  
    • The scheme follows the principle of “One Nation, One Crop, One Premium”, ensuring uniform premium rates across the country.  
    • More than 4 crore farmers are insured annually under the scheme, reflecting its wide outreach and growing acceptance among cultivators.  
  • Pension and Social Security: Pradhan Mantri Kisan Maan-Dhan Yojana (PM-KMY)  provides a pension of ₹3,000 per month after age 60; about 24.95 lakh farmers enrolled as of Feb 2026. 
  • Minimum Support Price (MSP) and Procurement: The Minimum Support Price (MSP) acts as a safeguard by ensuring pre-determined prices for agricultural produce. MSPs are announced annually for 22 mandated crops. Since 2018-19, MSPs have been fixed at a minimum of 50 percent above the cost of production.  
    • Public procurement backed by MSP support expanded significantly, with the total MSP value during 2014–2026 being 3.5 times higher than during 2004–2014, reflecting stronger market support for farmers.

Agricultural Policy Reforms (2014-2026)  

Diversification into Allied Sectors 

  • Livestock: GVA increased from ₹5.10 lakh crore to ₹15.06 lakh crore during FY 2014–15 to FY 2026–27. India is the largest global milk producer, with production rising from 146.31 MT (2014–15) to 247.87 MT (2024–25). 
  • Poultry & Meat: Under the National Livestock Mission (NLM) and allied animal husbandry initiatives, egg production rose from 78.48 billion to 149.11 billion, while meat production increased from 6.69 MT to 10.50 MT. 
  • Fisheries & Aquaculture: Supported by the Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY), fisheries production increased from 9.58 MT to 19.78 MT, with 2,195 FFPOs formed and 4.39 lakh fishers covered under KCC. 
  • Bio-energy & Beekeeping: Under the National Beekeeping & Honey Mission (NBHM) and Ethanol Blended Petrol Programme, honey production rose from 0.081 MT to 0.152 MT, while ethanol procurement increased from 38 crore litres to 904 crore litres, achieving 20% blending. 

Technology, Digitalization, and Sustainability 

  • Digital Agriculture: Under the Digital Agriculture Mission, 7.63 crore Farmer IDs have been created and 23.5 crore crop plots digitized to improve planning, targeting, transparency and delivery of farmer-centric services. 
  • AgriStack and Digital Public Infrastructure: Initiatives such as AgriStack, digital crop surveys, Farmer Registry and geo-referenced land records are enabling better identification of beneficiaries, faster scheme delivery and data-driven agricultural planning. 
  • Kisan e-Mitra and AI-based Advisory: Digital advisory platforms such as Kisan e-Mitra are helping farmers access timely information on schemes, weather, crops, inputs, pest management and government support. 
  • Drone Technology in Agriculture: The Namo Drone Didi Scheme and drone-based agricultural services are promoting precision farming, crop monitoring, pesticide spraying and women-led agri-entrepreneurship. 
  • Renewable Energy: PM-KUSUM promotes solar pumps and renewable energy use in agriculture with 10 lakh standalone solar pumps installed and 13 lakh grid-connected pumps solarized. 
  • Soil Health & Organic Farming: 26 crore Soil Health Cards have been issued and 6.85 lakh seed villages established. Schemes such as PKVY (Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana), MOVCDNER (Mission Organic Value Chain Development for North Eastern Region) and NMNF (National Mission on Natural Farming) are promoting organic, natural and chemical-free farming across large areas. 
  • Climate-Resilient Farming: 2,996 climate-resilient crop varieties have been released, 448 villages promoted as climate-resilient models, and Integrated Farming Systems expanded to 1.04 lakh farm households. 
  • Water-Use Efficiency: Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY), especially the “Per Drop More Crop” component, is promoting micro-irrigation, efficient water use and climate-resilient agriculture. 
  • Seed and Crop Improvement: Initiatives under ICAR, seed village programmes and climate-resilient variety development are improving access to quality seeds, better yields and stress-tolerant crops. 
  • Extension Services: 1.27 crore farmers have been trained under Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) and ATMA programmes between 2021 and 2025, improving adoption of modern farming, pest management and sustainable agricultural practices. 

Credit, Financial Inclusion, and Institutional Support 

  • Kisan Credit Cards (KCCs) Scheme: The scheme provides a single window for farmers to meet their short-term and investment needs in agriculture and allied activities.  
  • Ground Level Credit (GLC) to agriculture rose from ₹7.30 lakh crore (2013–14) to ₹28.67 lakh crore (2024–25), improving access to working and investment capital. 
  • Cooperative Sector Reforms: 
    • Ministry of Cooperation strengthened Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS); 79,630 PACS computerised, 61,866 onboarded on ERP systems. 
    • 10,000 Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) registered by 2026, enabling collective marketing and better bargaining power. 
  • National Agriculture Market e-NAM: e-NAM is a pan-India digital trading platform that links agricultural markets to improve transparent price discovery, online trading and digital payments. So far, 1,656 mandis have been integrated, with 1.8 crore farmers and 4,724 FPOs registered. 
  • Agriculture Infrastructure Fund: AIF supports the creation of post-harvest infrastructure such as warehouses, cold chains, logistics and processing units to strengthen agricultural supply chains. So far, ₹84,202 crore has been sanctioned for 1.68 lakh projects, mobilising ₹1.33 lakh crore in total investment. 
  • Mega Food Parks: The Mega Food Parks scheme promotes integrated food-processing clusters with storage, processing, logistics and value-addition facilities. The number of parks increased from 2 in 2014 to 41 by 2025, helping reduce wastage and improve farmer-market linkages.  
  • Agricultural Exports: India’s agricultural exports increased from USD 37.29 billion in 2013–14 to USD 51.1 billion in 2024–25, reflecting stronger global demand and improved competitiveness in agri-trade. 
  • Value-Added Export Growth: The share of processed products rose from 13.7% to 20.4%, while seafood exports doubled from USD 3.64 billion to USD 7.52 billion, reaching over 130 countries and signalling a shift towards higher-value, quality-driven exports.  

What are the Key Challenges Affecting Farmers in India? 

  • Income and Market Risks: Farmers face price volatility and market uncertainty, especially for perishable and high-value crops. This risk is sharper for small and marginal farmers, who form about 86% of operational holdings but operate less than half of the agricultural area, limiting their marketable surplus and bargaining power.   
  • Limited MSP Reach: Although MSP and procurement have expanded, their benefits are concentrated mainly in crops like paddy and wheat and in better-procurement States such as Punjab, Haryana and parts of western Uttar Pradesh. Many farmers growing pulses, oilseeds, vegetables or fruits still sell in local or unorganised markets, often below remunerative prices.  
  • Climatic and Natural Risks: Agriculture is highly weather-dependent, with frequent exposure to droughts, floods, cyclones, and pest attacks. Extreme heat is emerging as a major threat to agriculture in South Asia, reducing labour productivity, stressing crops and livestock, and increasing the need for climate-resilient seeds, irrigation and early-warning systems. 
  • Limited Access to Credit & Financial Inclusion: Although KCCs and Ground Level Credit have expanded, access to timely and affordable loans is still limited in remote and marginalized regions. 
  • Resource and Input Constraints: Farmers face rising stress over water, soil health, quality seeds and balanced fertiliser use. For example, groundwater-dependent farming in northwestern India has boosted output but also created long-term sustainability concerns, while excessive urea use and inadequate micronutrient management affect soil productivity. 

What Measures are Needed to Empower India’s Farmers? 

  • Strengthening Income Support and Risk Mitigation: Expand schemes like PM-KISAN to provide direct income support and ensure timely disbursal through DBT. Broaden PMFBY coverage for crop insurance, including pre-sowing, standing crops, and post-harvest losses, with improved grievance redressal through portals like Krishi Rakshak Helpline. 
  • Expanding Credit and Financial Inclusion: Increase access to Kisan Credit Cards (KCCs) and Ground Level Credit for small, marginal, and remote farmers.  Encourage formal financial institutions to provide affordable loans for both cultivation and post-harvest activities. 
  • Developing Infrastructure and Post-Harvest Facilities: Expand Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF) projects for storage, cold chains, logistics, and processing at the farm gate. Promote Mega Food Parks and modern collection centers to reduce post-harvest losses and improve supply chain efficiency. 
  • Promoting Technology Adoption and Digital Tools: Strengthen digital initiatives like Kisan e-Mitra, Digital Agriculture Mission, and Drone-based programs to improve farm management and advisory services. Train farmers through KVKs and ATMA programs to adopt modern farming techniques, precision agriculture, pest management, and climate-resilient practices. 
  • Encouraging Sustainable and Climate-Resilient Practices:  Promote organic and natural farming through PKVY, NMNF, and climate-resilient crop varieties. Improve water-use efficiency via PMKSY and support renewable energy adoption through PM KUSUM (solar pumps and grid-connected solutions).

Conclusion 

India’s agricultural transformation over the past decade shows that sustainable farmer empowerment depends on a coordinated approach—where the Centre provides policy vision, financial backing, and technology, while States and local institutions adapt and implement reforms suited to regional realities—ensuring resilience, productivity, and enhanced income security for farmers.

What is the National Beekeeping and Honey Mission?

    • Strengthen infrastructure, quality control, traceability, and agri-entrepreneurship while empowering women and fostering cooperatives to enhance technology use, marketing, and export potential. 
  • Ranking:  
    • India is the second largest exporter of honey, after China, as of 2024. 
    • Major Indian states producing honey include Uttar Pradesh (17%), West Bengal (16%), Punjab (14%), Bihar (12%) and Rajasthan (9%). 
    • Major export destinations included the USA, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Libya.

National Bee Board (NBB) 

  • The National Bee Board was registered as a society under the Societies Registration Act, 1860 on July 19, 2000, and reconstituted under the Chairmanship of the Secretary (A&C) in June 2006. 
  • The primary objective of NBB is to promote scientific beekeeping, enhance crop productivity through pollination, and increase honey production to boost the income of beekeepers and farmers. 
  • It has been designated as the Nodal Agency for the overall development and promotion of scientific beekeeping in the country .

Strengthening Food Safety in India

02-07-2026

Source: TH 

Why in News?  

Large-scale food poisoning incidents across India have brought the country's food safety infrastructure under scrutiny, highlighting gaps in the implementation of the Food Safety and Standards (FSS) Act, 2006 and concerns over rising food poisoning deaths.  

What are the Concerns Regarding Food Safety in India? 

  • Food Poisoning: According to the Accidental Deaths and Suicides in India (ADSI) Report 2024, 1,122 people died due to food poisoning in India. 
    • Globally, the WHO Foodborne Disease Estimates 2026 indicate that unsafe food causes 866 million illnesses, 1.5 million deaths, with children under five bearing nearly 30% of the disease burden.  
    • Despite a decline in the global burden since 2000, India ranks 15th worldwide in Years of Life Lost (YLL) due to foodborne diseases.  
  • State Food Safety Index: The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) assesses the State Food Safety Index based on human resources, regulatory compliance, food testing and surveillance, training and capacity building, and consumer empowerment.  
    • In 2023–24, nearly 75% of States and Union Territories scored below 50 out of 100, reflecting weak food safety systems.  
    • Among the poorest performers, Jharkhand scored 26.5 and Uttar Pradesh 44.25, both of which also reported a high number of food poisoning deaths in 2024.  
  • Gaps in Regulatory Inspections and Sampling:   The FSS Act, 2006, mandates the registration and licensing of Food Business Operators (FBOs), periodic inspections, and laboratory testing of food samples.  
    • The Food Safety and Standards (Licensing and Registration of Food Businesses) Amendment Regulations, 2026, introduce a risk-based inspection framework, where inspection frequency is determined by an establishment's risk profile and compliance history.  
    • However, implementation remains weak, as evidenced by Maharashtra, where only around 21,000 food samples were collected in 2024–25 despite having over 1.8 lakh registered FBOs.  
  • Institutional and Manpower Deficits: The FSSAI operates with nearly 40% of its central positions vacant. At the state level, the crisis is worse as of early 2026, over 1,200 sanctioned Food Safety Officer (FSO) positions were unfilled across India.  
    • For example, Maharashtra needs 1,100 food safety officers but has only 130 on field. 
  • Chemical Hazards and Heavy Metals: Chemical hazards are emerging as a major food safety concern, with the World Health Organization (WHO) highlighting the growing risks from heavy metal contamination such as inorganic arsenic in rice and groundwater and lead in spices, cereals, and cookware, which contribute to chronic diseases and cognitive impairment in children.  
    • Additionally, the indiscriminate use of antibiotics in livestock and dairy farming is accelerating Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR), posing a serious threat to public health.  
    • Chronic exposure to unregulated pesticide residues is linked to an increased risk of cancer and endocrine disorders.  
  • Food Adulteration: It has evolved into a large-scale parallel economy, particularly in the dairy and edible oil sectors.  
    • The use of synthetic and toxic additives, such as ethylene glycol-contaminated milk, poses serious public health risks, while the widespread prevalence of counterfeit products like synthetic milk and fake paneer reflects the scale of the challenge in ensuring food authenticity and safety.  
  • Misleading marketing ("health-washing"): It has emerged as a growing food safety concern, with some FBOs using deceptive labels and unsubstantiated health claims to promote products.  
    • In 2025–26, the FSSAI initiated regulatory action against several brands for misleading claims, including the misuse of terms such as "100% Natural" and inaccurate nutritional or disease-curing claims. 
  • Temperature Anomalies: Rising baseline temperatures and frequent heatwaves accelerate bacterial proliferation and food spoilage, particularly in regions without robust cold-chain networks.  
  • Hygiene Bottlenecks: They often lack access to clean municipal water, proper waste disposal, and basic food-handling training, making them primary vectors for water-borne and food-borne pathogens (like Salmonella and E. coli).

Food Safety 

  • About: Food safety is a scientific discipline that dictates the handling, preparation, and storage of food to prevent foodborne illnesses. 
    • While food safety focuses on preventing accidental contamination (biological, chemical, physical), it frequently overlaps with food defense, which is the protection of the food supply from intentional contamination or tampering.  
  • Institutional Framework in India: 
    • Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI): It is the apex statutory body established under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, functioning under the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare (MoHFW) to regulate food safety standards, licensing, and scientific risk assessment.  
      • Its key initiatives include Eat Right India, the State Food Safety Index, RUCO (Repurpose Used Cooking Oil), and the Food Safety Mitra Scheme.  
    • State Food Safety Apparatus: Implementation and enforcement of the FSS Act, 2006, primarily rests with state governments through Food Safety Commissioners, Designated Officers, and Food Safety Officers (FSOs).

What Measures are Needed to Strengthen Food Safety in India? 

  • Turnover-Linked Penalties: Current flat fines for food adulteration are effectively treated by large corporations as the "cost of doing business." Legislation must mandate that penalties be linked to the annual turnover of the offending Food Business Operator (FBO), creating real financial deterrence.  
    • Establish dedicated Food Safety Tribunals at the district level to ensure rapid disposal of cases, bypassing the overloaded mainstream judicial system.  
  • IoT-Enabled Cold Chains: Mandate the installation of IoT-based temperature and humidity sensors in all organized-sector cold storage and transport vehicles to prevent the "hidden" biological spoilage that occurs long before food hits the shelves.  
  • Micro-Entrepreneurship Training: Link FSSAI compliance directly to digital credit access (e.g., PM SVANidhi).  
    • Vendors who pass basic hygiene training should receive lower interest rates or digital-first financial benefits, creating a natural economic incentive for safety compliance.  
  • The "Farm to Fork" Integrated Dashboard: Establish an inter-ministerial digital ledger (involving FSSAI and Ministry of Agriculture) that tracks the use of pesticides and antibiotics at the farm level.  
    • This allows for the pre-emptive rejection of contaminated raw produce before it enters the food manufacturing pipeline. 
  • Ban on "Health-Washing": The Supreme Court of India directed the FSSAI to mandate clear Front-of-Pack (FOP) warning labels on pre-packaged foods.  
    • Labels must move from "nutritional fine print" to high-visibility, color-coded red/amber/green warnings to prevent the deceptive marketing of hyper-processed foods as "healthy."  
  • Citizen-Science Vigilance: Develop a unified, mobile-first "Food Watch" app that allows consumers to geo-tag and report violations in real-time. This should be linked to an automated dashboard that triggers mandatory inspection audits for locations receiving multiple complaints.  

Conclusion 

To combat the rising threat of food poisoning, India urgently needs a "Farm to Fork" framework that bridges institutional gaps, enhances testing infrastructure, and enforces strict, turnover-linked penalties. Crucially, a robust food safety ecosystem is not just about compliance; it is fundamental to achieving the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), specifically ending hunger (SDG 2), ensuring good health (SDG 3), and fostering responsible consumption (SDG 12).

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006?
TheFood Safety and Standards Act, 2006 is India's umbrella food law that consolidates various food-related legislations and establishes a science-based framework for regulating food safety through FSSAI. 

2. What is the role of the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI)?
FSSAI, established under theFood Safety and Standards Act, 2006 and functioning under the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, regulates food standards, licensing, food testing, risk assessment, and consumer awareness initiatives such as Eat Right India. 

3. What is the State Food Safety Index?
TheState Food Safety Index is an annual assessment by FSSAI that ranks States and Union Territories based on regulatory compliance, food testing, surveillance, human resources, training, and consumer empowerment. 

4. What are the major challenges affecting food safety in India?
Key challenges includefood adulteration, manpower shortages, inadequate inspections, heavy metal and pesticide contamination, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), misleading food claims, and weak cold-chain infrastructure. 

5. What measures have been suggested to strengthen food safety in India?
Major recommendations includeturnover-linked penalties, risk-based inspections, IoT-enabled cold chains, Farm-to-Fork digital traceability, Front-of-Pack warning labels, stronger laboratory infrastructure, and citizen-based food safety monitoring.
