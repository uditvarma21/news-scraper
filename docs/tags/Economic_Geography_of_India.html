India’s Cotton Sector

21-07-2026

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

Cotton is one of India’s most important commercial crops and occupies a central place in the country’s agricultural and industrial economy. 

  • Its significance extends beyond farming, as it supports the textile value chain, rural livelihoods, employment, trade and exports, making the cotton sector an important part of India’s development story.  

How Does Cotton Contribute to India’s Growth and Development? 

  • Economic and Employment Significance: Cotton is one of India’s most important commercial crops, linking agriculture, industry, trade and exports. It accounts for around 23% of global fibre production, supports nearly 6 million farmers and provides employment to 40–50 million people in processing, textiles, transport and trade. 
  • Exports and White Gold: Exports of cotton fibre, yarn, fabrics and garments contribute significantly to India’s foreign exchange earnings. Owing to its economic importance, cotton is popularly known as “White Gold”. 
  • Sectoral Strength: India’s cotton sector is supported by rich biological diversity, cultivation of all four recognised cotton species, extensive area under cultivation and a well-developed textile and value-chain ecosystem. 
  • Government Support and Future Growth: MSP operations, the Mission for Cotton Productivity and Kasturi Cotton Bharat, along with technology adoption, market reforms and quality improvement measures, are enhancing productivity, competitiveness and long-term growth. 

From Swadeshi to Atmanirbhar Bharat 

  • Ancient Legacy and Global Trade: India has been associated with cotton cultivation and textiles for thousands of years. Ancient India was a global centre for cotton textiles, exporting fabrics to Eastern and European markets, while cotton cloth also served as a medium of exchange. 
  • Rise of the Modern Industry: The modern cotton textile industry emerged in the 19th century with mills in Kolkata, Mumbai and Ahmedabad, which later became known as the “Manchester of India”. 
  • Swadeshi and Wartime Expansion: The industry expanded rapidly during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The Swadeshi Movement promoted indigenous mills and reduced dependence on British goods, while global demand during the World Wars accelerated growth. 
  • Growth before Independence: By the mid-20th century, the number of textile mills and cotton cloth production had increased significantly, reflecting the scale, resilience and self-reliance of India’s cotton industry and laying the foundation for Atmanirbhar Bharat. 

What Are the Major Dimensions of India’s Cotton Sector? 

  • Four Cotton Species: India is the only country cultivating all four recognised cotton species—G. arboreum and G. herbaceum (Asian cotton), G. barbadense (Egyptian cotton) and G. hirsutum (American upland cotton). G. hirsutum accounts for nearly 90% of India’s hybrid cotton production, and all Bt cotton hybrids currently cultivated in the country belong to this species. 

Bt Cotton 

  • Bt cotton is a genetically modified crop containing genes from Bacillus thuringiensis, a naturally occurring soil bacterium. 
  • These genes provide resistance against bollworms, a major cotton pest. 
  • Commercially introduced in India in 2002, Bt cotton reduced bollworm infestation and insecticide use, while higher yields and lower pest-control costs helped improve farm incomes.
  • Staple-Based Classification: Cotton is classified according to staple length, which determines fibre quality and its end use. India produces the complete range of staple categories.

  • Major Cotton-Growing Zones: Cotton production is concentrated in nine major states across three agro-ecological zones—the Northern Zone comprising Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan; the Central Zone comprising Gujarat, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh; and the Southern Zone comprising Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka. Cotton is also cultivated in Odisha and Tamil Nadu. 

Area, Production and Consumption 

  • Largest Cultivation Area: India ranks first globally in cotton cultivation area, with 114.84 lakh hectares under the crop. This represents around 38% of the global cotton area of 304.08 lakh hectares. 
  • Rain-fed Cultivation: Around 62% of India’s cotton area is rain-fed, while the remaining 38% is cultivated under irrigated conditions, making production significantly dependent on rainfall. 
  • Production and Global Position: India ranks second globally in cotton production and consumption. Cotton production is measured in bales, with one bale equivalent to 170 kg. Production stood at 297.24 lakh bales, or 5.06 million tonnes, in 2024–25, accounting for nearly 20% of global cotton output. 
  • Provisional Production and Consumption: In 2025–26, provisional cotton production stood at 290.91 lakh bales, or 4.95 million tonnes. Domestic consumption across MSME, non-MSME and non-textile segments reached 328 lakh bales, or 5.58 million tonnes, reflecting the strength of India’s textile industry. 

Cotton Exports and Trade Footprint 

  • Export Volume: India exported an estimated 18 lakh bales, equivalent to 0.31 million metric tonnes, in 2024–25. This accounted for around 3.37% of total global cotton exports of 541.69 lakh bales, or 9.21 million metric tonnes. 
  • Export Value and Major Markets: India’s cotton exports were valued at US$ 11.49 billion in FY25. The United States was the largest destination for Indian cotton fabrics and made-ups, accounting for 26.35% of exports, followed by Bangladesh at 19.81%, Sri Lanka at 5.11%, the United Kingdom at 2.38% and the United Arab Emirates at 2.32%. 

How Is India Building a Competitive Cotton Ecosystem? 

  • Minimum Support Price (MSP) System: The MSP system safeguards cotton farmers by ensuring remunerative prices when market prices fall below the announced support level. 
    • Before every cotton season (October–September), the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommends the MSP for Medium Staple and Long Staple seed cotton (kapas), ensuring at least 50% returns over the cost of production. 
  • Cotton Corporation of India (CCI): The Cotton Corporation of India (CCI) is the nodal agency responsible for procuring cotton under MSP whenever market prices fall below the support price. 
    • Expanded Procurement Network: Procurement centres increased from 508 (2024–25) to 571 (2025–26), covering 152 districts across 11 cotton-growing states.  
  • Mission for Cotton Productivity: The five-year Mission for Cotton Productivity, launched in 2025-26 with a budget of Rs. 5,659.22 crore, aims to enhance cotton production through research and innovation, focusing on climate-resilient and high-yielding varieties, particularly Extra Long Staple (ELS) cotton. It targets an increase in cotton production from 297 lakh bales to 498 lakh bales by 2031, benefiting around 32 lakh farmers across 140 districts in 14 states. 
    • Aligned with the ‘5F’ vision of farm, fibre, factory, fashion and foreign, the initiative seeks to enhance farmer incomes and ensure a steady supply of quality cotton.  
  • Special Project on Cotton under NFSM: Implemented under the National Food Security Mission (NFSM) since 2023–24 to improve productivity through field demonstrations of location-specific technologies in 8 major cotton-growing states. 
    • The pilot interventions focus on three key technologies: the High-Density Planting System, the Closer Spacing Planting System, and production technology for Extra-Long Staple (ELS) cotton, with the aim of improving productivity, fibre quality, and farmers' incomes. 
    • The 2023–24 fiscal year saw a marked increase in cotton productivity across 9,175 hectares, a success that drove further expansion in 2024–25. Given the positive impact on crop yields and farmer earnings, the project has been extended to 2025–26 to sustain these gains. 

  • Kapas Kisan App: A digital platform designed to make MSP procurement transparent, paperless and farmer-friendly. 
    • Key Features: Enables self-registration, advance slot booking, Aadhaar-linked payments and real-time SMS updates, significantly reducing waiting time at procurement centres. 
    • Impact: With over 41 lakh registered farmers, the app has improved transparency, efficiency and ease of cotton procurement. 
  • Kasturi Cotton Bharat: A flagship branding initiative to position Indian cotton globally through traceability, certification and branding. 
    • Implementation: Executed jointly by the Ministry of Textiles, Cotton Corporation of India (CCI) and TEXPROCIL, with a total outlay of ₹30 crore shared equally between the government and industry.  
    • Supporting Initiatives: The programme promotes Kasturi Villages, awareness workshops, sustainability practices, branding and a dedicated digital platform, positioning Indian cotton as a global symbol of quality, purity and sustainability.

Conclusion 

India’s cotton story reflects continuity, resilience and transformation. From ancient cotton trade and the Swadeshi movement to modern biotechnology, digital procurement and global branding, cotton remains deeply woven into India’s agricultural and industrial economy. With stronger productivity, quality, traceability and value addition, India’s “White Gold” can support farmer incomes, textile competitiveness, exports and the vision of Atmanirbhar Bharat.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. Why is cotton called White Gold?
Cotton is called White Gold because of its high economic value, wide industrial use and importance for farmers, textiles, exports and livelihoods.

2. Which cotton species are cultivated in India?
India cultivates all four recognised cotton species:G. Arboreum, G. Herbaceum, G. Barbadense and G. Hirsutum. 

3. What is Bt cotton?
Bt cotton is genetically modified cotton containing genes fromBacillus thuringiensis, which provides resistance against bollworms. 

4. What is Kasturi Cotton Bharat?
Kasturi Cotton Bharat is a branding, certification and traceability initiative to position Indian cotton as a high-quality and sustainable global product.

5. What is the Mission for Cotton Productivity?
It is a five-year mission launched in 2025-26 to improve cotton productivity, quality and farmer incomes through research, innovation and field-level extension.
