Sengol in New NCERT Textbook

03-10-2026

Source: TH 

Why in News?  

The ‘Sengol’, which was installed in the new Parliament building in 2023, has been featured in the newly released NCERT class 9 textbook, titled Understanding Society and Beyond – Part 2. The textbook links it to the Chola tradition of righteous governance and the transfer of power in 1947.  

What is the Historical and Cultural Significance of the Sengol? 

  • Symbol of Righteousness: The word 'Sengol' is derived from the Tamil word 'semmai', which means righteousness. It fundamentally represents just and fair governance by a ruler, standing in direct contrast to 'Kodungol', which signifies authoritarianism or unjust rule. 
    • In ancient Tamil tradition, the Sengol was viewed primarily as a profound virtue or concept of equity rather than just a decorated rod. It served as a constant reminder to the king that he held the “aanai” (decree) to govern fairly and uphold his duties to the people. 
  • Prominence in Classical Literature: 
    • Silappadikaram ( was written by the poet and prince Ilango Adigal): The first Tamil epic highlights its importance when Chera King Cheran Senguttuvan praises a Pandiyan king for sacrificing his own life to restore the "uprightness of the Sengol" after mistakenly executing an innocent man. 
    • Tirukkural (authored by Thiruvalluvar): Dedicates an entire chapter to 'Sengonmai' (The Right Sceptre), emphasising that a ruler’s equity, not his weapons, brings true victory. 
    • Sangam Era Texts: The practice of using a sceptre to denote righteous rule finds mention in 2,000-year-old texts like Purananooru, Kurunthogai, and Tholkappiyam. 
  • Integral to Coronation Rites: Historically associated with the Chola dynasty and Nayaka kings, the Sengol was handed to a new monarch by high priests during coronation ceremonies.  
    • It was recognized as a sacred symbol marking the transfer of power and the bestowment of ethical responsibility. 
  • A Core Pillar of the Kingdom: Ancient Tamil treatises classify the Sengol as one of the 10 essential constituents of a kingdom, standing alongside other symbols of sovereignty like the venkottra kudai (white umbrella) and murasu (drum). 

Sengol Connection to India’s Independence in 1947 

  • Freedom fighter C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) suggested to Jawaharlal Nehru that the Sengol be used as a ceremonial symbol to mark the transfer of power from British rule to independent India. 
  • Rajaji approached the Thiruvavaduthurai Adheenam, a 14th-century Saivaite Math in Tamil Nadu, which commissioned a gold-plated silver Sengol topped with a Nandi. 
  • On 14th August 1947, the Sengol was brought to Delhi, first presented to Lord Mountbatten, sanctified with holy water, and then ceremonially handed over to Jawaharlal Nehru. 
    • The ceremony symbolised the transfer of power from British rule to independent India, along with the ethical responsibility associated with authority. 
  • The Sengol, installed in the new Parliament building in 2023, is topped with a Nandi, which symbolises steadfast justice, firmness, and the vigilant observance of truth (Nyaya). 

Other Ancient Governance Concepts Highlighted in the New NCERT Textbook 

  • Institutional Mottos: The textbook links India’s national motto, Satyameva Jayate, to the Mundaka Upanishad, noting that it replaced the British-era motto “Heaven’s Light, Our Guide.”  
    • Similarly, the Supreme Court’s motto, Yato Dharmastato Jayah (“Where there is righteous duty, there is victory”), is linked to the Mahabharata.  
  • Ram Rajya: The text cites Mahatma Gandhi’s reference to Ram Rajya (drawing from the Ramayana and the 16th-century Ramcharitmanas) as an ideal governance structure based on probity, transparency, compassion, and public welfare. 
  • Governance as a 'Sacred Responsibility': It asserts that from the Vedic period through the eras of the Ramayana and Mahabharata, Indian traditions viewed governance as a sacred duty to the people, rather than merely a mechanism for exercising power. 
  • Divine Validation of Resistance: In the chapter titled 'Resistance and Resilience,' the text highlights traditional beliefs where Indian kings received swords from Goddesses (such as Kali, Durga, and Bhavani) to resist foreign invasions. 
    • Examples cited include Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj receiving a sword from Goddess Tulja Bhavani and Kumara Kampana using a goddess-given sword against Sultanate forces. 

Frequently Asked Questions 

1. What is Sengol?
The Sengol is a ceremonial sceptre associated with righteous governance, derived from Tamil traditions and linked to the symbolic transfer of power in 1947. 

2. What does the Sengol symbolise?
It symbolises just and ethical rule, where political authority is accompanied by a responsibility to govern fairly. 

3. What is the significance of Satyameva Jayate?
Satyameva Jayate, meaning “Truth alone triumphs,” comes from the Mundaka Upanishad and is India’s national motto. 

4. What is the meaning and source of Yato Dharmastato Jayah?
Yato Dharmastato Jayah means “Where there is righteous duty, there is victory” and is associated with the Mahabharata. 

5. What is Ram Rajya in the context of governance?
Ram Rajya is presented as an ideal of good governance, associated with probity, transparency, compassion and public welfare.
