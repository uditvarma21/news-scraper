Sheshnag Lake

26-11-2026

Source: IE 

Recently, concerns have been raised over the potential ecological impact of track-widening work near Sheshnag Lake along the Amarnath Yatra route, given the region’s fragile Himalayan ecosystem. 

  • The Border Roads Organisation (BRO) maintains that the work is confined to widening and stabilising existing pilgrimage tracks to ensure pilgrim safety. 
  • In 2012, the Supreme Court directed the widening of pilgrimage tracks to a minimum of 12 feet, subject to environmental safeguards, without authorising their conversion into motorable roads. 

Sheshnag Lake 

  • Location: It is a high-altitude glacial lake in the Anantnag district of Jammu and Kashmir, situated at an altitude of 3,590 metres, approximately 23 km by trek from Pahalgam via Chandanwari. 
    • It lies along the Pahalgam–Chandanwari–Amarnath Cave route, one of the two traditional pilgrimage routes, the other being the Baltal route via Sonamarg.  
  • Physical Features: The lake is approximately 1 km long and 0.7 km wide and is surrounded by snow-covered mountains, alpine meadows, steep slopes and loose moraine deposits.  
  • Lake Type: It is an oligotrophic freshwater lake, characterised by low nutrient concentrations, low biological productivity and generally high water transparency. 
  • Hydrology: It is primarily fed by glacial meltwater, snowmelt and surrounding mountain streams. 
    • It feeds the East Lidder River, which joins the West Lidder near Pahalgam to form the Lidder River, a tributary of the Jhelum River within the Indus River system. 
  • Significance: According to Hindu tradition, Lord Shiva left his serpents at Sheshnag while proceeding towards the Amarnath cave. 
    • The lake is mentioned in Kalhana’s Rajatarangini, a 12th-century historical chronicle of Kashmir, as an abode of a Naga deity. 
  • Ecological Significance: Sheshnag forms part of a fragile Himalayan glacial ecosystem vulnerable to climate change, excessive tourism and infrastructure development. 
    • It has been identified as a high-risk glacial lake for Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs). 
    • Excavation and debris dumping may aggravate slope instability, soil erosion, sedimentation and disruption of natural drainage. 
    • Other high-altitude glacial lakes in the region include Sonsar, Harnag, Konnag and Harbhagwan.

Kerala to Revive Traditional Surangam Water-Harvesting System

16-11-2026

Source: TH 

Eight grama panchayats in Kerala's Kasaragod district will undertake a project to restore traditional underground water-harvesting tunnels known as Surangams, aiming to improve groundwater recharge, conserve rainwater, reduce summer water shortages and sustain river flows amid declining rainfall and increasing drought risks.  

  • Implementation: The project will utilize workers under the Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) (VB-G RAM G), which replaced MGNREGA on 1st July 2026.   
  • Technical Support: The Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) will provide scientific guidance on tunnel restoration, groundwater recharge and sustainable water management. 

Surangam Water-Harvesting System 

  • Traditional Technique: Surangams are horizontal tunnels excavated into laterite hills to collect groundwater through natural seepage and gravity-driven flow. 
  • Geographical Distribution: Primarily associated with Kasaragod district of Kerala and parts of Dakshina Kannada district in Karnataka, particularly in laterite-rich, undulating terrain. 
  • Local Names: Surangams are also known as Thurangam, Thorapu and Mala in different localities. The term Surangam is derived from the Kannada word for tunnel. 
  • Physical Dimensions: A typical Surangam is approximately 0.45–0.70 metres wide, 1.8–2 metres high and 3–300 metres long. Longer tunnels may include vertical ventilation shafts. 
  • Water Collection: Groundwater emerging from the tunnels is generally collected in an open pit, pond or storage structure, sometimes called a Mathakkam, before being used for drinking water, domestic purposes or irrigation.  
  • Significance: Provides a low-energy, gravity-based water supply system that supports local water security and demonstrates the value of indigenous water-management knowledge.
