Cyclone Arnab

29-10-2026

Source: IE 

Why in News?  

Cyclonic Storm Arnab, originating in the Bay of Bengal (BoB), made landfall along the Andhra Pradesh–Yanam coast, causing fatalities, mass evacuations and extensive agricultural and infrastructure damage before subsequently weakening.

Summary

  • Cyclone Arnab highlights India's vulnerability to tropical cyclones, causing coastal damage, agricultural losses and displacement, underscoring the need for effective early warning and disaster response systems. 
  • Climate change and ocean warming are altering cyclone risks in the North Indian Ocean, increasing the importance of coastal resilience, impact-based forecasting and disaster preparedness. 

What are the Key Facts About Cyclone Arnab? 

  • Meteorological Classification: Cyclone Arnab classified by the IMD as a "Very Severe Cyclonic Storm" (VSCS).  
    • According to the India Meteorological Department (IMD) classification, a VSCS is defined by maximum sustained surface wind speeds ranging between 118–166 km/h (64–89 knots). Arnab struck with sustained speeds of 125–135 km/h. 
  • Genesis: Originated as a low-pressure area over the Bay of Bengal, capitalizing on the high Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential (TCHP), before moving west-northwestwards to make landfall near Yanam (Andhra Pradesh coast). 
  • Institutional Nomenclature: "Arnab" was proposed by Bangladesh, meaning "ocean" or "sea" in Bengali. Arnab was the first named cyclone of 2026 in the North Indian Ocean basin. 
  • Climatological Anomalies & El Niño Impact: The cyclone underwent Rapid Intensification (RI) due to high Sea Surface Temperatures (>28°C) and a thick freshwater "Barrier Layer" in the Bay of Bengal. 
    • Prevailing El Niño conditions altered standard atmospheric circulation, shifting the cyclone's trajectory toward the lower latitudes (Andhra Pradesh) rather than the traditional northern hook toward Odisha/West Bengal, and fueling its sustained intensity post-landfall. 
    • Its prolonged landfall was influenced by interactions among an anticyclonic circulation over the China–Myanmar region, strong westerly winds and a low-pressure system over the southern Bay of Bengal, which slowed its movement and prolonged exposure to heavy rainfall and strong winds. 
  • Socio-Economic and Ecological Impact: 
    • Agrarian Devastation: Over 80,000 hectares of standing crops (paddy, horticulture) were destroyed. 
    • Saltwater Intrusion: The 1 to 1.5-meter storm surge pushed saline seawater deep into the Krishna-Godavari delta, altering soil chemistry and threatening medium-term coastal agricultural viability. 
    • Blue Economy Disruption: Destruction of localized aquaculture and fishing trawlers severely impacted the livelihoods of coastal artisanal fisherfolk. 
  • Disaster Response Framework: 
    • Impact-Based Forecasting (IBF): The IMD utilized Doppler Weather Radars and the INSAT satellite series to issue color-coded, impact-based early warnings, significantly reducing human casualties. 
    • Statutory Action: Executed under the Disaster Management Act, 2005. The National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) and State Executive Committees coordinated mass preemptive evacuations of over 1.8 lakh people to multi-purpose cyclone shelters. 
    • Institutional Coordination: The National Crisis Management Committee (NCMC), chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, coordinates preparedness and response measures during major disasters, working with central ministries, state governments and disaster-response agencies. 
    • Financial Redressal: The State requested immediate mobilization of the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) (managed by the Central Government) to augment the State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) for recovery operations. 

Naming Mechanism for North Indian Ocean Cyclones 

  • Institutional Mandate: The naming of tropical cyclones in the North Indian Ocean (NIO) encompassing the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea is managed by the World Meteorological Organization/United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific (WMO/ESCAP) Panel on Tropical Cyclones (PTC). 
  • Naming Framework and Member Nations: The formal naming protocol for the North Indian Ocean commenced in September 2004 with eight original member nations.  
    • As cyclonic threats evolved and regional cooperation deepened, the WMO/ESCAP PTC expanded the panel during its 45th Session in 2018. 
    • The panel currently comprises Bangladesh, India, Iran, Maldives, Myanmar, Oman, Pakistan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Yemen.  
  • Master List: In April 2020, the panel adopted a comprehensive new list of 169 names (13 names proposed by each of the 13 countries)  
  • No Repetition Policy: Unlike some other global ocean basins, names of tropical cyclones over the North Indian Ocean are never repeated; once a name is used, it is permanently retired.  
  • Purpose of Naming: It helps the scientific community, disaster managers, and the public to easily identify individual cyclones, raise rapid awareness, remove confusion during simultaneous storms, and effectively disseminate warnings. 
  • Strict Naming Criteria: Proposed names must be politically, religiously, culturally, and gender-neutral. They must have a maximum length of eight letters, be easy to pronounce, and not hurt the sentiments of any global community. 
  • Wind Speed Threshold: A storm is officially named by RSMC New Delhi only when its diagnosed maximum sustained surface wind speed reaches 34 knots (62 kmph) or more. 
  • Trans-Regional Exception: If a tropical cyclone originates in the South China Sea, crosses Thailand, and emerges into the Bay of Bengal, it will not be renamed and will retain its original name.

Why is the Bay of Bengal More Prone to Cyclones than the Arabian Sea? 

  • Higher Cyclone Frequency and Seasonality: Historically, the Bay of Bengal experiences approximately four times as many cyclones as the Arabian Sea, with the North Indian Ocean recording around five cyclones annually on average. 
    • Cyclone activity peaks during the pre-monsoon (April–June) and post-monsoon (October–December) seasons, particularly around May and November. 
  • Salinity Stratification and the ‘Barrier Layer’: Freshwater from rivers like the Ganga, Brahmaputra and Godavari creates a low-salinity surface layer in the Bay of Bengal, preventing cooler deep water from rising.  
    • This maintains sea surface temperatures above 26.5°C, a key condition for tropical cyclone formation.  
  • Typhoon Remnants (Easterly Waves): The Bay of Bengal lies downwind of the highly active Northwest Pacific basin. Remnants of Pacific typhoons can cross the Indochinese Peninsula and re-intensify over the Bay’s warm waters under favourable atmospheric and oceanic conditions, sometimes developing into new cyclonic systems. 
  • Absence of Dry Air Intrusions: The Arabian Sea is exposed to dry air from the arid Middle East and the Horn of Africa, which can suppress deep convection and cyclone formation.  
    • In contrast, the Bay of Bengal receives abundant moisture from its humid surroundings, favouring convection and providing the moisture needed for cyclone development and intensification.  
  • Favorable Wind Shear and Heat Potential For a cyclone to grow, the wind speed at the bottom and top of the storm must be similar (low vertical wind shear), otherwise, the storm gets torn apart.  
    • The Bay provides this calm environment, along with a deep thermal reservoir of warm water (High Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential) that allows storms to rapidly intensify.  
  • Funnelling Coastline and Storm Surge: The funnel-shaped northern Bay of Bengal and its shallow continental shelf can amplify storm surges, increasing coastal flooding and inundation risks, particularly along the Odisha–West Bengal–Bangladesh coast. 
    • This geographical feature primarily intensifies cyclone impacts, rather than directly increasing cyclone formation.

Arabian Sea Anomaly 

  • The Arabian Sea anomaly primarily refers to the rapid warming of sea surface temperatures (SSTs) and the dramatic shift in weather and cyclone patterns in the Arabian Sea. 
  • Rapid Warming of the Arabian Sea: A study by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM), Pune, reveals that the Arabian Sea is currently warming at a significantly faster rate than the Bay of Bengal.  
    • This accelerated rise in Sea Surface Temperature (SST) is primarily driven by anthropogenic global warming and increased oceanic heat absorption.  
    • Furthermore, the localized accumulation of aerosols like Black Carbon (the Asian Brown Cloud) alters regional atmospheric thermodynamics. Together, these climate anomalies have provided a massive continuous thermal reservoir, resulting in a 52% increase in the frequency of Arabian Sea cyclones in recent decades.  
  • Positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD): Increasing frequencies of strong Positive IOD events have concentrated exceptionally warm water in the western Indian Ocean/Arabian Sea, triggering severe pre-monsoon cyclogenesis (e.g., Cyclones Tauktae and Biparjoy). 
  • Increased Lifespan: Cyclones in the Arabian Sea are now lasting 80% longer than they did two decades ago, increasing their destructive potential along India's West Coast.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What was Cyclone Arnab, and where did it make landfall?
Cyclonic Storm Arnab originated in the Bay of Bengal and made landfall along the Andhra Pradesh–Yanam coast, causing fatalities, evacuations and agricultural and infrastructure damage. 

2. What factors contribute to cyclone formation in the Bay of Bengal?
High sea surface temperatures, low-salinity surface waters, abundant moisture and favourable atmospheric conditions support tropical cyclone formation and intensification. 

3. What is the role of the IMD in managing cyclones such as Arnab?
The India Meteorological Department (IMD) monitors cyclones and issues forecasts and early warnings using satellites, Doppler weather radars and forecasting models. 

4. How do the SDRF and NDRF support cyclone recovery?
The State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) provides primary state-level disaster response funding, while the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) supplements resources for disasters of severe nature, subject to prescribed norms. 

5. How is climate change affecting cyclones in the Arabian Sea?
Rising ocean temperatures can increase the potential for cyclone intensification and heavy rainfall, although cyclone frequency and tracks also depend on atmospheric circulation and wind shear.

IMD Launches Interactive Cyclone Warning and Marine Weather Platform

16-10-2026

Source: DD  

The India Meteorological Department (IMD) launched an interactive Cyclone Warning and Marine Weather Services Decision Support System(DSS) to strengthen cyclone preparedness and support timely decision-making. 

  • About DSS: It is a WebGIS-based platform providing interactive maps and visualisations of cyclone-related hazards, including cyclone tracks, intensity forecasts, wind distribution, rainfall warnings, storm surge information and rainfall forecasts for river catchments.  
  • Background: The launch coincided with the anniversaries of the landfalls of Very Severe Cyclonic Storms Phailin (2013) and Hudhud (2014), whose successful forecasting marked important milestones in India's cyclone early-warning capabilities.  
  • Objective: It provides location-specific hazard information to disaster management authorities, government agencies and the public to improve preparedness and reduce losses. 
  • Accessibility: The platform is accessible through IMD and Regional Specialised Meteorological Centre((RSMC) websites and IMD’s Decision Support System. 
  • Institutional Coordination: IMD's biannual Pre-Cyclone Exercise Meeting, conducted since 2008, brings together disaster management authorities, defence forces, coastal states, fisheries departments and scientific organisations to review cyclone preparedness.  
  • Significance: The platform enables location-specific early warnings, supports evacuation planning and disaster risk reduction, and improves coordination among forecasting agencies, disaster management authorities and other stakeholders.

Cyclone Shakhti

07-10-2025

Source: IE 

Cyclone Shakhti, a name given by Sri Lanka, which originated in the Arabian Sea, has strengthened into a severe cyclonic storm (SCS) with wind speeds reaching 100 kmph. 

Cyclones 

  • About: Cyclones are fast air circulations around low-pressure areas, rotating anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern, often causing storms and severe weather. 
  • Cyclone Formation in Arabian Sea: The Arabian Sea, particularly its central and southern parts, serves as a warm pool with ideal Sea Surface Temperature (SST) (at least 27°C) favourable for cyclone formation. 
    • Also, the Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO) is a large weather system that moves eastward around the tropics.  
      • When its active phase is over the Arabian Sea, it creates favorable conditions for cyclones — including more moisture, low wind shear, and strong upward air movement. 
  • Classification of Cyclones: IMD classifies low-pressure systems in the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea by damage potential, following World Meteorological Organization (WMO) guidelines.

  • Naming of Cyclones: The WMO/ESCAP (United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific) Panel on Tropical Cyclones, which includes 13 North Indian Ocean countries, manages cyclone naming for the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. 
    • The panel has a pre-set list of names submitted by 13 countries to be used sequentially. 
    • Names are chosen in order, column by column, regardless of where the cyclone forms.
