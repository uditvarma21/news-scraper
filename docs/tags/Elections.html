Supreme Court to Review Anonymous Political Donations

27-11-2025

For Prelims: Supreme Court, Income Tax Act, 1961, Right to Information, Election Commission of India (ECI), Election Symbol Order, 1968, Representation of People Act (RPA), 1951, Electoral Trust, Electoral Bond Scheme.  

For Mains: Supreme Court review of political funding, regulatory framework for political funding, need of transparency in political funding and further reforms needed. 

Source: TH 

Why in News? 

The Supreme Court has decided to review a petition challenging the rule that allows political parties to receive anonymous cash donations below Rs 2000, which petitioners say creates a loophole for opaque, untraceable political funding. 

What Key Concern has been Highlighted in the Petition? 

  • Total Ban on Cash Donations: It seeks to end cash donations even up to Rs 2000 allowed under Section 13A(d) of the Income Tax Act, 1961, which enables anonymous contributions, and demands full disclosure of donor details.  
  • Violation of Fundamental Rights: The petition argues that Section 13A(d) violates Article 19(1)(a) by denying citizens their right to information, asserting that voters must know the sources of political funding to make informed choices. 
  • Directions Sought on Form 24A: The petition seeks a mandamus directing the Election Commission of India (ECI) to scrutinise Form 24A contribution reports and require political parties to deposit contributions lacking address or PAN details.  
    • It also urges the ECI to suspend or withdraw party symbols under Paragraph 16A of the Election Symbol Order, 1968 by issuing notices to defaulting parties. 
  • Further Proposed Reforms: It urges that political parties’ accounts be audited by independent auditors appointed by the ECI, and calls for stronger mechanisms to ensure timely submission of contribution and audit reports. 

What Rules Govern Political Donations in India? 

  • Representation of People Act (RPA), 1951: Section 29B of the RPA, 1951 permits political parties to receive voluntary contributions from individuals and companies (excluding government companies and foreign sources). 
  • Companies Act, 2013: Any company—except a government company or one that is less than three years old—can donate any amount, directly or indirectly, to a political party. However, it is capped at a maximum of 7.5% of their average annual net profit over 3 years. 
  • Income Tax Act, 1961: Indian companies and individuals can claim tax deductions on donations made to political parties or electoral trusts under Sections 80GGB and 80GGC. 
  • Foreign Contributions (Regulations) Act, 2010 (FCRA): Generally, foreign donations are prohibited. However, the definition of a foreign source was amended to exclude Indian companies with foreign investment (an Indian company with foreign shareholding beyond 50% is not treated as a foreign source). This allows companies (including foreign subsidiaries) to donate, provided they comply with FEMA sectoral caps. 
  • Electoral Trusts Scheme, 2013: An Electoral Trust is set up by companies to collect donations from individuals and firms and distribute them to political parties.  
    • It can retain up to 5% of its funds (for administrative expenses), must distribute 95% to eligible parties (registered under section 29A of the RPA, 1951), and cannot accept cash donations. 

Why is Transparency in Political Funding Necessary? 

  • Informed Political Choice: Citizens have a fundamental Right to Information on who funds political parties, implicitly protected under Article 19(1)(a), and affirmed in Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002) that helps voters make informed choices. 
    • The Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024) struck down the Electoral Bond Scheme for violating voters’ Right to Information under Article 19(1)(a). 
  • Supports Institutional Probity: Transparent funding helps dismantle the cycle of quid pro quo and strengthens institutional probity. By reducing opacity, it curbs routine policy distortions in areas such as resource allocation, taxation, and regulation.  
  • Safeguard for National Sovereignty: Anonymous funding channels enable foreign interference, allowing hostile state or non-state actors to influence foreign policy, defence procurement, or internal security, threatening India’s strategic autonomy. 
  • Preventing Market Distortions: Hidden corporate donations fuel crony capitalism, where political connections override market efficiency.  
    • This distorts resource allocation, suppresses innovation, hurts honest businesses, and undermines sustainable economic growth. 
  • Upholds "Egalitarian Democracy": Without transparency, democracy risks becoming a plutocracy, where the wealthy buy political access and influence, undermining the egalitarian principles of the Constitution’s Preamble. 
  • Meeting Global Standards: Many countries mandate transparent political funding, such as the United States, which has required disclosures since 1910.  

What Reforms Required for Transparent Political Funding in India? 

  • Complete Ban of Anonymous Cash Donations: A crucial reform is to amend Section 13A(d) of the Income Tax Act, 1961, by removing cash donations up to Rs 2000 or replacing it with a lower threshold as a viable solution.  
    • Mandating all political contributions through digital modes would ensure a clear audit trail, eliminate cash, and provide digital access and literacy to avoid voter disenfranchisement. 
    • The 170th Law Commission Report recommended removing Explanation 1 of Section 77 RPA, which allowed friends or parties to spend freely, ensuring all candidate-related expenses count toward the expenditure limit. 
      • Deleting it would make all expenses count. 
  • Strengthening Institutional Enforcement: Strengthen the ECI’s powers to de-register non-compliant parties, and appoint independent auditors, while ensuring RBI and SEBI enforce stricter oversight of corporate donations to prevent black money into political funding. 
    • Introducing a mechanism for whistleblower protection or addressing public grievances related to political donations could further strengthen enforcement. 
  • Real-Time Transparency: All political parties should be required to publicly upload donor details (PAN, address, amount) on a single ECI portal in real time. This portal should be integrated with the Income Tax Department to automatically verify donations and flag discrepancies for investigation. 
  • Systemic & Long-Term Reforms: Implement partial state funding for campaign expenses (e.g., public airtime, limited printing) to reduce dependence on private capital, as recommended by the Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998) and Law Commission (1999). Additionally, set a national election expenditure limit with strict monitoring to ensure fair spending. 
    • State funding reforms could be tied to further accountability measures, such as transparency in how these funds are spent by political parties 

Conclusion 

The Supreme Court’s review of political funding transparency highlights the need to eliminate anonymous cash donations, strengthen institutional oversight, and ensure real-time disclosure of contributions. Transparent funding safeguards democracy, curbs corruption, prevents policy capture, and aligns India with global best practices, enabling voters to make informed electoral choices.

Delimitation in India

12-08-2026

For Prelims: Delimitation, Census , Articles 81 and 82, Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023 ,  Delimitation Commission, Finance Commission, Rajya Sabha 

For Mains: Delimitation and Indian federalism, One Person, One Vote, One Value vs representation of States, Gerrymandering and electoral fairness

Source:TH 

Why in News?

The delimitation of Lok Sabha constituencies has once again become a major political issue as the Centre seeks to revive its proposed electoral reforms ahead of the 2029 Lok Sabha elections.  

  • The discussion is significant because the 2027 Census is expected to trigger fresh delimitation under Articles 81 and 82, potentially shifting Lok Sabha representation towards populous northern States at the expense of demographically stabilised southern States.

Summary

  • The 2027 Census could trigger fresh delimitation, potentially shifting Lok Sabha representation towards populous northern States and raising concerns over federal balance, women’s reservation and regional equity. 
  • A fair exercise requires balancing “One Person, One Vote, One Value” with federal interests through consensus, transparent boundary-making and reforms such as pro-rata seat augmentation and weighted representation.

What are the Challenges Associated with the Delimitation? 

  • Federal vs. Demographic Dilemma (The North-South Divide): The primary objective of delimitation is to uphold the democratic principle of "One Person, One Vote, One Value".  
    • However, strictly applying this principle based on current population data will severely penalize Southern states (like Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Andhra Pradesh). These states successfully implemented national family planning policies and stabilized their populations. 
    • Conversely, states in the Hindi heartland (like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan) with historically higher population growth rates stand to gain a massive influx of new Lok Sabha seats. 
    • This could shift political power towards the North, reducing the South’s influence over national policies, constitutional amendments and resource allocation.  
  • Linkage to Women's Reservation: The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023 provides 33% reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, linked to the next delimitation exercise. 
    • This creates a challenge as women’s reservation is widely supported, while population-based delimitation faces strong federal concerns, particularly from southern States. 
    • Linking the two could delay women’s reservation if consensus on delimitation remains elusive. 
  • Delays and Ambiguity in Census Data: According to Articles 81 and 82, delimitation is contingent upon the publication of the first Census taken after 2026. 
    • However, the decadal 2021 Census was delayed indefinitely due to the Covid-19 pandemic. 
    • Attempts by the Union Government (via the failed Delimitation Bill of 2026 alongside the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026) to bypass this delay by using the outdated 2011 Census for delimitation triggered intense political mistrust among opposition states.  
    • The delimitation process is historically time-consuming, with the previous Commission, constituted in 2002 after the 2001 Census, submitting recommendations that were implemented only in 2008 for the 2009 elections.  
    • However, the 2027 Census is fully digitalised, potentially allowing faster completion. If the exercise is not completed before the 2029 Lok Sabha elections, it could extend into the next government’s tenure, as seen during the previous delimitation cycle. 
  • Logistics of a Larger Parliament: The present elected strength is 543, while Article 81 permits up to 550 seats. The defeated 131st Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2026, proposed raising the constitutional ceiling to 850 seats.  
    • A larger Lok Sabha could accommodate population growth but may also create significant logistical, administrative and deliberative challenges. 
    • A significantly larger Lok Sabha means less time for individual Members of Parliament (MPs) to speak, debate bills, or raise matters of public importance. Maintaining order and managing disruptions in a much larger legislative body will be a formidable task. 
  • Gerrymandering and Sub-Nationalism: Delimitation is not just about allocating seats between states, but also drawing the boundaries of constituencies within states.  
    • This opens the door to potential gerrymandering - manipulating electoral boundaries to favor a specific political party (through tactics like "cracking" and "packing" voter demographics). 
      • Cracking: Spreading an opposing party’s supporters across constituencies so they remain a minority in each, reducing their chances of winning seats. 
      • Packing: Concentrating opposing supporters in a few constituencies, where excess votes are wasted, allowing the rival party to win more seats elsewhere. 
    • Because delimitation fundamentally alters political destinies, any perceived unfairness can inflame sub-nationalism and regional grievances, leading to deep political ramifications and protests.

What is Delimitation?

  • About: Delimitation is the process of fixing or redrawing the boundaries of territorial constituencies for the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies to ensure that each seat represents a roughly equal number of voters.   
  • Objective: To operationalize the democratic principle of “one person, one vote, one value” by adjusting boundaries as population densities shift over time.   
    • Determines reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST), ensuring political representation of marginalised communities.  
  • Constitutional Mandate: Article 82 mandates Parliament to enact a Delimitation Act after each census, readjusting Lok Sabha seat allocation to States and dividing States into territorial constituencies.   
    • Article 170 provides for a similar readjustment of seats and constituencies in State Legislative Assemblies.   
  • Delimitation Commission: This ‘delimitation process’ is performed by the ‘Delimitation Commission’ that is set up under an act of Parliament. 
    • It is a high-powered, independent body  consisting of 3 members, i.e., a Chairperson (a serving or retired Supreme Court Judge), the Chief Election Commissioner (or an Election Commissioner nominated by them), and State Election Commissioners of the concerned states.   
    • Delimitation orders generally have statutory finality and protection from judicial interference. However, in the Kishorchandra Chhanganlal Rathod case (2024), the Supreme Court held that a Delimitation Commission order may be subject to limited judicial review if it is manifestly arbitrary or violates constitutional values.  
    • It has been set up four times till March 2026 i.e., 1952, 1963, 1973, and 2002 under respective Delimitation Acts. 
    • Article 329(a) states that the validity of any law relating to the delimitation of constituencies or the allotment of seats, made under Article 327, shall not be called in question in any court.   
  • Freeze on Delimitation: The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 froze the total number of seats in the Lok Sabha on the 1971 Census to ensure that states implementing population control measures (primarily in the South) were not penalized with reduced political representation.   
    • The 84th Amendment Act, 2001 extended the freeze on the total number of seats until the first census after 2026.   
    • While the 2002 Commission redrew internal boundaries within states (based on the 2001 Census), the inter-state allocation of seats remains based on 1971 data.

What Reforms can Ensure a Fair and Consensus-based Delimitation?

"Pro-Rata" Seat Augmentation (The Blanket Increase Model) 

  • A pro-rata augmentation model has been proposed to increase Lok Sabha seats while retaining existing State-wise ratios, allowing smaller constituencies without penalising Southern States for population stabilisation. 
  • The 50% Flat Increase: Instead of reallocating the national seat share based on the latest census, Parliament could increase the total number of Lok Sabha seats by a flat percentage (e.g., 50%, bringing the house from 543 to roughly 815 seats) while maintaining the existing 1971 state-wise ratios. 
  • Impact: Uttar Pradesh’s seats would increase (e.g., 80 to 120), as would Tamil Nadu’s (e.g., 39 to roughly 59). This expands total representation without altering the relative voting power or weightage of any single state in Parliament. 
  • Intrastate Redrawing: The Delimitation Commission would then use the latest census solely to redraw the internal boundaries within each state to equalize urban-rural disparities. 

Adopting a "Weighted Representation" Formula 

  • A Weighted Representation Formula  (similar to  Finance Commissions) could balance population with demographic performance, development, economic contribution and geographical area, rather than relying solely on population. 
  • Population Size (e.g., 50%): Fulfilling the democratic "One Person, One Vote" mandate. 
  • Demographic Performance / TFR (e.g., 25%): Rewarding states that stabilized their Total Fertility Rate (TFR) in alignment with national family planning goals. 
  • Economic & Developmental Contribution (e.g., 15%): Factoring in Human Development Index (HDI), GST contribution, and fiscal discipline. 
  • Geographic Area (e.g., 10%): Ensuring adequate representation for states with vast terrain but low population density. 
  • However, under Article 81, Lok Sabha representation is primarily population-based. Giving weightage to GST contribution, HDI, fiscal discipline or TFR would require a major constitutional amendment. 

Decoupling the Women's Reservation Act 

  • Parliament must pass a constitutional amendment to decouple the two issues. Rolling out women’s reservation on the existing 543-seat matrix ensures that a widely supported democratic reform is not held hostage by deeply contentious federal disputes over seat distribution. 

Bicameral Rebalancing: Empowering the Rajya Sabha 

  • Equal State Representation: India can emulate the United States Senate or the Australian Senate model, where every state regardless of its population size has an equal number of representatives in the Upper House. 
  • Veto Powers: The  Rajya Sabha (Council of States) has weaker powers over Money Bills. Strengthening its role in financial legislation can help it function as a genuine federal check against demographic majoritarianism originating in the Lok Sabha.  

Enhancing Institutional Transparency and Consensus 

  • A fair delimitation cannot be executed through executive fiat; it requires deep institutional consensus to avoid allegations of gerrymandering. 
  • Empowering Associate Members: Currently, the Delimitation Act allows 5 Lok Sabha MPs and 5 MLAs from each state to sit as "Associate Members" on the Commission, but they have no voting rights.  
    • Reforming the Act to give Associate Members binding input on internal boundary drawing would prevent arbitrary mapping (packing and cracking). 
  • Utilizing the Inter-State Council: Before finalizing the terms of reference for the new Delimitation Commission, the Union Government must convene the Inter-State Council (Article 263) to debate and agree upon the methodology with all Chief Ministers. 
  • Algorithmic and Open-Source Mapping: To eliminate political bias in redrawing local boundaries, the Commission should mandate the use of open-source Geographic Information System (GIS) algorithms to draw compact, contiguous districts, subject to extensive public hearings and civil society audits. 

Conclusion 

A fair delimitation process must recognize that "representation" in a diverse, federal union is not merely a mathematical exercise of counting heads. It must be a negotiated consensus that balances the democratic rights of individuals with the political and economic rights of the states.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is delimitation and what is its constitutional basis?
 Delimitation is the redrawing of electoral constituencies to ensure relatively equal representation. Articles 81, 82 and 170 provide the constitutional framework.

2. How has the Constitution addressed the population-representation dilemma?
 The 42nd Amendment, 1976 froze seat allocation based on the 1971 Census, while the 84th Amendment, 2001 extended the freeze until the first Census after 2026.

3. Why is the 2027 Census significant for delimitation?
 The 2027 Census is expected to trigger fresh delimitation under Articles 81 and 82, potentially changing the inter-State distribution of Lok Sabha seats.

4. What is gerrymandering?
 Gerrymandering is the manipulation of constituency boundaries to favour particular political interests, commonly through cracking and packing of voters.

5. What reforms can ensure fair delimitation?
 Possible measures include pro-rata seat augmentation, weighted representation, greater Rajya Sabha safeguards, Inter-State Council consultation, transparent GIS-based mapping and wider stakeholder participation.
