Lake Ontario

31-08-2026

Source: IE 

Recently, the US President signed an executive order to rename Lake Ontario as “Lake America”, citing its significance as a US asset and part of American heritage. 

  • Canada opposed the move, stating that the name Ontario has Indigenous Wendat origins and is over 400 years old, predating both the Canadian Confederation and the US Declaration of Independence. 
  • The new name is not binding on Canada or other countries as there is no single global authority for standardising geographical names. 

Lake Ontario 

  • Location and Identity: Lake Ontario is one of the five Great Lakes of North America (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario) and lies between the Ontario province of Canada and New York State of the USA. It is the only Great Lake that does not border Michigan. 
  • Size: Lake Ontario is the smallest of the five Great Lakes by surface area and forms the easternmost and final lake of the Great Lakes system.  
    • The Great Lakes system contains around 20% of the world’s surface freshwater, making it one of the largest freshwater ecosystems globally. 
  • Connectivity and Navigation: Its waters flow into the Saint Lawrence River, which connects the Great Lakes with the Atlantic Ocean, making it an important corridor for navigation, trade, and freshwater exchange.  
    • It is connected to Lake Erie through the Welland Canal and forms part of the Great Lakes–Saint Lawrence navigation system. 
  • Major Inflow: The Niagara River is the principal source of water for Lake Ontario, contributing about 85% of its inflow. 
  • Ecological Importance: The lake supports diverse aquatic ecosystems, including species such as the Lake Sturgeon, which faces conservation challenges.

Bitra Island

Source: HT 

The government has initiated the acquisition of Bitra Island in Lakshadweep for defence purposes.

Bitra Island:  

  • About: It is the smallest inhabited island in the Union Territory of Lakshadweep.  
  • Geography and Location: Situated at 11°36′N, 72°11′E, Bitra lies about 483 km (261 nautical miles) from Kochi, the gateway to Lakshadweep. 
    • It is part of the Aminidivi subgroup and spans 0.57 km in length and 0.28 km at its widest. 
  • Climate : The climate of Bitra closely resembles that of Kerala.  
    • Hot period: March to May, temperatures range from 25°C to 35°C. 
    • Annual Rainfall: 1600 mm. 
  • Lagoon and Marine Ecosystem:It is surrounded by a vast lagoon area of 45.61 sq km, supporting rich marine biodiversity.  
  • Population and Lifestyle:  As per the 2011 census , it has a population of 271, mostly dependent on fishing and coconut cultivation. 
  • Strategic Significance: Bitra is strategically located near major shipping lanes and is set to host a defense establishment, complementing naval bases at Kavaratti (INS Dweeprakshak) and Minicoy (INS Jatayu).

India’s Cotton Sector

21-07-2026

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

Cotton is one of India’s most important commercial crops and occupies a central place in the country’s agricultural and industrial economy. 

  • Its significance extends beyond farming, as it supports the textile value chain, rural livelihoods, employment, trade and exports, making the cotton sector an important part of India’s development story.  

How Does Cotton Contribute to India’s Growth and Development? 

  • Economic and Employment Significance: Cotton is one of India’s most important commercial crops, linking agriculture, industry, trade and exports. It accounts for around 23% of global fibre production, supports nearly 6 million farmers and provides employment to 40–50 million people in processing, textiles, transport and trade. 
  • Exports and White Gold: Exports of cotton fibre, yarn, fabrics and garments contribute significantly to India’s foreign exchange earnings. Owing to its economic importance, cotton is popularly known as “White Gold”. 
  • Sectoral Strength: India’s cotton sector is supported by rich biological diversity, cultivation of all four recognised cotton species, extensive area under cultivation and a well-developed textile and value-chain ecosystem. 
  • Government Support and Future Growth: MSP operations, the Mission for Cotton Productivity and Kasturi Cotton Bharat, along with technology adoption, market reforms and quality improvement measures, are enhancing productivity, competitiveness and long-term growth. 

From Swadeshi to Atmanirbhar Bharat 

  • Ancient Legacy and Global Trade: India has been associated with cotton cultivation and textiles for thousands of years. Ancient India was a global centre for cotton textiles, exporting fabrics to Eastern and European markets, while cotton cloth also served as a medium of exchange. 
  • Rise of the Modern Industry: The modern cotton textile industry emerged in the 19th century with mills in Kolkata, Mumbai and Ahmedabad, which later became known as the “Manchester of India”. 
  • Swadeshi and Wartime Expansion: The industry expanded rapidly during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The Swadeshi Movement promoted indigenous mills and reduced dependence on British goods, while global demand during the World Wars accelerated growth. 
  • Growth before Independence: By the mid-20th century, the number of textile mills and cotton cloth production had increased significantly, reflecting the scale, resilience and self-reliance of India’s cotton industry and laying the foundation for Atmanirbhar Bharat. 

What Are the Major Dimensions of India’s Cotton Sector? 

  • Four Cotton Species: India is the only country cultivating all four recognised cotton species—G. arboreum and G. herbaceum (Asian cotton), G. barbadense (Egyptian cotton) and G. hirsutum (American upland cotton). G. hirsutum accounts for nearly 90% of India’s hybrid cotton production, and all Bt cotton hybrids currently cultivated in the country belong to this species. 

Bt Cotton 

  • Bt cotton is a genetically modified crop containing genes from Bacillus thuringiensis, a naturally occurring soil bacterium. 
  • These genes provide resistance against bollworms, a major cotton pest. 
  • Commercially introduced in India in 2002, Bt cotton reduced bollworm infestation and insecticide use, while higher yields and lower pest-control costs helped improve farm incomes.
  • Staple-Based Classification: Cotton is classified according to staple length, which determines fibre quality and its end use. India produces the complete range of staple categories.

  • Major Cotton-Growing Zones: Cotton production is concentrated in nine major states across three agro-ecological zones—the Northern Zone comprising Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan; the Central Zone comprising Gujarat, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh; and the Southern Zone comprising Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka. Cotton is also cultivated in Odisha and Tamil Nadu. 

Area, Production and Consumption 

  • Largest Cultivation Area: India ranks first globally in cotton cultivation area, with 114.84 lakh hectares under the crop. This represents around 38% of the global cotton area of 304.08 lakh hectares. 
  • Rain-fed Cultivation: Around 62% of India’s cotton area is rain-fed, while the remaining 38% is cultivated under irrigated conditions, making production significantly dependent on rainfall. 
  • Production and Global Position: India ranks second globally in cotton production and consumption. Cotton production is measured in bales, with one bale equivalent to 170 kg. Production stood at 297.24 lakh bales, or 5.06 million tonnes, in 2024–25, accounting for nearly 20% of global cotton output. 
  • Provisional Production and Consumption: In 2025–26, provisional cotton production stood at 290.91 lakh bales, or 4.95 million tonnes. Domestic consumption across MSME, non-MSME and non-textile segments reached 328 lakh bales, or 5.58 million tonnes, reflecting the strength of India’s textile industry. 

Cotton Exports and Trade Footprint 

  • Export Volume: India exported an estimated 18 lakh bales, equivalent to 0.31 million metric tonnes, in 2024–25. This accounted for around 3.37% of total global cotton exports of 541.69 lakh bales, or 9.21 million metric tonnes. 
  • Export Value and Major Markets: India’s cotton exports were valued at US$ 11.49 billion in FY25. The United States was the largest destination for Indian cotton fabrics and made-ups, accounting for 26.35% of exports, followed by Bangladesh at 19.81%, Sri Lanka at 5.11%, the United Kingdom at 2.38% and the United Arab Emirates at 2.32%. 

How Is India Building a Competitive Cotton Ecosystem? 

  • Minimum Support Price (MSP) System: The MSP system safeguards cotton farmers by ensuring remunerative prices when market prices fall below the announced support level. 
    • Before every cotton season (October–September), the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommends the MSP for Medium Staple and Long Staple seed cotton (kapas), ensuring at least 50% returns over the cost of production. 
  • Cotton Corporation of India (CCI): The Cotton Corporation of India (CCI) is the nodal agency responsible for procuring cotton under MSP whenever market prices fall below the support price. 
    • Expanded Procurement Network: Procurement centres increased from 508 (2024–25) to 571 (2025–26), covering 152 districts across 11 cotton-growing states.  
  • Mission for Cotton Productivity: The five-year Mission for Cotton Productivity, launched in 2025-26 with a budget of Rs. 5,659.22 crore, aims to enhance cotton production through research and innovation, focusing on climate-resilient and high-yielding varieties, particularly Extra Long Staple (ELS) cotton. It targets an increase in cotton production from 297 lakh bales to 498 lakh bales by 2031, benefiting around 32 lakh farmers across 140 districts in 14 states. 
    • Aligned with the ‘5F’ vision of farm, fibre, factory, fashion and foreign, the initiative seeks to enhance farmer incomes and ensure a steady supply of quality cotton.  
  • Special Project on Cotton under NFSM: Implemented under the National Food Security Mission (NFSM) since 2023–24 to improve productivity through field demonstrations of location-specific technologies in 8 major cotton-growing states. 
    • The pilot interventions focus on three key technologies: the High-Density Planting System, the Closer Spacing Planting System, and production technology for Extra-Long Staple (ELS) cotton, with the aim of improving productivity, fibre quality, and farmers' incomes. 
    • The 2023–24 fiscal year saw a marked increase in cotton productivity across 9,175 hectares, a success that drove further expansion in 2024–25. Given the positive impact on crop yields and farmer earnings, the project has been extended to 2025–26 to sustain these gains. 

  • Kapas Kisan App: A digital platform designed to make MSP procurement transparent, paperless and farmer-friendly. 
    • Key Features: Enables self-registration, advance slot booking, Aadhaar-linked payments and real-time SMS updates, significantly reducing waiting time at procurement centres. 
    • Impact: With over 41 lakh registered farmers, the app has improved transparency, efficiency and ease of cotton procurement. 
  • Kasturi Cotton Bharat: A flagship branding initiative to position Indian cotton globally through traceability, certification and branding. 
    • Implementation: Executed jointly by the Ministry of Textiles, Cotton Corporation of India (CCI) and TEXPROCIL, with a total outlay of ₹30 crore shared equally between the government and industry.  
    • Supporting Initiatives: The programme promotes Kasturi Villages, awareness workshops, sustainability practices, branding and a dedicated digital platform, positioning Indian cotton as a global symbol of quality, purity and sustainability.

Conclusion 

India’s cotton story reflects continuity, resilience and transformation. From ancient cotton trade and the Swadeshi movement to modern biotechnology, digital procurement and global branding, cotton remains deeply woven into India’s agricultural and industrial economy. With stronger productivity, quality, traceability and value addition, India’s “White Gold” can support farmer incomes, textile competitiveness, exports and the vision of Atmanirbhar Bharat.

Recurring Flood in Punjab

10-09-2025

For Prelims: Rivers of Punjab, BBMB, Dhussi bundhs, IPCC AR6, MSP, C-FLOOD system, BHUVAN platform, Krishi Vigyan Kendras.

For Mains: Causes of Floods in Punjab, Role of Governance and Infrastructure, Measures for Sustainable Flood Management.

Source: IE

Why in News?

Punjab (land of five rivers) is facing one of its worst floods in 40 years, with all 23 districts affected, 3.8 lakh people impacted, and over 11.7 lakh hectares of farmland destroyed. 

  • This has raised debates on the continuous and large-scale flooding in Punjab and related issues.

What are the Causes of Flooding in Punjab?

Natural Causes

  • Heavy Monsoon Rains: Intense rainfall in catchment areas (Himachal, J&K, Punjab), amplified by cloudbursts, leads to sudden river swelling.
  • Geographical Vulnerability: Punjab is drained by three perennial rivers—Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej—along with the seasonal Ghaggar and smaller tributaries (choes). 
    • These rivers make the state fertile (producing ~20% of India’s wheat and 12% of rice from 1.5% of landmass), earning it the title “food bowl of India”, but also prone to floods.
    • Past major floods occurred in 1955, 1988, 1993, 2019, and 2023.
  • Climate Change: Altered weather patterns, with intense and erratic rainfall, have transformed the monsoon from an agricultural ally to a destructive force, as per IPCC AR6 findings.

Human-Induced Factors

  • Dam Management Issues: Bhakra (Sutlej), Pong (Beas), and Thein/Ranjit Sagar (Ravi) dams release water during heavy rains (45% excess rainfall in 2025), often without timely coordination and timely warnings.
    • In 2025, unprecedented inflows (20% higher than 2023 at Pong) led to sudden releases, flooding downstream areas.
  • Inadequate Flood Cushion: The Bhakra Beas Management Board (BBMB) is criticized for maintaining high reservoir levels in July–August for irrigation and power, leaving little buffer for heavy rains in August–September.
  • Barrage Failures: In August 2025, two gates of Madhopur Barrage on Ravi failed after sudden dam releases.
  • Weak Embankments (Dhussi Bundhs): Poor maintenance and illegal mining have weakened flood protection structures.
    • Failure to implement a 2024 flood-preparedness guidebook led to unmaintained canals, and clogged drainage systems obstructing natural water flow.
  • Governance Gaps: Lack of coordination between Centre-controlled BBMB, Punjab’s irrigation authorities, and disaster response agencies.
    • Poor drainage systems in southern Punjab’s Malwa region, coupled with incessant local rainfall, have caused severe waterlogging.
  • Unregulated Development: Illegal construction on floodplains and riverbanks, coupled with deforestation, has reduced natural flood buffers. 
    • The Supreme Court and National Green Tribunal noted illegal tree felling as a contributor to floods and landslides.

What are the Key Challenges in Punjab’s Flood Management?

Governance Issues

  • Centralized Control: The major centrally controlled dams prioritizes irrigation and power over flood management, leaving Punjab with limited influence. 
    • The 2022 amendment allowing non-Punjab/Haryana officers in top BBMB posts has further strained state-Centre relations.
  • Reactive Approach: Governments often respond post-flood rather than investing in preventive measures like embankment strengthening or desilting.

Infrastructure Deficiencies

  • Weak Embankments: Illegal sand mining and poorly maintained drainage systems exacerbate waterlogging, particularly in southern Punjab.
  • Underinvestment: Strengthening embankments and desilting rivers require Rs 4,000–5,000 crore, which remains unaddressed due to funding constraints.
  • Climate Variability: Increasingly erratic monsoons and extreme rainfall events, driven by climate change, challenge existing flood management strategies.

What are the Impacts of the Floods in Punjab?

  • Agricultural Devastation: Over 4 lakh acres of farmland submerged, with crops like paddy and basmati rice facing quality issues, potentially fetching prices below MSP.
    • Post-flood challenges include land erosion, silt deposition, and difficulties in sowing new crops, threatening Punjab’s role as India’s food bowl.
  • Economic Fallout: Farmers face financial hardship due to crop losses and degraded land, exacerbating existing agricultural debt.
    • Infrastructure damage, including roads and irrigation systems, requires significant repair costs, straining state resources.
  • Public Health Crisis: Floodwaters, particularly from polluted rivers like Buddha Dariya in Ludhiana, have caused “black floods,” carrying industrial pollutants and untreated waste, risking outbreaks of cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A, dengue, and malaria.
    • Long-term groundwater contamination and soil degradation pose environmental threats.
  • Social and Humanitarian Impact: Several people were evacuated, but displaced families face challenges in accessing food, shelter, and safety, with particular risks for women and children.

What Measures Can be Taken?

  • Scientific Dam Management: Revise BBMB “rule curves” (storage and release policies) to include climate forecasts and ensure adequate flood cushion.
  • Strengthening Embankments: Invest in dhussi bundhs (earthen embankments), prevent illegal mining (through satellite monitoring), and modernise drainage networks.
  • Integrated Flood Management: Improve Centre–State coordination on dam releases; establish transparent communication channels.
    • Adopt the C-FLOOD system for village-level forecasting and integrate it with meteorological and hydrological data via NRSC’s BHUVAN platform.
  • Community-Centric Preparedness: Expand flood forecasting, digital alerts, and village-level disaster plans and adoptive capacity building through Krishi Vigyan Kendras.
    • Implement Zero Casualty Approach through local monitoring, early warning, and mock drills.
  • Climate-Resilient Infrastructure: Build urban drainage systems, restore wetlands, and undertake river desilting to absorb excess flows.
    • Integrate climate models into flood forecasting to anticipate extreme rainfall events.
    • Promote flood-resistant crops and diversify agriculture to reduce dependency on flood-prone Kharif crops.

Conclusion

Punjab’s geography makes it inherently flood-prone, but poor dam management, weak embankments, and governance lapses convert natural hazards into human-made disasters. A shift towards scientific water regulation, resilient infrastructure, and transparent governance is essential to safeguard lives, agriculture, and Punjab’s role as India’s food bowl.

Andaman & Nicobar Islands Set Guinness World Records

09-05-2026

Source: TH 

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands recently hosted two unique underwater world records at Swaraj Dweep (Havelock Island) to promote eco-tourism and establish the islands as a prime adventure destination. 

  • The events included the "Largest Flag Unfurled Underwater" near Radha Nagar Beach, where a 60m x 40m Indian flag was unfurled, and the "Tallest Human Stack Underwater" near the lighthouse area, where divers formed a 10-meter-high stack, both aiming to set new world records.  

Andaman and Nicobar Islands 

  • The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are a Union Territory of India comprising 836 islands, islets, and rocks located in the Bay of Bengal, with Port Blair as its capital. 
    • Swaraj Dweep, formerly known as Havelock Island, is part of the Ritchie’s Archipelago in the Andaman Islands. 
    • It was renamed in 2018 to honour India’s freedom struggle and national identity. 
  • The Ten Degree Channel, located near the 10° north latitude, separates the Andaman Islands from the Nicobar Islands. 
  • Great Nicobar Island is the largest island in the Nicobar group, covering about 910 sq km, while Indira Point on this island marks the southernmost point of India. 
  • The islands are home to five Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs): Great Andamanese, Jarwa, Onge, Shompen, and Sentinelese. 
  • The region is known for its rich biodiversity, including tropical rainforests, mangroves, coral reefs, and endemic flora and fauna species, and is also important for tourism activities such as eco-tourism and scuba diving. 
  • The islands hold major strategic significance due to their proximity to the Strait of Malacca, one of the world’s busiest maritime trade routes, and host India’s only integrated tri-service command.

Nauru Renamed as Republic of Naoero

06-08-2026

Source: TH 

The Pacific island nation formerly known internationally as Nauru has adopted its indigenous name, the Republic of Naoero, to reaffirm its cultural identity, linguistic heritage and national pride. 

  • About: Located northeast of Australia, Naoero is a coral-limestone island covering approximately 21 square kilometres. It is the world’s smallest island country and, with a population of about 12,000, the third-smallest country by population. By land area, it is the world’s third-smallest sovereign country, after Vatican City and Monaco. 
  • Change in Identity: The country’s international code will change from NRU to NRO, while its citizens will be called dei-Naoero instead of Nauruans. National aircraft, ships and official records will also adopt the revised name. 
  • Colonial History: The island was introduced to the Western world as Pleasant Island, annexed by Germany in 1888, administered by Australia, Britain and New Zealand, occupied by Japan during the Second World War and placed under a United Nations trusteeship before gaining independence in 1968.  
  • Colonial Origin of Nauru: Naoero was altered to Nauru because foreign colonisers found the indigenous pronunciation difficult. The name Nauru entered official usage during German colonial rule and continued after independence in 1968. 
  • Language Preservation: The renaming seeks to protect dorerin Naoero, the country’s indigenous language, which UNESCO classifies as severely endangered, and to strengthen its transmission to future generations.  
  • International Recognition: The government has formally notified countries and international organisations, while the United Nations and several foreign governments have begun using the name Naoero. 
  • Broader Significance: The change reflects a wider process of decolonisation in which countries reclaim indigenous names, similar to Eswatini, Türkiye and the Micronesian State of Chuuk.

Shipki La Pass

04-08-2026

Source: TH 

Cross-border trade between India and China through Shipki La Pass resumed on 1st August 2026, after a six-year suspension due to the Covid-19 pandemic.  

Shipki La Pass 

  • About: Shipki La is a high-altitude mountain pass situated at over 4,000 metres in the Kinnaur district of Himachal Pradesh, along the India-China border with the Tibet Autonomous Region.  
    • The pass was earlier known as Pema La or Shared Gate and was renamed Shipki La by the Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP) after 1962.  
  • Terrain: The Sutlej River, known as Langqen Zangbo in Tibet, enters India through the Shipki La region. The 28-km stretch from Namgia, the last Indian village, to Shipki La is barren, while heavy snowfall cuts off the pass during winter.  
  • Strategic Importance: Shipki La is strategically important because Indian positions are located at a higher elevation than those on the Chinese side. The area also contains several buildings used by Indian security agencies.  
  • Historical Significance: The pass forms part of an ancient Silk Route through which trade between Tibet and present-day Himachal Pradesh has taken place for centuries.  
    • Before Independence, Tibetan traders used Shipki La and the old Hindustan-Tibet Road to reach Rampur Bushahr, especially during the Lavi Fair.  
  • Trade: The route was closed after the 1962 India-China War and reopened in the early 1990s following a bilateral agreement. Presently, Trade generally takes place for a few months from June, after the snow recedes.  
    • Trade primarily involves the import of wool, livestock, yak products, religious items and minerals, and the export of grains, spices, tobacco, timber and metal tools. 
  • Barter System: Shipki La is unique among India’s border trade routes with China because trade traditionally takes place through barter system rather than monetary transactions. In contrast, trade through Lipulekh Pass in Uttarakhand and Nathu La Pass in Sikkim is conducted using currency.
