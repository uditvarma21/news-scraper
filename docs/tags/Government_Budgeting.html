RBI Urges States to Adopt Prudent Borrowing for Fiscal Sustainability

28-10-2026

For Prelims: Reserve Bank of India, Capital Expenditure, Inflation, Gross Fiscal Deficit, SASCI scheme  

For Mains: Centre-State financial relations, Fiscal federalism, Finance Commission recommendations, and State borrowing powers

Source: TH 

Why in News?

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor, while addressing the 36th Conference of State Finance Secretaries, urged State governments to adopt prudent borrowing strategies and efficient cash management practices to maintain their long-term fiscal health.

Summary 

  • Indian States face rising debt-to-GSDP ratios, fiscal deficits, off-budget borrowings and high committed expenditure, which reduce funds available for developmental activities and threaten long-term fiscal sustainability. 
  • States must adopt prudent borrowing, efficient cash management and FRBM compliance, prioritise capital expenditure, improve transparency in public finances and strengthen fiscal oversight to ensure sustainable growth and intergenerational equity.

What is Prudent Borrowing?

  • About: Prudent borrowing refers to a fiscal management strategy where a government limits its debt intake strictly to its long-term repayment capacity, ensuring that the borrowed funds are utilized efficiently. 
    • A core tenet of prudent borrowing is the "Golden Rule of Public Finance" borrowing should primarily be directed toward Capital Expenditure (Capex) (infrastructure, health, and education facilities) that generates future revenue, rather than Revenue Expenditure (day-to-day administrative costs or non-merit subsidies). 
  • Effective Cash Management: Prudent Borrowing involves aligning the borrowing calendar with actual cash-flow mismatches, preventing the accumulation of idle cash balances that incur unnecessary interest costs. 

Significance of Prudent Borrowing 

  • Macroeconomic Stability: It prevents the "crowding out" effect, ensuring that the government does not absorb the entire domestic credit pool, thereby leaving adequate and affordable credit for the private sector.  
  • Fiscal Space for Welfare: A large debt stock leads to massive interest payments. Currently, a significant portion of the Union and State revenue receipts is consumed merely by servicing debt. 
    • Lower debt through Prudent Borrowing lowers interest payment obligations, freeing up the budget (fiscal space) for critical developmental sectors like public health, green transitions, and education. 
  • Vulnerability to Sovereign Downgrades: High debt-to-GDP ratios keep sovereign credit ratings in the lower investment grades (e.g., BBB-), making external commercial borrowings expensive for Indian corporates. 
  • Inflationary Pressures: Unchecked fiscal deficits, if monetised, or if they drive up aggregate demand without commensurate supply, can lead to persistent structural inflation. 
  • Inter-Generational Equity: Prudent borrowing ensures that current consumption is not financed by imposing an unsustainable tax burden on future generations.

What is the Current State of Fiscal Health of Indian States?

While the consolidated General Government Debt (Centre + States) hovers around 81- 85% of GDP, state government debt accounts for a massive one-third of this overall figure. The fiscal health across states is highly asymmetric, characterised by the following trends: 

  • Elevated and Divergent Debt-to-GSDP Ratios: Aggregate state debt stood at 29.2% of GDP at the end of March 2026, remaining significantly above the 20% prudence limit recommended by the FRBM Review Committee. 
    • Highly Stressed States: States like Punjab and West Bengal struggle with severe fiscal stress, carrying debt burdens near or above 40% of their GSDP. 
    • The Prudent Performers: Conversely, Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Odisha have successfully contained their debt-to-GSDP ratios within the 20% limit. 
    • Consolidation Efforts: States such as Kerala (35.5%) and Tamil Nadu (29.2%) have shown fiscal discipline, reducing their outstanding liabilities by nearly 4 to 5 percentage points since 2021. 
  • Breaching Fiscal Deficit Targets: The states' consolidated Gross Fiscal Deficit (GFD) has widened and is budgeted at 3.3% of GDP for FY 2025-26.  
    • Alarmingly, 16 states budgeted a GFD exceeding the standard 3% limit, driven largely by a decline in central grants and revenue shortfalls. 
  • Rising Cost of Borrowing & Expenditure Rigidity: The sharp rise in government bond yields in October 2026 threatens to inflate the cost of financing state deficits. 
    • Furthermore, states are trapped by high committed expenditures collectively spending over 60% of their revenue receipts strictly on interest payments, salaries, pensions, and subsidies. This leaves minimal fiscal space for discretionary developmental projects. 
  • Crucial Role in the Economy: States account for two-thirds of total public spending and one-third of total revenue in India.  
    • Their fiscal performance, particularly their capacity to sustain Capital Expenditure (Capex) currently heavily supported by the Centre's 50-year interest-free loans under the SASCI scheme is central to the nation's long-term developmental trajectory.

What are the Key Issues in Current Borrowing Practices of States? 

  • Off-Budget Borrowings: States frequently bypass the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) borrowing limits by raising funds through State Public Sector Enterprises (SPSEs) or Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs). These loans are often backed by state government guarantees, circumventing legislative scrutiny.  
  • Revenue vs. Capital Expenditure: Borrowing is economically justified if utilized for Capex, which creates income-generating assets and has a high fiscal multiplier (approx. 2.45).  
    • However, states increasingly borrow to fund Revenue Expenditure (subsidies, loan waivers, and freebies), which yields no or less future returns. 
  • Contingent Liabilities: State guarantees to DISCOMs (power distribution companies) remain a ticking fiscal time bomb, threatening to devolve onto state budgets if these entities default. 
  • Market Volatility: Uncalibrated borrowing calendars by the Centre and States can lead to sudden spikes in bond yields, disrupting the bond market and monetary policy transmission. 

What Measures are Needed for Sustaining Long-Term Fiscal Health of States? 

  • Leveraging Digital Financial Platforms: As advised by the RBI Governor, states must onboard onto the Unified Lending Interface (ULI) and utilize Programmable CBDC (P-CBDC) for Direct Benefit Transfers.  
    • Programmable CBDCs can ensure that welfare funds are strictly utilized for their intended purpose, plugging leakages and enhancing the efficiency of public spending. 
  • Effective Use of the SLBC Forum: The State Level Bankers’ Committee (SLBC) is a vital inter-institutional forum for coordinating banking and development.  
    • States must actively use SLBCs to monitor credit disbursement, resolve bottlenecks in priority sector lending, and improve rural infrastructure financing, thereby stimulating local economic growth and increasing state revenues. 
  • Strict Adherence to FRBM Targets: States must strictly adhere to the fiscal deficit glide path mandated by their respective State Fiscal Responsibility Legislations (typically capped at 3 to 3.5% of GSDP). 
  • Establishing an Independent Fiscal Council: As recommended by the 13th and 14th Finance Commissions and the N.K. Singh Committee (2016), an independent Fiscal Council should be established to assess government forecasts, ensure debt sustainability, and bring transparency to off-budget financing.  
  • Capping Off-Budget Borrowings: The RBI and the Ministry of Finance must enforce strict accounting standards.  
    • The move by the Union Government to equate state off-budget borrowings with their standard borrowing limits under Article 293(3) is a positive step.  
  • Dynamic Debt Management: States should adopt the practices outlined in the RBI's 'Compendium on Cash and Debt Management'.  
    • This includes aligning market borrowings accurately with expenditure needs and adopting the Benchmark Issuance Strategy to improve liquidity and yield curves. 

Conclusion 

Prudent borrowing is not synonymous with zero borrowing; rather, it is about maintaining a delicate balance between leveraging debt for productive capital formation and avoiding the debt trap of consumption-led borrowing. As India navigates the Amrit Kaal towards becoming a developed nation, institutionalizing fiscal discipline and inter-generational equity will be the bedrock of its macroeconomic resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is prudent borrowing, and why is it important for State governments?
Prudent borrowing aligns public debt with repayment capacity and prioritises productive capital expenditure over routine consumption, supporting debt sustainability and intergenerational equity. 

2. What does Article 293 of the Indian Constitution provide?
Article 293 governs borrowing by States. Under Article 293(3), a State indebted to the Union requires the Union Government's consent to raise further loans, subject to the constitutional conditions. 

3. What is the FRBM framework, and how does it promote fiscal discipline?
The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) framework seeks to promote fiscal stability through deficit and debt management, greater transparency and responsible government borrowing. 

4. What are off-budget borrowings, and why are they a concern?
Off-budget borrowings are liabilities raised through public entities or other arrangements outside the regular State budget. They can conceal the true debt burden and weaken fiscal transparency. 

5. What is the SASCI scheme, and how does it support State finances?
The Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment (SASCI) provides long-term interest-free loans to States for capital investment, supporting infrastructure creation and economic growth.

2nd Regional Open Digital Health Summit (RODHS) 2025

22-11-2025

Source: PIB

India hosted the 2nd Regional Open Digital Health Summit (RODHS) 2025 in New Delhi, bringing South-East Asian countries together to advance Universal Health Coverage (UHC) through Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) and open standards.

  • It was organised by National e-Governance Division (NeGD) under the Ministry of Electronics & IT, National Health Authority (NHA), World Health Organization South-East Asia Regional Office and UNICEF.
  • India highlighted the strength of its DPI stack such as Aadhaar, Unified Payments Interface (UPI), CoWIN, and Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission showcasing their role in secure, scalable health systems.
  • WHO and UNICEF underlined the need for trust, skills, interoperability, and community-centric design for successful digital health adoption.

RODHS 

  • It is a key regional platform to advance open, interoperable, and people-centered digital health systems across the WHO South-East Asia Region. 
  • It brings together policymakers, technologists, and health experts to discuss Digital Public Infrastructure, WHO SMART (Standards-based, Machine-readable, Adaptive, Requirements-based, and Testable) Guidelines, and AI-enabled innovation.
  • The Summit aims to strengthen regional cooperation and develop country-specific roadmaps to build scalable digital health systems that support UHC, health security, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), building on the momentum of the inaugural summit held in Nairobi.

Cess and its Role in Union Finance

16-08-2025

Source: TH  

Why in News? 

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has flagged a Rs 3.69 lakh crore shortfall in transferring cess collections to their intended funds, bringing into focus the purpose and proper utilisation of such levies.

What is the Purpose of Levying a Cess? 

  • About: A cess, recognized under Article 270 is an additional tax levied by the Government of India for a specific purpose. It is levied on top of existing taxes or duties listed in the Union List. 
  • Purpose: Cess is distinct from regular taxes as it is earmarked for a designated purpose. The purpose of a cess must be clearly stated in the law imposing it and should be for a Union purpose, outside List II (State List) of the Seventh Schedule. 
    • Cesses are named after their purpose (like Education Cess or Swachh Bharat Cess) and must be used only for that purpose, without being diverted for general government expenditure. 
  • Role in Union Finance:  Proceeds from a cess, along with surcharges levied by the Union, are credited to the Consolidated Fund of India and are excluded from the divisible pool of taxes, remaining under the Union’s control. 

Surcharge 

  • About: Article 271 of the Indian Constitution empowers Parliament to impose a surcharge on certain taxes and duties for Union purposes.  
    • This surcharge is in addition to the existing taxes and duties, often referred to as a “tax on tax.” 
  • Applicability:  Applied to individuals, companies, and other taxpayers in certain income brackets. Usually applicable when income exceeds Rs 50 lakh in a financial year. 
    • The rate varies based on income level and type of income. 
  • Purpose and Nature: It is progressive in nature (higher earners contribute more), it promotes social equity and addresses income disparity 
    • Increases total tax liability for high-income taxpayers. 
  • Cess vs. Surcharge:  Both Cess and surcharge are credited to Consolidated Fund of India (CFI) and are not shared with the states but differ in usage.  
    • Surcharge is spent like other taxes, while cess must be allocated separately and used only for its specific purpose.  

The 13th and 14th Finance Commissions upheld the exclusion of surcharge from the divisible tax pool, but recommended reducing the Centre’s dependence on surcharge revenues.

Income Tax Bill, 2025

16-08-2025

Source: IE

Both houses of the Parliament passed the Income Tax Bill, 2025, which seeks to simplify, rationalise, and shorten the existing Income Tax 1961 Act. 

  • The Bill defines virtual digital space as any digital environment including email, social media, online accounts, cloud servers, websites, and digital platforms.  
    • If retained, tax authorities could access or bypass passwords to investigate potential tax evasion or under-reported income, with companies potentially required to assist. 
  • It replaces the dual concepts of ‘assessment year’ and ‘previous year’ with a uniform ‘tax year’, defined as 1st April 1 to 31st March. 
  • The Bill removes the restriction limiting refunds to on-time filed returns, allowing claims even for belatedly filed returns.  
  • The Bill clarifies there will be no Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) remittances for education purposes financed by financial institutions. 
    • Individuals with no tax liability can seek a nil Tax Deduction at Source certificate in advance. 
  • The applicability of the Alternate Minimum Tax (AMT) for Limited Liability Partnerships (LLPs) has been aligned with the existing provisions of the IT Act. 
    • AMT ensures that individuals benefiting from tax deductions and exemptions pay at least the minimum amount of tax. 

Income Tax 

  • It is a direct tax on the income earned by individuals, companies, or other entities during a financial year. For individual taxpayers in India, it is levied according to progressive tax slabs.  
  • These slabs may vary under the new tax regime or with applicable rebates and deductions. 
  • According to the Central Board of Direct Taxes, India’s gross direct tax collections for 2025-26 stood at Rs 7.99 lakh crore, down 1.9% from Rs 8.14 lakh crore in FY 2024-25.
