Regulation of Social Media Addiction Among Children

31-08-2026

For Prelims: Artificial Intelligence, Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act), 2023, Article 19, Section 79 of the IT Act, 2000 

For Mains: Social Media Regulation and Governance, Protecting children from online exploitation, addiction and targeted advertising, Intermediary Liability

Source: IE 

Why in News?

Meta Platforms has reached a landmark settlement of up to USD 17.1 billion with multiple US states, agreeing to new safety restrictions for users under 18 on Facebook and Instagram, following allegations that its platforms encouraged addictive and compulsive use among children and teenagers and violated child-privacy norms. 

  • The settlement is significant for India as the Economic Survey 2025–26 has flagged digital addiction as a growing public health concern, particularly among the 15–24 age group, while the Government is examining age restrictions, usage limits and stronger parental-consent mechanisms for minors. 
  • The US settlement offers India a potential regulatory model by combining age assurance, daily usage caps, night-time restrictions, parental controls and independent oversight, shifting greater responsibility from children and parents to social-media platforms themselves.

Summary 

  • Social media addiction among children is emerging as a major concern due to addictive platform design, algorithmic manipulation and excessive screen time, causing risks to mental health, privacy, sleep, education and social development. 
  • India needs a rights-based, risk-sensitive regulatory framework combining child data protection, age verification, algorithmic accountability, safety-by-design, deepfake regulation and digital literacy, while balancing privacy, free speech and access to digital benefits. 

Why is Meta’s $17.1-Billion Settlement Significant? 

Significance 

  • Transition to Legally Binding Product Redesigns: Moves child safety from voluntary, easily ignored features (e.g., "Take a Break") to court-enforceable product mandates including default 2-hour daily limits, midnight-to-6 AM lockouts, disabled cosmetic-procedure filters, and independent audit oversight.  
  • Stronger Platform Accountability: Shifts responsibility from parents and children towards tech platforms for preventing harmful and compulsive use. 
  • Child Privacy Protection: Highlights the importance of laws such as US Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) (1998) in holding platforms accountable for collecting children’s data without parental consent. 
  • Industry-Wide Impact: Could push other major platforms such as YouTube and TikTok to adopt similar safeguards. 

Key Takeaways for India 

  • Policy Blueprint: Offers India a practical model for age-based restrictions, daily usage limits, night-time controls and parental consent. 
  • Beyond Voluntary Regulation: Supports India’s debate on making platforms legally responsible for protecting minors rather than relying solely on parental supervision. 
  • Need for Platform-Wide Rules: Highlights the need for consistent safeguards across social-media platforms to prevent children from simply shifting to less-regulated apps. 

How do Social Media Platforms "Hook" Children? 

  • Dopamine Loops and Variable Rewards: Social media platforms operate on the principles of the Attention Economy, utilizing behavioral psychology and neuroscience to maximize user engagement. 
    • Similar to a slot machine, platforms use unpredictable "variable rewards" (likes, tags, comments, and notifications) to trigger the release of dopamine in the brain's mesolimbic system.  
    • This creates a neurological dependency where the anticipation of a reward compels the user to continuously check their device. 
  • Frictionless Design : Features like infinite scrolling and auto-playing videos remove the brain's natural "stopping cues".  
    • This bypasses conscious decision-making, leading to continuous, uninterrupted consumption. 
  • Algorithmic Curation: Artificial Intelligence (AI) tracks micro-interactions (like pause durations on a video) to curate hyper-personalized feeds. These adaptive algorithms prioritize engagement over safety, frequently pushing extreme, emotionally triggering, or highly idealized content. 
  • Fear of Missing Out (FOMO): The design exploits a child's primal need for social validation and peer inclusion. Features like "disappearing stories" or read receipts create artificial urgency, making adolescents feel they must remain constantly connected. 

Impacts of Social Media Addiction on Children 

  • Mental Health: Increases anxiety, depression, loneliness, low self-esteem, and body-image issues, especially among teenage girls.  
    • 2024 meta-analysis covering 143 studies and over one million adolescents found that higher platform engagement directly correlates with heightened rates of depression and anxiety.  
  • Cognitive & Brain Effects: Excessive use may affect the prefrontal cortex  (it manages high-level thinking, decision-making, and emotional control) and amygdala (an area of your brain responsible for emotional processing), contributing to reduced attention, impulse control, and reward sensitivity. 
  • Sleep & Physical Health: Late-night scrolling and blue-light exposure disrupt melatonin, sleep quality, and cognitive development. 
  • Withdrawal Effects: Sudden restriction can cause irritability, distress, and compulsive behaviour in heavily dependent users. 
  • Academic & Social Impact: Excessive smartphone use can reduce academic performance, real-world social interaction, and development of social skills.

Global Responses: 

  • International Bans: Countries like Australia, Malaysia, and Indonesia have instituted strict bans or mandated parental controls for social media access for children under 16. 
  • South Korea: The Cinderella/Shutdown Law restricted online gaming for under-16s from midnight to 6 AM. It was abolished in 2021. 
  • US: Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) (1998) requires parental consent for collecting data from under-13s.  
    • Children’s Internet Protection Act (CIPA) (2000) requires federally funded schools and libraries to filter harmful online content. 
  • EU: General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), 2018 provides strong data-privacy protections. The EU has also pursued stronger age and parental-consent safeguards for minors. 
  • UK: Age-Appropriate Design Code requires platforms to prioritise children's privacy and safety through stronger default settings. 
  • France: A 2023 law requires parental authorisation for under-15s to access social media, with penalties for non-compliance. 
  • China: Limits minors' internet use by age, with daily time limits and nighttime restrictions. 

Indian Framework: 

  • Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act), 2023: Regulates the processing of children’s personal data (under 18) by requiring: 
    • Parental Consent: Verifiable consent from a parent/guardian. 
    • Child Well-being: Data processing must protect the child’s welfare. 
    • No Tracking & Targeted Ads: Restricts behavioural monitoring, tracking and targeted advertising. 
  • Information Technology (IT) Rules, 2021: Places the onus on social media intermediaries to ensure a safe online environment, though it relies heavily on self-regulation.

What are the Challenges in Regulating Social Media? 

  • Age Verification Problem: Self-declared age limits are easily bypassed, while KYC or biometric verification can create risks of surveillance, privacy violations and data breaches. 
  • Platform Profit vs Child Safety: Social-media platforms depend on user engagement and screen time, creating a conflict between their revenue model and efforts to curb addiction. 
  • Digital Literacy Gap: Many parents lack the skills to use parental controls and privacy settings, making age restrictions difficult to implement effectively. 
  • Freedom of Speech vs Censorship: Regulation must balance Article 19(1)(a) with restrictions under Article 19(2).  
    • In Anuradha Bhasin vs. Union of India (2020), the Supreme Court ruled that the Freedom of Speech and Expression and the Freedom to Practice any Profession over the medium of the Internet is a constitutionally protected Fundamental Right under Article 19. 
    • Vague definitions of harmful content can cause over-censorship and a chilling effect on legitimate expression. 
  • Privacy vs Traceability: The IT Rules, 2021 require certain messaging platforms to identify the first originator of information. This raises concerns that traceability could weaken end-to-end encryption and privacy. 
  • Safe Harbour Dilemma: Section 79 of the IT Act, 2000 provides intermediary protection, but platforms increasingly recommend, curate and monetise content, blurring the line between passive intermediaries and active publishers. 
  • Algorithmic Opacity: Engagement-based algorithms can amplify misinformation, hate speech and sensational content, while proprietary algorithms remain difficult for regulators to scrutinise. 
  • Deepfake Challenge: AI-generated deepfakes, voice clones and synthetic identities make rapid detection and authentication of online content increasingly difficult. 
  • Cross-Border Enforcement: Foreign-based platforms and overseas data storage create difficulties in evidence collection, content removal and law enforcement, particularly when jurisdictions have conflicting laws. 
  • Scale & Linguistic Diversity: Billions of posts and India's regional languages and dialects make automated moderation prone to errors, especially in distinguishing sarcasm, context and legitimate criticism. 
  • Reactive Regulation: Reliance on notice-and-takedown mechanisms often means action is taken only after harmful content has spread. India's fragmented regulatory framework also creates coordination gaps. 
  • Blanket Ban vs Digital Benefits: Strict restrictions may protect children from online harms but can also limit learning, creativity, socialisation, access to information and digital-skills development. 

How Can Social Media Regulation Be Strengthened? 

  • Adopt Graded Age-Based Safeguards: Introduce different levels of protection for 8–12, 12–16 and 16–18 age groups, recognising differences in maturity and vulnerability.  
    • Consider age-appropriate screen-time limits and night-time restrictions, while allowing reasonable parental flexibility.  
  • Modernise Legal Framework: Replace the IT Act, 2000 with the proposed Digital India Act (DIA) and shift from reactive notice-and-takedown to preventive ex-ante regulation. 
  • Tiered Safe Harbour: Hold platforms that actively curate, amplify and monetise content to higher accountability standards than passive intermediaries. 
  • Independent Digital Regulator: Establish an autonomous, quasi-judicial authority to oversee platforms, handle appeals and protect constitutional rights. 
  • Safety by Design: Mandate algorithmic audits, restrict addictive dark patterns like infinite scroll and autoplay, and make child-safety settings the default. 
  • Deepfake Regulation: Require AI-generated content watermarking and provenance metadata to improve identification of synthetic media. 
  • Privacy-Preserving Enforcement: Use tools such as Zero-Knowledge Proofs and metadata analysis instead of weakening end-to-end encryption, with surveillance subject to judicial oversight and proportionality. 
  • International Cooperation: Align with frameworks such as the EU Digital Services Act (DSA) and strengthen MLATs for faster cross-border access to digital evidence. 
  • Digital & Media Literacy: Integrate digital wellness, misinformation detection, source verification, algorithmic awareness and responsible social-media use into school education.  
    • Punjab’s introduction of AI as a mainstream subject from Classes I–XII can serve as a model for expanding such digital literacy to include mandatory training on safe and responsible social-media use.  
  • Institutional Support: Expand specialised centres like AIIMS Delhi’s Behavioural Addictions Clinic and CAR-AB for early detection, counselling and treatment of digital addiction.  

Conclusion 

India needs to move from a binary approach of unrestricted access vs blanket bans towards a rights-based, risk-sensitive regulatory model that combines child safety, privacy, free speech, digital literacy and platform accountability. The focus should be on making digital platforms safer by design rather than simply restricting access.

 It provides conditional intermediary liability protection for third-party content, but platform curation, amplification and monetisation increasingly challenge the traditional passive-intermediary model.

 Personalised recommendations, variable rewards, infinite scrolling and autoplay maximise engagement by exploiting behavioural patterns and reducing natural stopping cues.

Generative AI and Copyright Issues

17-07-2025

For Prelims: Artificial intelligence, Copyright Infringement, ChatGPT, DU Photocopy Case (Oxford v. Rameshwari Photocopy Services, 2016) case 

For Mains: Copyright Infringement and Use of AI, Fair Use and Transformative Use in the Context of AI-Generated Works 

Source: IE 

Why in News?

The rapid growth of generative artificial intelligence (AI) like ChatGPT and Gemini has triggered concerns over the use of copyrighted content, raising key debates around intellectual property rights, authorship, deep fakes, and ethical AI governance.  

  • These developments challenge conventional legal and ethical frameworks and require urgent attention. 

 

What is Artificial Intelligence (AI)? 

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI), first coined by John McCarthy in 1956 broadly refers to the ability of machines to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, and problem-solving. 
  • Generative AI represents a major advancement in AI, enabling machines to create original content—such as text, images, music, code, or videos—by learning from large datasets like books, websites, and digital art.  
  • Eg: ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude for natural language generation; DALL·E and Midjourney for image creation; and AIVA and Amper Music for music composition. 

 

What are the Key Copyright Challenges Related to AI-generated Content? 

  • AI Mimics Copyrighted Material: AI models require vast datasets, often comprising copyrighted texts, images, and music, to train and optimize performance, raising concerns of unauthorized copying when AI mimics or replicates creative elements of original works. 
    • Although the AI may not directly reproduce works, its outputs can closely resemble protected content, leading to potential copyright infringement. 
  • Fair Use & Transformative Use: The doctrine of fair use allows limited use of copyrighted material without permission, especially for research, education, or commentary. In the US, it is based on 4 factors: purpose, nature, amount used, and market impact.  
    • Tech firms claim AI training is transformative use, adding new expression, meaning or utility and qualifying as “non-expressive use”, i.e., not directly replicating expressive elements. 
    • In the Bartz vs. Anthropic (Claude AI) case, a US court upheld fair use for AI training on pirated books but acknowledged liability for storage. 
    • In Silverman vs. Meta  (LLaMA AI) case, no market harm was found, but the court emphasized the need for creator compensation frameworks — highlighting evolving legal standards. 
  • AI, Creativity & Legal Liability: AI is reshaping copyright norms through AI-generated works (created solely by machines) and AI-assisted works (human-created with AI support).  
    • AI-assisted content is owned by humans, but AI-generated works raise unresolved questions of authorship and ownership.  
    • Legal liability in copyright infringement cases remains unclear, whether on the developer, user, or platform. 

What is the Legal Status of AI-Generated Content in India? 

  • Legal Status of AI-Generated Content in India: 
    • Legal Gaps for AI-Generated Content: Indian law currently does not recognize non-human authorship, the Indian Copyright Act, 1957 attributes authorship solely to natural persons, excluding AI and its developers. As a result, AI-generated works without significant human input are not protected.  
      • Additionally, the use of copyrighted material for AI training remains a legal grey area, with no explicit provisions in place.  
      • While Section 52 permits certain uses for "research," its applicability to AI training has not been tested in Indian courts. 
    • Protection for AI-Assisted Works: When a human uses AI as a creative tool, the output may be protected and in such cases, authorship lies with the human, akin to traditional works created using digital tools. 
  • Fair Use Provisions under Indian Copyright Law: Section 52 of the Copyright Act, 1957 outlines exceptions to infringement, including: 
    • Private or personal use, including research or education 
    • Criticism or review of any work 
    • Reporting of current events or public lectures 
    • Reproduction for judicial proceedings 
    • Transient or incidental storage during digital transmission or linking 
      • These exceptions form the doctrine of fair dealing, similar to the fair use doctrine in the US. 
  • Judicial Interpretation Regarding Use of Copyrighted Material: 
    • Civic Chandran v. Ammini Amma (1996): The Kerala High Court held that parody does not amount to infringement, establishing a 3-factor test: quantity/value of content taken, purpose of use and likelihood of market competition 
    • Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak (2008): The Supreme Court (SC) held that raw SC judgments are public domain and not copyrightable, but editorial additions (like headnotes, formatting) by publishers are copyrightable if they show originality through skill and judgment.  
      • The Court rejected the “sweat of the brow” doctrine and adopted the “skill and judgment” test, marking a key judicial interpretation on the originality threshold under Indian copyright law. 
    • India TV v. Yashraj Films (2012): Delhi High Court, in this case expanded fair dealing to cinematographic and musical works, especially after the Copyright (Amendment) Act, 2012, which included exceptions for disabled access and non-commercial public use. 
    • DU Photocopy Case (Oxford v. Rameshwari Photocopy Services, 2016):  The Delhi High Court ruled that photocopying book excerpts for educational use was fair dealing, affirming access to knowledge and public interest as guiding principles. 
  • Comparative & Evolving Frameworks: Indian courts increasingly rely on US fair use factors to interpret fair dealing under Section 52 of the Copyright Act, 1957, including the purpose and character of use, nature of the copyrighted work, amount and substantiality, market impact, and transformative character.  
    • However, India lacks a clear definition of “substantial portion”, leaving judicial discretion to determine fair use on a case-by-case basis.  
    • As a TRIPS-compliant nation, India seeks to align with Article 13 of the TRIPS Agreement, which mandates that exceptions to copyright must not conflict with normal exploitation of the work or unreasonably prejudice the rights holder. 
  • Policy Developments: A 2025 Commerce Ministry panel is reviewing the Indian Copyright Act, 1957 to address digital and AI-related gaps.  

Comparative Global Approaches to AI-Generated Content 

  • US: Copyright is granted only if there’s substantial human creativity (Thaler v. Perlmutter, 2023). Purely AI-generated works are not protected. 
  • European Union: The AI Act 2024 mandates transparency of training data. Discussions are ongoing on a sui generis right for AI outputs, though the 2019 Copyright Directive lacks direct provisions. 
  • China: Beijing Internet Court delivered a first-of-its-kind judgment in mainland China, recognizing that a picture generated using the AI software Stable Diffusion qualifies as an artwork protected under copyright law.  
    • The court emphasized the “originality” and the intellectual contribution of the human creator, despite the use of AI tools. 
  • United Kingdom: Section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act, 1988 allows copyright for computer-generated works (CGWs) without a human author, assigning authorship to the person making the “necessary arrangements”. However, such works lack moral rights and the provision remains rarely applied due to legal ambiguities and limited judicial interpretation. 

Note 

  • In 2021, South Africa became the first country to grant a patent to a machine-generated work, recognizing AI system DABUS as the inventor of a food container design based on fractal geometry.  

Way Forward 

  • Legal Modernization & Fair Use Assessment: To address AI-related challenges such as training data usage and algorithmic reproduction, the Indian Copyright Act, 1957 should be updated to include AI-specific provisions.  
    • Additionally, courts can adopt a structured approach to fair use assessment by applying the 4-factor test outlined by the Kerala High Court in Civic Chandran v. C. Ammini Amma (1996), aligning India's framework more closely with US fair use standards. 
  • Data Governance & Compliance: Establish clear data usage policies for AI training with oversight mechanisms, audit trails, and mandatory compliance officers in AI firms to ensure adherence to copyright norms and ethical data handling. 
  • Balanced Innovation & Rights Protection: Develop a multi-stakeholder regulatory framework to strike a balance between innovation and copyright protection. This includes enabling collective licensing models and ensuring fair compensation for content creators. 
  • International Cooperation & Standard Setting: India should proactively engage in global forums like WIPO to shape harmonized copyright rules for AI and contribute to building global ethical and legal standards for generative technologies.

Deepfakes

16-07-2025

Source: IE 

Why in News?

Denmark has proposed a landmark copyright amendment to ban the sharing of deepfakes without consent, protecting individuals’ voices, faces, and likenesses.  

  • The proposed law treats realistic deepfakes as copyright violations, gives individuals control over their digital likeness for 50 years after death, and requires platforms to remove such content or face penalties. 

What are Deepfakes? 

  • About: Deepfakes are synthetic media (videos, images, or audio) that are digitally altered using Artificial Intelligence (AI) to make it look like someone said or did something they never actually did. They blur the line between reality and manipulation. 
  • Technology Used: Deepfakes are powered by deep learning, a subset of machine learning, which itself is a subset of AI. 
    • They're created using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), where two neural networks (a generator and a discriminator) work together to create and refine fake content. 
      • GANs use real data to recreate faces, voices, or movements. A generator makes fake content, and a discriminator tries to detect it. The generator improves until it can fool the discriminator, 
      • Natural Language Processing (NLP) is used for cloning voices. Lip-syncing techniques align deepfake audio with video. 
  • Common Types: 
    • Face swaps: Replace a person’s face in a video with another’s. 
    • Voice clones: Imitate someone’s voice to say anything. 
    • Source Video Manipulation: Make someone appear to do or say things they never did. 
  • Detection: 
    • Clues: unnatural blinking, facial distortions, mismatched audio, lighting glitches. 
    • Tools: Adobe, Microsoft, Sensity AI, and others offer deepfake detection software. 
      • Social media platforms are beginning to flag or remove malicious deepfakes. 

How has India Dealt with Deepfakes? 

  • India doesn’t have a dedicated law for deepfakes, but several existing laws offer partial protection. 
  • Information Technology Act, 2000 (“IT Act”): Section 66D of IT Act, targets impersonation and cheating via digital means. 
    • Further, Sections 67, 67A, and 67B of the IT Act can be used to prosecute individuals for publishing or transmitting deepfakes that are obscene or contain any sexually explicit acts. 
  • IT Rules, 2021: Require platforms to remove impersonation or morphed content when alerted quickly or lose their safe harbour’ protection (a provision that protects social media companies from regulatory liability for third-party content shared by users on their platforms). 
  • Copyright Act, 1957: The Copyright Act, 1957 can be applied if deepfakes use copyrighted images or videos without permission. It bars the unauthorised use of any content over which someone holds exclusive rights. 
  • Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In): Issued advisories on deepfake threats and measures that need to be followed to stay protected against. 
  • Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C): Assists law enforcement agencies in effectively tackling cybercrimes, including deepfakes. 
  • Judicial Interventions:  
    • Anil Kapoor’s Case (2023): Delhi High Court (HC) granted an ex-parte, omnibus injunction restraining from using Anil Kapoor’s name, image, or traits (like dialogue phrases) through AI or morphing for commercial gain. 
      • The court held that his personality rights (including name, likeness, and image) deserve protection not just for his own sake, but also for the dignity of his family and friends. 
    • Mr.Shivaji Rao Gaikwad (Rajnikanth) vs M/S.Varsha Productions (2015): The Madras High Court granted an injunction restraining the use of Rajnikanth’s name, image, caricature, and dialogue style in the film Main Hoon Rajnikanth, recognizing his personality rights as a celebrity.

Social Media Platforms Under Regulatory Scrutiny

07-07-2026

For Prelims: Copyright Act, 1957, Cinematograph Act, 1952, Article 21, Freedom of speech 

For Mains: Governance of Digital Platforms and Social Media, Data Protection, Privacy and Digital Rights

Source: IE 

Why in News?

The Indian government has taken stringent regulatory actions against major Social Media platforms Meta (WhatsApp and Instagram), Telegram and Signal over a spectrum of issues ranging from digital fraud and copyright infringement to the circulation of Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM).

Summary 

  • The government has intensified regulatory action against Meta (WhatsApp & Instagram) and Telegram over concerns related to digital fraud, online piracy, AI-generated harms, and Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM), highlighting growing scrutiny of digital platforms. 
  • The developments have reignited the debate on intermediary liability, algorithmic accountability, privacy versus traceability, and the need for a comprehensive, risk-based regulatory framework for digital platforms in India.

What are the Concerns Regarding the Growing Power and Functioning of Social Media Platforms? 

  • Rise in Digital Fraud and Impersonation: Proposed features such as WhatsApp usernames, which allow users to communicate without displaying mobile numbers, could facilitate phishing, name-squatting, identity theft, and digital arrest scams. 
    • MeitY has raised concerns over the rollout, while Meta maintains that phone numbers will remain linked at the backend and usernames of verified public figures and government bodies will be reserved. 
  • Anonymity vs Traceability: Privacy-enhancing features such as username-based communication and End-to-End Encryption (E2EE) can reduce public exposure of personal information but complicate law-enforcement investigations.  
    • They may also facilitate offences under Sections 66C (identity theft) and 66D (cheating by impersonation) of the IT Act, 2000. 
    • The government classified WhatsApp as a Significant Social Media Intermediary (SSMI) under the IT Act, 2000 and IT Rules, 2021, making it subject to due-diligence obligations.  
      • However, the Internet Freedom Foundation argued that Section 79 only provides conditional safe-harbour protection and does not empower MeitY to approve or withdraw platform features. 
  • Inadequacy of Reactive Anti-Piracy Enforcement: The government identified over 3,100 Telegram channels allegedly distributing pirated films and OTT content, demonstrating the limitations of a purely channel-by-channel notice-and-takedown approach, as infringing content can rapidly reappear. 
    • Copyright infringement is a criminal offense under the Copyright Act, 1957, and the Cinematograph Act, 1952. 
    • The government has asked Telegram to deploy systems to proactively detect, report, and disable access to pirated audio-visual content. The development reflects a shift from reactive content removal towards preventive due diligence by digital intermediaries.  
    • Telegram had become a “new dark web” used for cybercrime, fraud, and examination paper leaks. Earlier, the Centre had temporarily blocked the platform ahead of the NEET-UG 2026 re-examination under Section 69A of the IT Act, 2000. 
  • Safe Harbour Conundrum: Section 79 of the IT Act, 2000 grants intermediaries conditional immunity for third-party content.  
    • However, failure to observe due diligence under the IT Rules, 2021, particularly regarding persistent piracy and unlawful content, can result in the loss of safe harbour protection. 
  • Algorithmic Facilitation of Child Exploitation: Investigations alleging that paid advertisements on Instagram promoted access to Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM) and redirected users to Telegram channels expose serious vulnerabilities in automated advertisement approval and content-moderation systems. 
    • Despite Meta's stated zero-tolerance policy towards CSAM, sophisticated criminal networks can manipulate platform algorithms and moderation systems to distribute illegal content, highlighting the limitations of technology-driven moderation at scale. 
  • Online Child Safety and Human Rights: The circulation and commercialisation of CSAM/CSEAM violate children's dignity and fundamental rights and attract stringent provisions under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012 and the Information Technology Act, 2000 and undermine the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC). 
    • The government has directed Meta to disable such content and submit an Action Taken Report (ATR). 
  • Weaponisation of Synthetically Generated Information (SGI): AI-generated deepfakes, voice clones, Non-Consensual Intimate Imagery, and synthetic identities threaten individual dignity, electoral integrity, cybersecurity, and public trust. 
  • Algorithmic Amplification of Online Harms: Digital platforms increasingly act as active curators rather than passive intermediaries, as recommendation and advertising algorithms can amplify misinformation, fraudulent content, piracy, and harmful synthetic media.  
    • In some cases, viral misinformation and inflammatory content have contributed to communal violence and riots, prompting governments to temporarily block platforms or internet services, raising questions about the need for greater platform accountability and liability.  
  • Cross-Border Enforcement Challenges: Platforms such as WhatsApp, Telegram, and Instagram operate across multiple jurisdictions, complicating investigation, evidence collection, content removal, and prosecution of transnational digital crimes.

Judgments Regarding Digital Regulation & Social Media 

  • Shreya Singhal vs. Union of India, 2015: The Supreme Court (SC) upheld the constitutional validity of Section 69A, noting that it contains adequate procedural safeguards, including the requirement to record reasons in writing and the provision of a review committee to oversee the blocking orders. 
  • Tehseen Poonawalla vs. Union of India (2018): In response to mob lynchings fueled by fake news and WhatsApp forwards, the SC directed governments to take rigorous steps to stop the dissemination of irresponsible and explosive messages on social media that incite mob violence and lynching. 
  • Anuradha Bhasin vs. Union of India (2020): The SC ruled that the Freedom of Speech and Expression and the Freedom to Practice any Profession over the medium of the Internet is a constitutionally protected Fundamental Right under Article 19. 
  • Ajit Mohan (Facebook India) vs. Delhi Legislative Assembly (2021): The SC held that platforms like Facebook (Meta) have become "centers of concentrated power" and are no longer merely passive intermediaries.  
    • Their algorithms actively curate content, giving them the power to influence mass opinion. 

Cybersecurity Concepts in News 

  • BGP (Border Gateway Protocol): BGP is the fundamental routing protocol that makes the internet work. The internet is a network of networks (called Autonomous Systems or AS).  
    • BGP is the set of rules that determines the fastest and most efficient path for data packets to travel across these different networks to reach their destination. 
    • BGP lacks built-in trust verification, making it vulnerable to BGP hijacking, where attackers reroute or intercept internet traffic.  
  • RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure): It is a cryptographic security framework designed to secure BGP.  
    • If BGP is the internet's "postal service," RPKI is the "wax seal" that proves the sender’s identity. 
  • DNS Hijacking (Domain Name System Spoofing): The DNS is the "phonebook" of the internet, translating human-readable website names into numerical IP addresses.  
    • DNS Hijacking alters this phonebook to secretly redirect users from a legitimate website to a malicious, fake website. 
  • Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA): An IT security model based on the strict principle of "Never Trust, Always Verify."  
    • Traditionally, cybersecurity relied on a "castle-and-moat" model (trusting anyone inside the network). ZTA assumes the network is already compromised and requires strict identity verification for every person and device trying to access resources, regardless of whether they are inside or outside the network perimeter. 
  • Splinternet (Cyber Sovereignty): The concept of the global, open internet fragmenting into a series of divided, nationally or regionally controlled networks. Also known as "Balkanization of the Internet." 

What are the Key Challenges in Regulating Social Media Platforms? 

  • Defining the Scope of Intermediary Liability: Digital platforms increasingly curate, recommend, and monetise content through algorithms, blurring the distinction between a neutral intermediary under Section 79 of the IT Act, 2000 and an active publisher.  
    • Determining when safe harbour protection should cease remains a major legal challenge. 
  • Absence of Ex-Ante Regulation: India's regulatory framework is largely reactive (notice-and-takedown) rather than preventive (risk-based regulation). 
    • Despite government requirements for platforms to clearly label AI-generated and synthetically generated content, enforcement and detection remain challenging.  
    • Initiatives such as the PIB Fact Check Unit, which identifies fake and misleading information related to the Union Government, help counter misinformation but largely operate after such content has already circulated, highlighting the need for stronger preventive mechanisms to address systemic digital risks.  
  • Regulating Proprietary Algorithms: Recommendation engines, advertising systems, and content moderation tools operate as opaque "black-box" algorithms, limiting regulatory oversight, independent auditing, and accountability for harmful outcomes. 
  • Fragmented Regulatory Ecosystem: Digital platforms are simultaneously governed by the IT Act, 2000, DPDP Act, 2023, Copyright Act, 1957, POCSO Act, 2012, Competition Act, 2002, and sector-specific regulations, leading to overlapping jurisdiction. 
  • Balancing Fundamental Rights with Platform Regulation: Regulatory measures must strike a balance between privacy (Article 21), freedom of speech (Article 19(1)(a)), innovation, and legitimate state interests such as national security, copyright protection, and child safety. 
  • Institutional Capacity Deficit: Effective regulation increasingly requires expertise in AI governance, algorithm audits, digital forensics, cryptography, and platform engineering, capacities that regulatory institutions and enforcement agencies are still developing. 
  • Lack of a Comprehensive Digital Platform Law: Unlike the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) and Digital Markets Act (DMA), India currently relies on multiple fragmented statutes, resulting in regulatory uncertainty and inconsistent compliance obligations for digital platforms. 

What Measures are Needed to Strengthen Digital Platform Regulation in India? 

  • Statutory Clarity over Executive Discretion: India requires a dedicated, comprehensive AI and Digital Platforms regulatory framework (as envisioned in the proposed Digital Networking Platforms Bill, 2026) rather than relying on piecemeal amendments to the two-decade-old IT Act. 
  • Proportionality in Takedowns: Takedown mechanisms must adhere to the principles of natural justice.  
    • Users should be provided with reasoned orders and an opportunity to appeal, ensuring that artistic expression, satire, and legitimate dissent are protected. 
  • Algorithmic Audits: Regulatory bodies should mandate annual public-interest impact assessments for "Significant Digital Networking Platforms" to evaluate how their algorithms influence electoral integrity, public health, and social harmony. 
  • Develop a Comprehensive AI Governance Framework: Operationalise the IndiaAI Safety Institute and formulate standards for AI-generated content, deepfakes, synthetic media, and algorithmic transparency, complementing the IndiaAI Mission.  
  • Strengthen Traceability Through Lawful Processes: Establish judicially supervised traceability mechanisms for serious offences involving terrorism, child sexual exploitation, and organised cybercrime without weakening end-to-end encryption for ordinary users. 
  • Strengthen Child Online Protection: Mandate Safety-by-Design, age-appropriate platform design, mandatory CSAM detection technologies, and faster compliance with takedown requests in line with the POCSO Act, 2012 and IT Rules, 2021.  

Conclusion 

The controversies surrounding WhatsApp usernames, piracy on Telegram and CSAM on Meta platforms highlight a common regulatory challenge: how to preserve privacy and innovation while ensuring that digital platforms do not become safe spaces for impersonation, copyright infringement and child exploitation. India needs a proportionate, rights-based and technology-neutral regulatory framework that combines platform accountability, user safety, due process and effective enforcement.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is the 'Safe Harbour' protection under Section 79 of the IT Act, 2000?
Section 79 grants intermediaries conditional immunity from liability for third-party content, provided they observe due diligence under the IT Rules, 2021 and do not actively facilitate unlawful content. 

2. Why has the government raised concerns over WhatsApp's proposed username feature?
The feature may improve privacy by hiding phone numbers but could also increase impersonation, phishing, digital fraud, and traceability challenges for law enforcement. 

3. Why was Telegram directed to strengthen anti-piracy measures?
The government identified 3,100+ channels distributing pirated content and asked Telegram to adopt proactive detection and removal mechanisms, instead of relying solely on notice-and-takedown requests. 

4. Which laws govern online Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM) in India?
CSEAM is primarily regulated under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, the Information Technology Act, 2000, and the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021. 

5. Why is a comprehensive digital platform law considered necessary in India?
The existing framework is spread across multiple laws, creating regulatory overlaps and gaps. A dedicated law can provide clear intermediary liability, algorithmic accountability, AI governance, and platform-specific compliance obligations while safeguarding users' rights.
