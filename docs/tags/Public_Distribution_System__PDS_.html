Public Distribution System (PDS)

SARTHAK-PDS Scheme

28-05-2026

For Prelims: NFSA 2013, TPDS, One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC), e-PoS, Food Corporation of India (FCI), Fair Price Shops (FPS), Aadhaar Seeding, CCEA, Buffer Stock Norms 

For Mains: Food security in India, Technology in welfare delivery, Leakages in PDS, Direct Benefit Transfer, Inclusive growth, Governance reforms in subsidy delivery

Source:TH

Why in News? 

The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA), has approved the SARTHAK-Public Distribution System Scheme with an outlay of ₹25,530 crore for 2026–31 to modernize India’s Public Distribution System using technologies such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning, Blockchain, and Natural Language Processing, while strengthening the implementation of the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013

Summary

  • The SARTHAK-PDS Scheme (₹25,530 crore, 2026–31) aims to modernize India’s Public Distribution System using AI, ML, blockchain, and data-driven tools to strengthen NFSA implementation. 
  • It focuses on improving transparency, reducing leakages, enhancing last-mile delivery, and building a real-time, citizen-centric and interoperable PDS ecosystem. 
  • The scheme marks a shift towards a tech-enabled food security system, but its success will depend on balancing innovation with inclusion, accessibility, and federal coordination.

What is the SARTHAK-PDS Scheme? 

  • About: SARTHAK-PDS stands for “Scheme for Assistance in Ration Transport and Handling-Income with Automation in PDS.” 
    • It is an umbrella scheme aimed at strengthening India’s Public Distribution System (PDS) under the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013. 
    • It integrates two existing schemes: 
      • Assistance for intra-State movement and FPS dealer margin under NFSA 
      • SMART PDS (technology-driven reforms in PDS) 
  • Objectives: 
    • Technology-Driven PDS Reform: Introduce AI, ML, Blockchain and NLP for intelligent monitoring and analytics and enable real-time tracking of foodgrain movement and delivery. 
    • Reduction of Leakages: Improve transparency, reduce diversion of food grains and strengthen supply-chain monitoring and beneficiary authentication. 
    • Citizen-Centric Governance: Develop interoperable and unified PDS architecture. Enhance grievance redressal and beneficiary feedback systems. 
    • Strengthening Last-Mile Delivery: Provide financial assistance for intra-State transportation, handling of food grains and improve FPS dealer margins for smoother functioning of ration shops. 
    • Data-Driven Monitoring: Establish State Command and Control Centres and enable predictive analytics and real-time decision-making.

What is India’s Public Distribution System?

  • About: The Public Distribution System (PDS) is India’s largest food security mechanism through which subsidized food grains are distributed to eligible households via Fair Price Shops (FPS) established under the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution 
    • It operates under the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013 and ensures access to affordable food grains for vulnerable sections of society. 
  • National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013: It ensures equitable distribution of foodgrains to vulnerable populations. 
    • The Act legally entitles: 
      • Up to 75% of the rural population, and 
      • Up to 50% of the urban population 
      • to receive subsidized foodgrains through the Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS). 
    • Based on Census 2011, this covers around 81.35 crore beneficiaries. 
    • The Act follows a human life-cycle approach, ensuring access to adequate and nutritious food at affordable prices so that people can live with dignity. 
  • Categories of Beneficiaries under NFSA:  
    • Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY) Households: AAY households represent the “poorest of the poor” and are entitled to: 35 kg of foodgrains per household per month. 
      • Eligible Categories: Households headed by widows, elderly persons, disabled persons, or terminally ill persons without social support. 
        • Primitive tribal households. 
        • Landless agricultural labourers and marginal farmers. 
        • Rural artisans and slum dwellers. 
        • Informal sector workers dependent on daily wages. 
      • BPL families with HIV-positive members. 
    • Priority Households (PHH): Priority households are identified by State Governments/UT Administrations and are entitled to: 5 kg of foodgrains per person per month. 
    • Free Foodgrain Distribution under PMGKAY: To reduce the financial burden on poor households, the Government decided to provide free foodgrains under NFSA beginning from 1st January 2023.  
      • The scheme has been extended for five years from 1st January 2024 with an estimated financial outlay of ₹11.80 lakh crore, fully funded by the Central Government. 
  • Implementation Framework: The system operates under the joint responsibility of the Central and State Governments, making it one of the largest welfare distribution networks in the world. 
    • The Central Government, mainly through the Food Corporation of India (FCI), is responsible for the procurement, storage, transportation and bulk allocation of food grains to States and Union Territories. 
    • State Governments are responsible for operational activities such as Identification of beneficiaries, issuance of ration cards, intra-state distribution and monitoring of Fair Price Shops (FPSs). 
    • Several States and Union Territories also distribute additional mass-consumption items through PDS outlets, including pulses, edible oils, iodized salt and spices. 
  • Key Related Recent Developments:  
    • One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC): The One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) scheme enables NFSA beneficiaries to access subsidized foodgrains from any Fair Price Shop (FPS) across the country.  
      • It uses Aadhaar-based biometric authentication through ePoS devices, ensuring portability and transparency. 
    • Mera Ration 2.0: Mera Ration 2.0 enhances transparency and convenience for NFSA beneficiaries.  
      • The app provides real-time entitlement details, FPS location services, and withdrawal history tracking. It has recorded over 1 crore downloads. 
    • Anna Mitra App: The Anna Mitra App is designed to improve operational efficiency in the Public Distribution System (PDS).  
      • It helps officials and FPS dealers in stock tracking, inspection, compliance reporting, and beneficiary management. The app is currently operational in multiple States and Union Territories.

What are the Major Issues Associated with India’s Public Distribution System?

  • Leakages and Diversion: Foodgrain diversion and pilferage remain major concerns. Ghost beneficiaries and duplicate ration cards create inefficiencies.  
    • A November 2024 report stated that a staggering 28% of grains supplied through India's Public Distribution System (PDS) never reach their intended beneficiaries. 
  • Exclusion Errors: Genuine poor households are often excluded due to documentation issues. Migrants and homeless populations face access barriers.  
    • A 2022 report stated that more than 90 million eligible people have been excluded from legal entitlements under the Targeted Public Distribution System (TDPS) 
  • Poor Infrastructure: Storage losses, inadequate warehouses and transport inefficiencies persist. FPS infrastructure varies significantly across States. 
    • For instance, remote and hilly regions (e.g., tribal districts in Odisha and Jharkhand) face stock-outs due to transport bottlenecks, while urban shops in Delhi/Mumbai receive regular stock 
  • Fiscal Burden: India’s food subsidy expenditure remains very high, driven by rising costs of procurement, storage, and transportation. This steadily increases pressure on the government’s fiscal resources. 
  • Technological Challenges: Aadhaar-based authentication often fails, especially for elderly and remote beneficiaries. In rural areas, weak internet connectivity further disrupts e-PoS based transactions. 
  • Quality Concerns: Substandard quality of food grains undermines public trust in the PDS. Additionally, delays in transportation affect the timely and consistent distribution of rations.

What Measures can India Adopt to Strengthen Public Distribution System? 

  • Digital & Technological Upgrades 
    • AI-Driven Anomaly Detection: Utilize machine learning to analyze e-PoS transaction data in real-time, identifying suspicious patterns such as "ghost" beneficiaries or bulk withdrawals that indicate potential diversion. 
    • Improved Biometric Resilience: Introduce multi-modal authentication (e.g., iris scanning alongside fingerprints) for Fair Price Shop (FPS) transactions to reduce failure rates for elderly beneficiaries or manual laborers. 
  • Strengthening Last-Mile Delivery 
    • GPS-Enabled Transport Tracking: Mandate real-time GPS tracking for all vehicles transporting foodgrains from FCI depots to FPSs to prevent diversion during transit. 
    • Dynamic Ration Card Portability: Further promote the One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) by creating "Migrant Help Desks" at major transit hubs and industrial clusters to assist workers in accessing entitlements locally. 
  • Structural & Administrative Reforms 
    • Census Data Synchronization: Update beneficiary lists by linking the PDS database with the e-Shram portal and other socio-economic databases to ensure real-time inclusion of vulnerable populations who emerged post-2011. 
    • Decentralized Procurement (DCP) Expansion: Encourage more states to adopt the DCP model, which reduces transport costs, improves grain quality by sourcing local varieties, and supports local farm-level economies. 
    • Incentivizing FPS Dealers: Transition FPS dealers from mere ration distributors to service centers (e.g., providing banking services or utility payments) to ensure their financial viability and reduce the temptation for corrupt practices. 
  • Quality & Nutrition Assurance 
    • Universal Fortification Monitoring: Ensure rigorous quality testing at the blending stage for Fortified Rice Kernels (FRK) to guarantee the intended nutritional standards (Iron, Folic Acid, Vitamin B12) are maintained. 
    • Real-time Feedback Loops: Integrate the "Mera Ration 2.0" and "Anna Mitra" apps with a faster grievance redressal mechanism, ensuring that complaints about grain quality or dealer misconduct are resolved within a strictly monitored timeline. 
  • Fiscal & Policy Balancing 
    • Targeted Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT): Expand the optional cash transfer model in urban areas where market availability is high and infrastructure for digital banking is robust, reducing the physical handling and storage costs of grain. 
    • Strategic Buffer Management: Optimize the Central Pool reserves by more effectively utilizing the Open Market Sale Scheme (OMSS-D) to release excess stock during inflationary periods, thereby stabilizing market prices and reducing long-term storage losses.

Conclusion

The SARTHAK-PDS scheme marks a significant transition from a subsidy-based distribution framework to a technology-driven, intelligent food security architecture. By integrating AI, blockchain and interoperable digital systems, the scheme seeks to improve transparency, minimize leakages and strengthen last-mile delivery under the NFSA. However, balancing technological efficiency with inclusiveness, accessibility and federal coordination will remain critical to ensuring equitable food security for millions of beneficiaries.

Empowering Farmers for Sustainable and Inclusive Agriculture

15-06-2026

For Prelims: PM-KISAN, PMFBY, MSP, e-NAM, Kisan Credit Card, PMKSY, PM KUSUM, Pradhan Mantri Dhan-Dhaanya Krishi Yojana, Agricultural Infrastructure Fund (AIF), National Mission on Edible Oils, Crop Insurance, Soil Health Card, Digital Agriculture, Mega Food Parks 

For Mains: Agricultural Policy Reforms, Financial Inclusion and Social Security of Farmers, Market Access and Infrastructure Development, Technology-Driven and Sustainable Agriculture

Source: PIB 

Why in News? 

India’s agricultural sector has transformed significantly over the past 12 years, moving beyond welfare support toward productivity, income security, market access and resilient infrastructure. Schemes such as PM-KISAN, PMFBY, e-NAM and expanded MSP operations have strengthened income stability and market assurance for farmers. 

Summary 

  • Initiatives like PM-KISAN, PMFBY and MSP reforms have strengthened income stability, risk mitigation, and direct financial assistance for over 9 crore farmers. 
  • Investments in AIF, e-NAM, Mega Food Parks, and expanded Kisan Credit Card & Ground Level Credit have improved farm-to-market linkages, digital integration, and timely access to affordable credit. 
  • Farmer empowerment in India requires addressing income, climate, credit, infrastructure and technology gaps through stronger market access, risk mitigation, digital tools, sustainable practices, social security and value-added diversification.  

What is the Current Status of Agriculture in India? 

Growth and Economic Contribution 

  • The agriculture and allied sectors contribute around 18% of India’s Gross Value Added (GVA), making them a critical part of the economy. 
  • Over the past decade, the sector’s GVA increased from ₹20.9 lakh crore (2014–15) to ₹48.7 lakh crore (2023–24), reflecting a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 8.83% at current prices. 
  • Within agriculture, the crop segment GVA nearly doubled, rising from ₹12.92 lakh crore to ₹26.52 lakh crore, indicating sustained growth in primary crop production. 
  • Policy focus and public investment have been central drivers, with budgetary allocations rising from ₹27,663 crore in 2013–14 to ₹1,40,528.78 crore for 2026–27, reflecting significant investment in agricultural infrastructure, input support, and development programs. 

Crop Production and Food Security 

  • India’s total foodgrain production rose from 265.05 million tonnes in 2013–14 to 357.73 million tonnes in 2024–25, reflecting a significant increase in agricultural output. The growth was driven by higher production of rice, wheat, maize and coarse cereals, including millets, which are now promoted as Shree Anna. 
    • The National Food Security and Nutrition Mission supported this expansion by promoting improved seeds, better agronomic practices and technology adoption for rice, wheat, pulses and coarse cereals.  
  • India became the world’s largest rice producer, with rice production up 42.38% since 2014–15. Wheat production reached a record high of 117.94 million tonnes in 2024–25, while maize production nearly doubled, registering about a 79% increase during the same period.  
  • Oilseeds registered the highest production of 42.99 Mt (2024-25), which is 56% higher than 2014-15 (27.51 Mt), contributing to self-reliance and reducing edible oil import dependence from 63.2% (2015–16) to 56.25% (2023–24). 
  • Edible oil import dependence declined from 63.2 percent in 2015-16 to 56.25 percent in 2023-24, indicating gradual progress.  
  • The horticulture sector has become a major driver of agricultural growth, contributing nearly 37% of the crop sector’s Gross Value Output and reflecting a shift towards high-value crops driven by better farming practices and rising market demand.   

Farmer Welfare and Social Security 

  • Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN) provides ₹6,000 per year per eligible farmer family through Direct Benefit Transfer(DBT), reaching over 9.44 crore farmers. Over 25% of benefits have accrued to women beneficiaries, reflecting the scheme’s inclusive outreach across rural households.   
  • Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) provides farmers with a simple, affordable, and comprehensive crop insurance system. PMFBY covers the entire crop cycle, including pre-sowing and post-harvest losses.  
    • The scheme follows the principle of “One Nation, One Crop, One Premium”, ensuring uniform premium rates across the country.  
    • More than 4 crore farmers are insured annually under the scheme, reflecting its wide outreach and growing acceptance among cultivators.  
  • Pension and Social Security: Pradhan Mantri Kisan Maan-Dhan Yojana (PM-KMY)  provides a pension of ₹3,000 per month after age 60; about 24.95 lakh farmers enrolled as of Feb 2026. 
  • Minimum Support Price (MSP) and Procurement: The Minimum Support Price (MSP) acts as a safeguard by ensuring pre-determined prices for agricultural produce. MSPs are announced annually for 22 mandated crops. Since 2018-19, MSPs have been fixed at a minimum of 50 percent above the cost of production.  
    • Public procurement backed by MSP support expanded significantly, with the total MSP value during 2014–2026 being 3.5 times higher than during 2004–2014, reflecting stronger market support for farmers.

Agricultural Policy Reforms (2014-2026)  

Diversification into Allied Sectors 

  • Livestock: GVA increased from ₹5.10 lakh crore to ₹15.06 lakh crore during FY 2014–15 to FY 2026–27. India is the largest global milk producer, with production rising from 146.31 MT (2014–15) to 247.87 MT (2024–25). 
  • Poultry & Meat: Under the National Livestock Mission (NLM) and allied animal husbandry initiatives, egg production rose from 78.48 billion to 149.11 billion, while meat production increased from 6.69 MT to 10.50 MT. 
  • Fisheries & Aquaculture: Supported by the Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY), fisheries production increased from 9.58 MT to 19.78 MT, with 2,195 FFPOs formed and 4.39 lakh fishers covered under KCC. 
  • Bio-energy & Beekeeping: Under the National Beekeeping & Honey Mission (NBHM) and Ethanol Blended Petrol Programme, honey production rose from 0.081 MT to 0.152 MT, while ethanol procurement increased from 38 crore litres to 904 crore litres, achieving 20% blending. 

Technology, Digitalization, and Sustainability 

  • Digital Agriculture: Under the Digital Agriculture Mission, 7.63 crore Farmer IDs have been created and 23.5 crore crop plots digitized to improve planning, targeting, transparency and delivery of farmer-centric services. 
  • AgriStack and Digital Public Infrastructure: Initiatives such as AgriStack, digital crop surveys, Farmer Registry and geo-referenced land records are enabling better identification of beneficiaries, faster scheme delivery and data-driven agricultural planning. 
  • Kisan e-Mitra and AI-based Advisory: Digital advisory platforms such as Kisan e-Mitra are helping farmers access timely information on schemes, weather, crops, inputs, pest management and government support. 
  • Drone Technology in Agriculture: The Namo Drone Didi Scheme and drone-based agricultural services are promoting precision farming, crop monitoring, pesticide spraying and women-led agri-entrepreneurship. 
  • Renewable Energy: PM-KUSUM promotes solar pumps and renewable energy use in agriculture with 10 lakh standalone solar pumps installed and 13 lakh grid-connected pumps solarized. 
  • Soil Health & Organic Farming: 26 crore Soil Health Cards have been issued and 6.85 lakh seed villages established. Schemes such as PKVY (Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana), MOVCDNER (Mission Organic Value Chain Development for North Eastern Region) and NMNF (National Mission on Natural Farming) are promoting organic, natural and chemical-free farming across large areas. 
  • Climate-Resilient Farming: 2,996 climate-resilient crop varieties have been released, 448 villages promoted as climate-resilient models, and Integrated Farming Systems expanded to 1.04 lakh farm households. 
  • Water-Use Efficiency: Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY), especially the “Per Drop More Crop” component, is promoting micro-irrigation, efficient water use and climate-resilient agriculture. 
  • Seed and Crop Improvement: Initiatives under ICAR, seed village programmes and climate-resilient variety development are improving access to quality seeds, better yields and stress-tolerant crops. 
  • Extension Services: 1.27 crore farmers have been trained under Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) and ATMA programmes between 2021 and 2025, improving adoption of modern farming, pest management and sustainable agricultural practices. 

Credit, Financial Inclusion, and Institutional Support 

  • Kisan Credit Cards (KCCs) Scheme: The scheme provides a single window for farmers to meet their short-term and investment needs in agriculture and allied activities.  
  • Ground Level Credit (GLC) to agriculture rose from ₹7.30 lakh crore (2013–14) to ₹28.67 lakh crore (2024–25), improving access to working and investment capital. 
  • Cooperative Sector Reforms: 
    • Ministry of Cooperation strengthened Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS); 79,630 PACS computerised, 61,866 onboarded on ERP systems. 
    • 10,000 Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) registered by 2026, enabling collective marketing and better bargaining power. 
  • National Agriculture Market e-NAM: e-NAM is a pan-India digital trading platform that links agricultural markets to improve transparent price discovery, online trading and digital payments. So far, 1,656 mandis have been integrated, with 1.8 crore farmers and 4,724 FPOs registered. 
  • Agriculture Infrastructure Fund: AIF supports the creation of post-harvest infrastructure such as warehouses, cold chains, logistics and processing units to strengthen agricultural supply chains. So far, ₹84,202 crore has been sanctioned for 1.68 lakh projects, mobilising ₹1.33 lakh crore in total investment. 
  • Mega Food Parks: The Mega Food Parks scheme promotes integrated food-processing clusters with storage, processing, logistics and value-addition facilities. The number of parks increased from 2 in 2014 to 41 by 2025, helping reduce wastage and improve farmer-market linkages.  
  • Agricultural Exports: India’s agricultural exports increased from USD 37.29 billion in 2013–14 to USD 51.1 billion in 2024–25, reflecting stronger global demand and improved competitiveness in agri-trade. 
  • Value-Added Export Growth: The share of processed products rose from 13.7% to 20.4%, while seafood exports doubled from USD 3.64 billion to USD 7.52 billion, reaching over 130 countries and signalling a shift towards higher-value, quality-driven exports.  

What are the Key Challenges Affecting Farmers in India? 

  • Income and Market Risks: Farmers face price volatility and market uncertainty, especially for perishable and high-value crops. This risk is sharper for small and marginal farmers, who form about 86% of operational holdings but operate less than half of the agricultural area, limiting their marketable surplus and bargaining power.   
  • Limited MSP Reach: Although MSP and procurement have expanded, their benefits are concentrated mainly in crops like paddy and wheat and in better-procurement States such as Punjab, Haryana and parts of western Uttar Pradesh. Many farmers growing pulses, oilseeds, vegetables or fruits still sell in local or unorganised markets, often below remunerative prices.  
  • Climatic and Natural Risks: Agriculture is highly weather-dependent, with frequent exposure to droughts, floods, cyclones, and pest attacks. Extreme heat is emerging as a major threat to agriculture in South Asia, reducing labour productivity, stressing crops and livestock, and increasing the need for climate-resilient seeds, irrigation and early-warning systems. 
  • Limited Access to Credit & Financial Inclusion: Although KCCs and Ground Level Credit have expanded, access to timely and affordable loans is still limited in remote and marginalized regions. 
  • Resource and Input Constraints: Farmers face rising stress over water, soil health, quality seeds and balanced fertiliser use. For example, groundwater-dependent farming in northwestern India has boosted output but also created long-term sustainability concerns, while excessive urea use and inadequate micronutrient management affect soil productivity. 

What Measures are Needed to Empower India’s Farmers? 

  • Strengthening Income Support and Risk Mitigation: Expand schemes like PM-KISAN to provide direct income support and ensure timely disbursal through DBT. Broaden PMFBY coverage for crop insurance, including pre-sowing, standing crops, and post-harvest losses, with improved grievance redressal through portals like Krishi Rakshak Helpline. 
  • Expanding Credit and Financial Inclusion: Increase access to Kisan Credit Cards (KCCs) and Ground Level Credit for small, marginal, and remote farmers.  Encourage formal financial institutions to provide affordable loans for both cultivation and post-harvest activities. 
  • Developing Infrastructure and Post-Harvest Facilities: Expand Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF) projects for storage, cold chains, logistics, and processing at the farm gate. Promote Mega Food Parks and modern collection centers to reduce post-harvest losses and improve supply chain efficiency. 
  • Promoting Technology Adoption and Digital Tools: Strengthen digital initiatives like Kisan e-Mitra, Digital Agriculture Mission, and Drone-based programs to improve farm management and advisory services. Train farmers through KVKs and ATMA programs to adopt modern farming techniques, precision agriculture, pest management, and climate-resilient practices. 
  • Encouraging Sustainable and Climate-Resilient Practices:  Promote organic and natural farming through PKVY, NMNF, and climate-resilient crop varieties. Improve water-use efficiency via PMKSY and support renewable energy adoption through PM KUSUM (solar pumps and grid-connected solutions).

Conclusion 

India’s agricultural transformation over the past decade shows that sustainable farmer empowerment depends on a coordinated approach—where the Centre provides policy vision, financial backing, and technology, while States and local institutions adapt and implement reforms suited to regional realities—ensuring resilience, productivity, and enhanced income security for farmers.

Food Grain Storage in India

03-10-2025

Source: PIB

Why in News? 

India has achieved a record foodgrain production of 353.96 million tonnes for 2024-25, as per the Third Advance Estimates. To preserve this surplus, modern storage infrastructure is crucial to minimize post-harvest losses, ensure safe storage, and stabilize prices. 

What is a Foodgrain Storage System in India? 

  • About: The foodgrain storage system in India is a network of facilities and mechanisms designed to preserve harvested foodgrains, prevent post-harvest losses, and ensure year-round availability for consumers and the Public Distribution System (PDS).  
    • It integrates centralized, decentralized, and cold storage infrastructures, linking farmers, state agencies, and markets. 
    • Around 60-70% of the foodgrain produced is stored at the household level by small farmers, utilizing traditional indigenous storage methods like Morai and Mud Kothi. 

Storage System in India 

  • Government Storage Agencies: 
    • Food Corporation of India (FCI): Established in 1965 through an Act of Parliament, FCI is the primary agency responsible for the storage of food grains in India.  
      • It operates food storage depots nationwide, including silos, godowns, and Cover and Plinth (CAP) structures. 
      • Currently, FCI and State agencies manage 917.83 LMT capacity. 
    • Central Warehousing Corporation (CWC): Established under the Warehousing Corporations Act, 1962, the CWC manages the storage of agricultural produce and other notified commodities. 
    • State Warehousing Corporations: These are set up under respective state warehousing Acts to regulate the storage of certain goods within each state.

  • Private Agencies: 
    • FCI also rents storage capacity from private owners to meet storage needs. 
  • Other Stakeholders: 
    • Key contributors to grain management include the Warehouse Development and Regulatory Authority (WDRA), Railways, and the Civil Supply Departments of states. 

Importance of Foodgrain Storage 

  • Reduces Post-Harvest Losses: Proper storage preserves quantity and quality. 
  • Ensures Food Security: Maintains buffer stocks for PDS and national emergencies. 
  • Stabilizes Prices: Strategic storage prevents extreme price fluctuations. 
  • Supports Farmers’ Income: Enables farmers to sell at optimal times, avoiding distress sales. 
  • Strengthens Food Processing & Supply Chain: Provides raw material availability for industries and export potential.  

What Initiatives has India Taken to Enhance Food Grain Storage Infrastructure? 

  • Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF) was launched in 2020 to strengthen agricultural infrastructure across India.  
    • It is a medium-long term debt financing facility through interest subvention and credit guarantee support on loans for investment in viable projects for post-harvest management infrastructure and viable farming assets. 
  • Agricultural Marketing Infrastructure (AMI) scheme is a key component of the Integrated Scheme for Agricultural Marketing (ISAM).  
    • The objective of this scheme is to strengthen agricultural marketing infrastructure across rural India by providing financial assistance for the construction and renovation of godowns and warehouses. 
  • Pradhan Mantri Kisan SAMPADA Yojana (PMKSY)  is a comprehensive scheme designed to build modern infrastructure for the food processing sector, creating a smooth and efficient supply chain from the farm gate to retail. 
    • It helps farmers get better prices for their produce, reduces wastage, and supports the goal of doubling farmers’ income especially in rural areas, increases food processing levels, and boosts exports of processed food products. 
  • Schemes for Augmentation of Storage Capacity 
    • Construction of Steel Silos for Modernized Storage. 
    • Private Entrepreneurs Guarantee (PEG) scheme 
    • Central Sector Scheme “Storage & Godowns” (Focus on NE) 

What are the Key Challenges in Maintaining Grain Storage & Suggest Measures to Address Them? 

Challenges 

Measures to Enhance Grain Storage 

High Post-Harvest Losses: India loses about 22% of its foodgrain output annually (~74 million tonnes in 2022-23). 

Expand Scientific Storage: Scale up silos and modern warehouses (e.g., 50,000 MT silo in Bihar inaugurated in 2025). 

Storage-Specific Losses: Around 6.58% of foodgrains are lost due to poor storage (pests, rodents, moisture damage). 

Reduce Losses via Better Post-Harvest Practices: Curb cereal losses during drying, cleaning, and marketing by maintaining safe moisture levels. 

Economic Burden: Annual storage losses are estimated at worth ₹7,000 crore, with insects responsible for ~₹1,300 crore in losses. 

Encourage PPPs (Public-Private Partnerships): Strengthen the PEG Scheme by providing long-term hiring guarantees to attract private investors. 

Dependence on CAP Storage: Nearly 90% of wheat in Punjab is stored under Cover and Plinth (CAP), which is highly vulnerable. 

Empower PACS (Primary Agricultural Credit Societies): Fast-track the computerisation of ~63,000 PACS to benefit ~13 crore farmers and improve transparency. 

Focus on North-East, hilly, and tribal regions to address storage gaps through targeted schemes. 

Conclusion 

India’s journey from food deficit to food surplus has made scientific storage and distribution as critical as production itself. Strengthening storage through modern silos, PACS-based godowns, and cold chain infrastructure will not only reduce post-harvest losses but also ensure price stability, food security, and farmer welfare.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is foodgrain storage infrastructure in India?
It includes FCI godowns, PACS village storage, steel silos, and cold storage facilities. Its purpose is to preserve grains, reduce post-harvest losses, and ensure food security.

2. What are the Key types of storage systems?
India has centralized storage, decentralized storage, and cold storage for perishable commodities like fruits, vegetables, and dairy.

3. How do PACS contribute to storage?
PACS provide village-level storage near farms, helping farmers avoid distress sales, access better prices, and reduce transport costs for the PDS.
