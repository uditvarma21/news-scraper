Rising Digital Frauds and the Challenge of Ensuring Cyber Justice

30-09-2026

For Prelims: National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB), Social engineering, Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre,  Critical Information Infrastructure, Financial Fraud Risk Indicator (FRI) 

For Mains: Cybersecurity challenges arising from rapid digitalisation, Balancing digital innovation with cybersecurity and citizen protection

Source: TH 

Why in News?

According to data from the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB), cybercrime cases in India rose sharply by 17.9% from 2023 - 2024, moving against the broader national trend where overall registered crime fell by 6%.  

  • Concurrently, the Status of Policing in India Report (SPIR) 2026 highlights citizens’ vulnerability to cyber fraud and systemic challenges in securing justice for cyber fraud cases.

Summary

  • Cybercrime in India is rising rapidly, driven by social engineering, AI-enabled scams, digital arrests and financial fraud, while low conviction rates, weak digital forensics and jurisdictional challenges hinder effective justice. 
  • India needs a shift toward proactive cyber justice through stronger I4C coordination, FRI-based prevention, real-time fund freezing, digital literacy, faster FIR registration and accountable policing.

What is the Scale and Nature of Cyber Frauds in India? 

  • Unabated Surge in Digital Offences: According to the latest NCRB 2024 report, India recorded over 1.01 lakh cybercrime cases. 
    • The crime rate for digital offenses has escalated to 7.3 per lakh population. This highlights a definitive national transition from localized, physical offenses to sophisticated, borderless digital crimes. 
  • Low Conviction Rate: Despite the rising number of cybercrime cases, the cybercrime conviction rate remains below 18%, as per NCRB 2022, due to challenges in digital evidence collection, procedural lapses, and the transnational nature of cybercrimes. 

Nature and Modus Operandi of Cyber Frauds 

  • Social Engineering Over Technical Hacking: The contemporary nature of cyber fraud relies less on brute-force technical hacking and more on psychological manipulation (social engineering).  
    • Fraudsters exploit institutional credibility (impersonating police or bank officials), aspirational greed (high-return investment scams), and emotional distress. 
  • The "Tax on Connectivity": Financial fraud is no longer just a penalty for carelessness; it has evolved into a byproduct of deep digital integration.  
    • Official surveys indicate that cybercriminals systematically track digital footprints to target individuals with resources, making highly educated professionals and affluent demographics highly vulnerable to financial extortion. 
  • Emergence of Next-Generation Scams: The NCRB and Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) data point to a rise in complex typologies, including AI-enabled frauds, organized cyber syndicates, and "Digital Arrest" scams.  
    • Fraudsters are increasingly deploying AI-driven deepfakes, spoofing, and phishing campaigns to impersonate trusted sources and extract sensitive data. 
    • Criminals utilize Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) to mask locations and route stolen funds rapidly through layered "mule accounts," making asset recovery exceptionally difficult for law enforcement. 
  • Payment System Vulnerabilities: The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) is frequently targeted using compromised mobile numbers. 
  • Primary Motive: The overwhelming majority of cybercrimes registered under the Information Technology Act, 2000 are driven by the motive of financial gain through forgery, cheating, and fraud (FCF), followed by extortion and cyberstalking. 
    • Over 80% of cybercrime victims suffered financial losses, with 23% losing upwards of Rs 20,000.

India's Legal Framework for Cyberspace 

  • Information Technology Act, 2000: The foundational statute penalizing identity theft, impersonation, and the dissemination of harmful material.  
    • It empowers authorities to block malicious applications and designates the protection of critical infrastructure (Section 70A). 
  • IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021: Ensures accountability for social media intermediaries and digital marketplaces, mandating the swift removal of unlawful content. 
  • Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023: Places strict security obligations on data fiduciaries, mandating lawful data handling and explicit user consent to prevent unauthorized access. 
  • Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025: Promotes e-sports while imposing a complete ban on online money gaming, its promotion, and related financial transactions. 

Institutional Defence Mechanisms 

  • Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In): Monitors cyber threats, identifies vulnerabilities, and coordinates responses to cybersecurity incidents. 
  • Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C): Facilitates real-time information sharing and coordination among law-enforcement agencies to combat cybercrime. 
  • National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC): Protects Critical Information Infrastructure (CII) in sectors such as banking, telecom, power, and transportation. 

What are the Challenges in Securing Justice for Cybercrime? 

  • Procedural Attrition and Under-Reporting: The formal justice system suffers from a massive attrition rate before investigations even begin.  
    • A large plurality of cybercrime victims never approach law enforcement, and many abandon their cases due to complex procedural hurdles and a lack of faith in the police machinery. 
  • Institutionalised Rent-Seeking and Inequitable Justice: Procedural delays often push victims, particularly economically disadvantaged, rural and female victims, to seek police intervention through bribes or personal networks, allowing informal influence to override legal procedures and creating unequal access to financial recovery and justice.  
  • The "Golden Hour" Failure: Cyber syndicates rapidly siphon stolen capital through complex layers of synthetic or "mule" bank accounts.  
    • The lack of automated, legally binding, and real-time coordination between local police and commercial banks means that illicit funds are often withdrawn before bureaucratic freeze orders are executed. 
  • Jurisdictional Fragmentation vs. Borderless Crimes: Indian law enforcement remains structurally bound to rigid territorial jurisdictions.  
    • In contrast, cybercriminals operate trans-nationally using Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) and virtual private networks (VPNs), making inter-state police coordination exceptionally slow and cumbersome. 
  • Forensic Capacity Deficit at the Grassroots: While apex bodies like the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) possess advanced cyber capabilities, standard police stations lack the specialized digital forensics, blockchain tracking skills, and electronic evidence preservation mechanisms required to secure convictions under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023. 

What Measures are Needed to Ensure Accountable Cyber Justice? 

  • Enforceable FIR Registration: The registration of First Information Reports (FIRs) for cyber fraud must be made mandatory and time-bound to eliminate arbitrary police discretion. 
  • Proactive Telecom Intelligence: Expand the Financial Fraud Risk Indicator (FRI) to flag high-risk numbers and block suspicious transactions before fraud occurs.  
  • Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for Asset Freezing: Create institutionalized, real-time coordination protocols between the I4C, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), commercial banks, and telecom providers to freeze stolen funds within the "golden hour" without manual police intervention. 
    • Expanding the capacity of the 1930 Cybercrime Helpline and the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal to ensure seamless, automated transaction blocking. 
  • Targeted Digital Literacy: Awareness campaigns should pivot from generic warnings to targeted education on specific psychological manipulations used by fraudsters. 
    • Scale the Sahyog Portal for rapid removal of phishing links, malicious apps and fraudulent content. 
  • Anti-Corruption Safeguards within Cyber Cells: Implement internal vigilance audits and automated tracking of recovered funds to remove transactional corruption and protect vulnerable citizens seeking administrative redress. 

Conclusion 

India’s cybercrime challenge requires a shift from reactive investigation to proactive, technology-driven prevention and justice. A victim-centric and corruption-free cyber justice system can ensure that digitalisation strengthens trust rather than creating new vulnerabilities.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

I4C, under the Ministry of Home Affairs, facilitates coordination, information sharing and investigation among law-enforcement agencies to combat cybercrime. 

2. What is the Financial Fraud Risk Indicator (FRI)?
The FRI, developed by the Department of Telecommunications, classifies suspicious mobile numbers into Medium, High and Very High-risk categories to help prevent telecom and financial fraud. 

3. What is the significance of CFCFRMS and the 1930 helpline?
The Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System (CFCFRMS) and 1930 helpline enable rapid reporting and coordinated action to freeze fraudulent transactions and improve fund recovery. 

4. Which major laws govern India's cyberspace?
The key frameworks include the Information Technology Act, 2000, IT Rules, 2021, and Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, addressing cyber offences, intermediary accountability and personal-data protection. 

5. Why is cybercrime investigation difficult in India?
Major challenges include digital evidence preservation, limited grassroots forensic capacity, interstate jurisdictional barriers, mule accounts, transnational networks and rapidly evolving technologies.

Lottery Regulation in India

28-09-2026

For Prelims: Lottery regulation, Goods and Services Tax, Article 19, Futures & Options, SEBI 

For Mains: Regulation versus prohibition as a tool of public policy, Constitutional division of legislative powers over lotteries, betting and gambling.

Source: TH 

Why in News?

The debate over lottery regulation in India has gained renewed attention amid concerns that blanket prohibition may push gambling activity towards illegal lotteries, matka, satta and offshore betting platforms, while depriving States of revenue and legitimate vendors of livelihoods.  

  • The discussion highlights whether strict regulation rather than complete prohibition can better balance consumer protection, accountability and fiscal interests.

Summary 

  • India’s lottery debate centres on regulation versus prohibition: While lotteries can cause addiction, financial distress and socio-economic harms, blanket bans may push demand towards illegal lotteries, matka, satta and offshore platforms, while also reducing legitimate State revenue. 
  • A strictly regulated, transparent lottery framework with independent audits, consumer safeguards, inter-State coordination and dedicated welfare funding can balance public interest, accountability and revenue generation.

What is the Constitutional and Statutory Architecture Governing Lotteries? 

  • Seventh Schedule: 
    • Union List (Entry 40): Grants Parliament exclusive competence over "Lotteries organised by the Government of India or the Government of a State." 
    • State List (Entry 34): Vests competence over "Betting and gambling" within State legislatures. 
    • State List (Entry 62): Authorizes States to levy taxes on betting, gambling, and lotteries. 
  • Lotteries (Regulation) Act, 1998: The law defines a lottery as any scheme in which prizes are drawn by chance or lot among people who buy tickets. 
    • It lays down conditions for State-organised lotteries, including government-printed tickets and restrictions on certain lottery formats.  
    • It also allows States to prohibit the sale of lotteries from other States, while empowering the Union Government to prohibit lotteries violating the prescribed legal conditions. 
    • The Lotteries (Regulation) Rules, 2010 regulate online lotteries by requiring secure lottery software, banning hybrid paper-online formats and unauthorised online sales. States such as Punjab and Nagaland have also restricted online/WhatsApp-based lottery sales. 
  • Taxation: Lottery winnings are taxed at a flat 30% under the Income Tax Act, 1961, plus 4% cess, making the base effective rate 31.2%.  
    • Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) is deducted before payout, and winnings are reported under “Income from Other Sources” without standard deductions. 

Judicial Jurisprudence 

  • Taxation Framework under GST: The Supreme Court of India in Skill Lotto Solutions Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India (2020) ruled that lotteries, betting, and gambling are actionable claims that legally qualify as "goods" under the Goods and Services Tax (GST) law. 
  • The Res Extra Commercium Doctrine: In State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala (1957), the Supreme Court ruled that gambling activities are res extra commercium (activities outside commerce and trade).  
    • Consequently, lottery operators cannot claim fundamental rights protection under Article 19(1)(g) (freedom to practice any trade or business) or Article 301 (freedom of trade and commerce across territories). 
  • The "All-or-Nothing" Dilemma (B.R. Enterprises v. State of U.P., 1999): The Supreme Court interpreted Section 5 of the 1998 Act to hold that a State cannot selectively prohibit lotteries originating from other States while running its own. 
    • A State can ban outside lotteries only if it becomes completely lottery-free by abandoning its own state-run lotteries. 
  • The Regulatory Fallout: Because destination States possess zero administrative machinery to audit or regulate how outside States’ private marketing agents conduct draws, major States like Tamil Nadu (2003) and Karnataka (2007) opted for complete prohibition to block predatory external cartels, thereby sacrificing their own potential state revenues and welfare surpluses.  
    • As of 2023, only nine States (e.g., Kerala, Maharashtra, Goa, Punjab, West Bengal) operate lotteries.

What are the Key Concerns Regarding Lottery Regulation in India?

  • Regressive Socio-Economic Burden: Lotteries disproportionately attract low-income households. Rapid-draw formats, instant-win games, and misleading jackpot marketing foster compulsive gambling, loss-chasing, and chronic household debt. 
  • Expansion of the Underground Shadow Economy: Blanket prohibitions fail to eliminate inelastic demand.  
    • Banning state lotteries drives players to illegal markets such as matka, satta, single-digit rackets, and offshore digital betting, which operate without age checks, financial audits, or fraud safeguards. 
  • Loss of Public Revenue and Welfare Funding: Blanket prohibition can eliminate significant lottery revenue and GST collections.  
    • From September 2025, lottery, betting, gambling and related actionable claims attract 40% GST.  
  • Destruction of Marginalized Livelihoods: Legal paper lottery distribution provides decentralized employment to lakhs of vulnerable individuals, including persons with disabilities, widows, and low-income vendors, whose livelihoods vanish under sudden bans. 
  • Class Asymmetry in State Paternalism: Critics highlight a socio-economic asymmetry in the regulation of financial risk. Affluent citizens can legally participate in high-risk activities such as intraday trading, Futures & Options (F&O), and crypto-asset trading, primarily through disclosure and regulatory safeguards rather than competence tests.  
    • Yet, lotteries, often more accessible to lower-income groups, face stricter restrictions and, in some States, outright prohibition.  
    • SEBI data showing that around 9 out of 10 individual F&O traders incur net losses further fuels the debate over whether policy should prioritise risk regulation and consumer protection over blanket prohibition. 
  • Advertising Standards: The Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) is a non-statutory, self-regulatory body, so its advertising code does not have the same legal force as statutory regulations.  
    • This can create enforcement gaps and limited accountability in regulating lottery advertisements. 

International and Domestic Models on Lottery Regulation 

  • Global Standard of "Controlled Legality": Nearly 80% of nations permit lotteries under regulated oversight rather than prohibition. Prohibitions exist primarily in closed ideological regimes or strict Sharia jurisdictions. 
  • The Public-Operator Model: Globally, over 70% of jurisdictions utilize a state-owned or statutory corporation to directly run operations, hiring private contractors strictly for logistics and IT.  
    • This prevents "concessionaire capture", where private marketing agents divert profits. 
  • Multi-Jurisdictional Pooling: Federal democracies (e.g., the US, Canada, Germany) allow states to voluntarily pool prizes, technical platforms, and regulatory compliance through inter-state pacts while retaining destination-state sovereignty. 
  • The Kerala Template: 
    • Direct Public Oversight: Kerala conducts lottery draws departmentally, eliminating intermediary concessionaires and predatory private syndicates. 
    • Revenue Hypothecation: In FY 2023–24, Kerala generated over Rs 2,880 crore in gross proceeds, which is used to fund the Karunya Benevolent Fund for healthcare subsidies. 
    • Social Welfare Distribution: Distribution rights are reserved for registered street vendors and persons with disabilities, turning the system into a decentralized social security mechanism. 

What Measures are Needed for an Accountable Regulatory Regime? 

  • Ending the All-or-Nothing Rule: Parliament must amend Section 5 of the Lotteries (Regulation) Act, 1998, to clarify that a State may exclude outside lotteries regardless of whether it runs its own lottery, provided the exclusion is non-discriminatory (excluding all external operators uniformly). 
    • This removes the perverse incentive for States to shut down safe, transparent domestic lotteries simply to protect themselves from opaque external operators. 
  • Structural Separation of Operations and Oversight: Establish an Independent Statutory Lottery Authority in organizing States.  
    • The administrative department organizing the draw must not act as its own auditor; draw software, Random Number Generators (RNG), and ball-draw machines must undergo mandatory third-party cryptographic audits. 
  • Mandatory Consumer Safeguards: Implement daily/monthly purchase caps per individual to curb loss-chasing behaviour. 
    • Strictly prohibit credit-based sales and predatory marketing that misleads consumers on mathematical odds. 
    • Ban rapid-draw and instant scratch games that trigger addictive dopaminergic feedback loops. 
  • Unified Digital Enforcement: Create a specialized task force under the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) and the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) to systematically block illegal offshore betting apps and freeze domestic payment gateways facilitating untaxed gambling. 

Conclusion 

India needs to move beyond the binary choice of prohibition versus unrestricted gambling. A strictly regulated lottery framework, with transparent operations, consumer safeguards, responsible advertising and dedicated revenue for healthcare and social welfare, can address gambling-related harms while preventing the growth of illegal markets.

Rising Cyber Frauds in India

19-07-2025

For Prelims: Cybercrime, Digital arrest, Information Technology Act, Digital Personal Data Protection Act, Indian Computer Emergency Response Team, National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre, Cyber Swachhta Kendra, Ransomware attack, Budapest Convention on Cybercrime. 

For Mains: Current Framework for Cyber Security in India, Key Emerging Cyber Threats Affecting India's Digital Landscape. 

Source: IE 

Why in News? 

The Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C), a unit under the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA), has reported a sharp surge in financial cyber frauds targeting Indian citizens, primarily originating from Southeast Asian countries. 

What is the State of Financial Cyber Frauds in India as per I4C Analysis? 

  • Rising Financial Losses: In the first half of 2025, India lost an average of Rs 1,000 crore per month to cyber frauds, totaling Rs 7,000 crore.  
    • According to the I4C,the projected annual loss may exceed Rs 1.2 lakh crore (Rs 1.2 trillion) in 2025, amounting to 0.7% of India’s GDP. 
  • Origin & Nature of Scams: Over 50% of cyber frauds targeting Indians originated from Southeast Asian countries such as Cambodia, Myanmar, Vietnam, Laos, and Thailand, operated from high-security compounds allegedly run by Chinese handlers.  
    • These primarily involve stock trading/investment scams, digital arrest scams & task-based and investment-based scams. 
    • Indian intelligence has identified 45 scam centres in Cambodia, 5 in Laos, and 1 in Myanmar. 
  • Modus Operandi: Victims, including Indians, are being trafficked via fake job offers and routed through countries like Dubai, China, and Thailand to operate cyber scams.  
    • Recruitment agents are active across Indian states (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, J&K, Uttar Pradesh, and Delhi).  
  • Systemic Gaps & Enforcement Action: India’s cyber fraud ecosystem is exploited due to loopholes in digital banking transactions, issuance of ghost SIM cards by PoS agents in the telecom sector, and weak verification processes in immigration, enabling anonymous and cross-border cybercrimes. 

Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) 

  • About: The Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) was launched by the Ministry of Home Affairs in 2020 to provide a comprehensive and coordinated response to cybercrime, including financial frauds. 
  • Key Objectives: 
    • Act as the national nodal agency to monitor, prevent, and investigate cybercrimes, especially those targeting women, children, and critical infrastructure. 
    • Provide an early warning system and facilitate trend analysis, pattern recognition, and data sharing among law enforcement agencies. 
    • Enable easy reporting of cybercrime and promote public awareness on cyber hygiene and fraud prevention. 
    • Assist States/UTs in building capacity of police, prosecutors, and judicial officers in areas like cyber forensics and investigation. 
  • National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal: A citizen-centric platform under I4C that enables individuals to report cyber frauds online. Reported complaints are forwarded to the relevant law enforcement agencies for necessary legal action. 

What are Cyber Frauds? 

  • About: Cyber frauds are criminal activities conducted using digital technology (internet) to deceive individuals or organizations for financial gain.  
    • It exploits vulnerabilities in cybersecurity systems, digital platforms, or human behavior to steal money, data, or identities. 
  • Types of Cyberfrauds:  

Cyber Fraud/ Threats 

Description 

Digital Arrests 

Impersonating authorities (police or income tax officers) to extort money. 

Online Job/Task-Based Scams 

Fake work-from-home offers with upfront payments. 

Malware 

Malware is used to steal personal information that allows cyber criminals to gain control of a victim's computer. 

Ransomware 

Ransomware encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for decryption. E.g., WannaCry attack in 2016 

Phishing 

Phishing involves emails that appear to be from trusted sources, tricking users into clicking links that lead to fake websites and attackers gaining sensitive details e.g., credit card numbers.  

Cyberbullying 

Cyberbullying includes any threat to a person’s safety, coercion to say or do anything. 

Cyber Spying 

Cyber Spying targets a public or private entity’s network to gain access to classified data, private information, or intellectual property. 

Business Email Compromise (BEC)  

Scammers hack legitimate email accounts to impersonate suppliers, employees, or tax office members, considered a white-collar crime. 

Dating Hoodwinks 

Hackers use dating websites, chat rooms, and online dating apps to pose as potential partners and gain access to personal data.  

ATM/PoS Frauds 

Skimming card details or unauthorized transactions. 
  • Consequences of Cyber Fraud: 
    • Individuals face unauthorised financial transactions, loss of account access, and misuse of personal data for harassment or blackmail. 
    • Businesses risk legal penalties, regulatory fines, and loss of market value due to compromised client data. 
    • Governments face threats to national security as cyber breaches target defence and critical information systems. 
  • Notable Cyberfraud Incidents: 
    • Aadhaar Data Breach (2018): Personal data of 1.1 billion Aadhaar cardholders was compromised, including  Aadhar numbers, PAN and bank details. 
    • Canara Bank ATM Attack (2018): Hackers used skimming devices on 300 debit cards, leading to a theft of over Rs 20 lakh. 
    • Pegasus Spyware Case: The Israeli spyware Pegasus was allegedly used to access data from mobile devices without consent, affecting over 300 verified Indian phone numbers.

What are the Key Initiatives Related to Cybersecurity? 

  • Global Initiatives on Cybersecurity: 
    • Budapest Convention on Cybercrime: It is the first international treaty to address cybercrime through legal harmonization, investigative cooperation, and capacity building. It came into force on 1st July 2004. 
      • India is not a signatory of the Budapest Convention. 
    • Internet Governance Forum (IGF): IGF is a multi-stakeholder platform under the UN that facilitates dialogue among governments, private sector, academia, and civil society on public policy issues related to Internet governance and cybersecurity. 
    • UNGA Resolutions on ICT Security: The United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) has established 2 key platforms for addressing cybersecurity: 
      • Open-ended Working Group (OEWG), initiated by Russia, focuses on inclusive dialogue and capacity building in ICT security. 
      • Group of Governmental Experts (GGE), initiated by the USA, works on developing norms of responsible state behaviour in cyberspace and international legal frameworks. 
  • Indian Initiatives: 
    • Legislative Measures:   
      • Information Technology Act, 2000 (IT Act)  
      • Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023  
    • Institutional Framework:  
      • Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In)  
      • National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC)  
      • Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C)  
      • Cyber Swachhta Kendra  
      • Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System for real-time monitoring, tracking, and resolution of cyber fraud cases. 
        • The Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) registered FIRs against PoS agents for issuing fraudulent SIMs.  
    • Strategic Initiatives:   
      • Bharat National Cybersecurity Exercise 2024  
      • National Cyber Security Policy, 2013: Provides vision and strategies for securing cyberspace and protecting critical information infrastructure. 
      • Chakshu & Digital Intelligence Platform by DoT: 
        • Chakshu, a tool on Sanchar Saathi portal to report fraudulent calls, SMS, or WhatsApp messages related to KYC expiry or bank account updates. 
        • Digital Intelligence Platform to enable real-time coordination to tackle cyber frauds. 
    • Sector-Specific Regulations:  
      • Cybersecurity Framework for SEBI Regulated Entities: Mandates cybersecurity policies for securities markets.  
      • Telecommunications (Critical Telecommunication Infrastructure) Rules, 2024  

 

What Measures Should be Taken to Strengthen Cybersecurity Frameworks in India? 

  • Infrastructure & AI-Based Security: Strengthen digital infrastructure through firewalls, regular software/hardware updates, and AI-driven threat detection systems to proactively identify, predict, and neutralize cyber threats.  
    • AI tools must support ransomware prediction, incident response, and forensic analysis. 
  • Cyber Awareness & Literacy: Launch nationwide cyber literacy programs in regional languages targeting rural communities, youth, and senior citizens.  
    • Incorporate cybersecurity education in schools and universities to build digital resilience from an early age, supported by secure infrastructure and staff training. 
  • Institutional & Audit Reforms: Conduct mandatory cybersecurity audits in critical sectors like banking, healthcare, and utilities, including stress tests and employee preparedness.  
    • Establish district-level cybersecurity units for localized threat management and coordination with CERT-In. 
  • Corporate & Banking Safeguards: Enforce two-factor authentication (2FA), data encryption, and monitoring systems in businesses and banks.  
    • Financial institutions must track suspicious transactions, detect foreign IP logins, and prevent conversion of stolen funds into cryptocurrency. 
  • Personal Cyber Hygiene: Encourage citizens to adopt secure digital practices, such as avoiding suspicious communications, using strong, unique passwords, and not bypassing security warnings to reduce individual vulnerability to cyber fraud. 

Conclusion 

The rise of financial cyber frauds in India highlights the urgent need for strengthened cybersecurity measures and enhanced public awareness. While initiatives play a crucial role in combating cyber threats, the evolving nature of cybercrimes demands continuous adaptation of legal, technological, and institutional frameworks. By addressing systemic gaps and fostering digital literacy, India can mitigate the risks associated with cyber fraud and ensure a safer digital ecosystem for its citizens.

Supreme Court on Electoral Black Money

18-08-2026

For Prelims: Election Commission of India, Rule of law, Article 326, Right to Equality, Electoral Bonds Scheme, ECI’s cVIGIL app 

For Mains: Black money and electoral integrity, Free and fair elections, Political funding reforms, Ethical electoral conduct

Source: TH 

Why in News?

The Supreme Court observed that removing the influence of black money from elections is a responsibility of the Election Commission of India (ECI).  

  • The Court held that the use of ill-gotten money can distort the free choice of voters and undermine democracy, rule of law and the electoral process. It also issued directions for time-bound investigation, reporting of seizures and speedy trials in election-related black money cases.

Summary 

  • The Supreme Court has placed greater responsibility on the ECI to curb black money in elections, directing time-bound investigations, seizure reporting, quarterly monitoring and speedy trials to protect free and fair elections. 
  • Electoral black money undermines voter autonomy, equality, transparency and democratic accountability. Stronger political-funding transparency, expenditure regulation, financial intelligence, state funding and judicial fast-tracking are needed to address the problem.

What are the Key Directives Issued by the Supreme Court? 

  • Seizure Reporting: Seized cash or assets must be reported within 24 hours to the District Magistrate, Additional District Magistrate or competent court, along with reasons showing a prima facie link with the suspected electoral offence. 
  • Time-Bound Investigation: Investigating Officers should complete election-related FIR investigations within one year.  Any delay must be justified and reported to the ECI. 
    • Investigating Officers must submit quarterly status reports on such cases to the ECI. 
  • Income Tax Coordination: Static Surveillance Teams detecting cash above Rs 10 lakh must forward the information to Income Tax authorities. 
  • Speedy Trials: High Courts must ensure expeditious disposal of election-related black money cases, including through designated courts where required. 
  • Withdrawal of Cases: Withdrawal of criminal cases against candidates relating to an election cycle requires approval of the concerned High Court. 
  • Compliance: The ECI and State Governments have been directed to submit compliance reports by 18th November 2026. 

Why is Black Money Considered a Threat to Electoral Democracy? 

  • Subversion of Constitutional Rights: Democracy functions on the uncoerced and informed consent of the electorate. When voters are influenced by illicit monetary gratification, the choice exercised is clouded.  
    • This vitiates the core philosophy of Universal Adult Suffrage (Article 326), effectively thrusting external choices upon the electorate and turning democratic rights into market commodities. 
    • The essence of uncoerced choice was upheld in the NOTA Judgment (PUCL v. Union of India, 2013), where the Court ruled that the right to register a negative vote is vital to ensure that elections reflect the true democratic will, free from external monetary inducements. 
  • Destruction of the Level Playing Field (Article 14): Free and fair elections require a level playing field. Unaccounted wealth can create barriers for grassroots and resource-poor candidates, favouring plutocracy and weakening the Right to Equality. 
    • In Kanwar Lal Gupta v. Amar Nath Chawla (1974), the Supreme Court recognised that financial disparity between candidates can distort electoral competition and have anti-democratic effects. 
    • In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), the Court held that free and fair elections are part of the Constitution’s Basic Structure. 
  • Policy Capture and Crony Capitalism: Illicit electoral funding is a high-return investment. Candidates and parties funded by black money are structurally bound to return favors post-election through a quid pro quo arrangement.  
    • This leads to regulatory forbearance, biased public procurement, and the drafting of public policies that prioritize specific private financiers over overarching public welfare. 
    • In the Electoral Bonds Case (ADR v. Union of India, 2024), the Supreme Court struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme as unconstitutional, holding that anonymous political funding undermined citizens’ Right to Information under Article 19(1)(a) and could facilitate quid pro quo arrangements.  
  • Perpetuation of the Criminal-Political Nexus: As highlighted by the Vohra Committee (1993), the logistical distribution of massive amounts of undeclared cash often requires reliance on local criminal syndicates and strongmen.  
    • This operational necessity provides validation to criminal networks, seamlessly integrating organized crime into the mainstream political machinery. 
    • In Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India (2018), the Supreme Court observed that the entry of persons with criminal backgrounds can pollute the electoral process and emphasised the need to decriminalise politics and strengthen electoral transparency.

"Black Money" in the Electoral Context  

  • Black Money in elections refers to unaccounted and untaxed funds entering the electoral process outside formal and auditable channels.  
  • It may involve cash-for-vote inducements, liquor and gifts, under-reporting of candidate expenditure, and the use of hawala channels or shell/letterhead political parties to route illicit funds.

Legal and Institutional Framework to Curb Black Money in Elections 

  • Bribery as a Corrupt Practice: Section 123(1) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 treats bribery as a corrupt practice in elections. 
  • Candidate Expenditure Limits: The Conduct of Election Rules, 1961, framed under the RPA, provide the framework for prescribed expenditure limits for candidates. 
  • Political Party Expenditure Disclosure: Following Common Cause v. Union of India (1996), political parties are required to maintain and submit election-related financial statements. 
  • Candidate Disclosure: In Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002), the Supreme Court recognised voters’ right to know candidates’ criminal records, assets, liabilities and qualifications. 
  • Field-Level Monitoring: The ECI uses Expenditure Observers, Static Surveillance Teams (SSTs), Flying Squads and Video Surveillance Teams to monitor election expenditure and detect inducements. 
  • Citizen Reporting: The ECI’s cVIGIL app enables citizens to report Model Code of Conduct violations and voter inducements in real time. 
  • Financial Investigation: The Income Tax Act, 2025 and Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002 provide mechanisms to investigate unaccounted money and proceeds of crime. 

What are the Persisting Challenges in Curbing Black Money in Elections? 

  • Expenditure Loophole: Section 77 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 caps a candidate’s election expenditure, but the statutory ceiling is often considered inadequate given the actual cost of campaigning.  
    • Moreover, there is no equivalent overall ceiling on political-party expenditure, allowing funds to be routed through parties and creating scope for indirect spending.
  • Anonymous Small Donations: Under Section 29C of the RPA, 1951, political parties are not required to disclose the identity of donors contributing below Rs 20,000, creating scope for splitting larger contributions into smaller amounts to obscure their source. 
  • Limited ECI Enforcement Capacity: Although Article 324 gives the ECI broad powers to supervise elections, it relies heavily on State police, district administration, Static Surveillance Teams and Flying Squads for field-level enforcement and lacks an independent financial-forensics wing. 
  • Inter-Agency Coordination: Tracking hawala transactions, shell companies and complex financial flows requires coordination with agencies such as the Income Tax Department and Enforcement Directorate, which can complicate timely investigation. 
  • MCC Enforcement Gap: The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) is largely non-statutory, limiting the ECI’s ability to impose direct statutory penalties for certain financial violations. 
  • Cash-Based Economy: High reliance on cash, particularly in informal and rural economies, makes it difficult to establish the source of seized money and its direct connection with a specific electoral offence. 
  • Last-Mile Inducements: Cash, liquor and gifts are often distributed 24–48 hours before polling, making detection difficult despite surveillance mechanisms. 
  • Detection–Conviction Gap: Large-scale seizures and FIRs do not always translate into convictions, weakening the deterrent effect of the Representation of the People Act and related financial laws. 

What Measures are Needed to Ensure the Integrity of the Electoral Process? 

  • Statutory Capping of Party Expenditure: The legislative framework must be amended to introduce a rational, enforceable cap on the overall election expenditure of political parties, proportional to the number of seats they contest. 
    • Empower the ECI to deregister non-serious/shell parties - a long-pending reform demand. 
  • Complete Digitization of Political Funding: Cash donations to political parties must be entirely phased out.  
    • All contributions, regardless of the denomination, must be routed through traceable digital banking mechanisms, ensuring complete auditability by independent bodies like the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG). 
  • Empowering ECI with Financial Intelligence: The Election Commission must be equipped with an autonomous financial enforcement wing.  
    • Integrating real-time data from the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) and the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) would allow the deployment of big data analytics to track sudden, unnatural spikes in regional liquidity during election seasons. 
  • Partial State Funding of Elections: As recommended by the Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998), India should explore a phased transition toward state funding of elections in kind (e.g., providing free logistical support, digital infrastructure, and designated airtime) to systematically break the reliance on private and informal funding networks. 
  • Judicial Fast-Tracking and Decriminalisation: Establish specialised fast-track courts for electoral financial offences and enforce the Supreme Court’s one-year timeline for investigations to strengthen deterrence.  
    • Stronger disqualification provisions for candidates convicted of serious electoral and organised-crime offences can help weaken the criminal-political nexus. 

Committees and Commissions on Electoral Reform 

  • Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990): Focused extensively on curbing money power and recommended state support to candidates in kind (e.g., fuel, voter slips). 
  • Vohra Committee (1993): Officially documented the deeply entrenched nexus between crime syndicates, politicians, and the bureaucracy in managing election logistics. 
  • Law Commission — 170th Report (1999): Suggested a comprehensive overhaul of electoral laws to enforce internal party democracy and financial transparency. 
  • Law Commission — 255th Report (2015): Focused specifically on "Electoral Reforms," detailing the complete cash-laundering chain in elections and recommending stringent financial audits for parties. 

Conclusion 

The Supreme Court has strengthened the institutional enforcement of free and fair elections by placing responsibility on the ECI and requiring time-bound coordination among investigating agencies and High Courts. Rather than creating new offences, the judgment strengthens detection, investigation and judicial oversight under the existing RPA, 1951, reinforcing the broader push for electoral transparency and accountability.

FATF Report on Underground Banking and Digital Hawala

09-09-2026

For Prelims: Financial Action Task Force, Shell companies, Unified Payments Interface, Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) 1999, Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) 2002, Financial Intelligence Unit 

For Mains: Digital Hawala: Impact of fintech, virtual assets, stablecoins and encrypted platforms on financial crime, Financial Inclusion vs Financial Surveillance: Preventing de-risking while tackling illicit finance.

Source: TH 

Why in News?

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) released a report titled "Investigating Professional Money Laundering, Underground Banking, and the Use of Hawala and Other Similar Service Providers". The global watchdog highlighted how the centuries-old traditional underground banking system is transforming into a tech-enabled network.  

  • The fusion of virtual assets with the traditional "hawala" system has given rise to ‘digital hawala’, which is increasingly being exploited for money laundering and financing terrorist organisations.

Summary 

  • FATF highlights the global expansion of underground banking and HOSSPs, with the rise of digital hawala using fintech, virtual assets and encrypted platforms, making money laundering and terrorist financing harder to detect. 
  • India faces challenges from trade-based laundering, crypto layering and online gambling–hawala networks, requiring stronger inter-agency coordination, technology-driven detection, international cooperation and accessible formal remittance channels.

What are the Key Findings of the FATF Report? 

  • Widespread Scale and High-Risk Status: Nearly 90% of jurisdictions reported the presence of underground banking and Hawala and Other Similar Service Providers (HOSSPs). 
    • Over 80% identified these networks as major channels for professional money laundering (PML). 
    • Over 60% of respondents flagged these systems as high-risk in their National Risk Assessments (NRAs). 
    • Misuse is most frequently reported in Asia, Western Europe, and West Africa, particularly along corridors with strong remittance flows and diaspora networks. 
  • Professionalisation of Money Laundering: PML is increasingly operating as a specialized service, with networks offering illicit financial intermediation for a fee. 
    • These networks are evolving into structured, business-like infrastructures featuring hierarchical models, digitized ledgers, and specialized roles. 
    • They increasingly involve professional facilitators such as lawyers, accountants, notaries, real-estate agents, and casino operators. 
  • The Rise of 'Digital Hawala': Nearly 70% of jurisdictions report the growing use of technology to coordinate and settle transactions, transforming traditional systems into "digital hawala". 
    • Criminals leverage encrypted apps (WhatsApp, Telegram), virtual assets, stablecoins, and AI-based tools for automated transaction structuring and mule-account sweeping. 
    • This digital shift enables faster cross-border transfers and greater anonymity, making it significantly harder to trace financial flows and secure evidence. 
  • Integration with the Formal Sector and Diversification: PML actors combine informal transfers with formal financial systems, using bank accounts, fintech platforms, virtual International Bank Account Numbers (IBANs), and prepaid cards as entry and exit points to exploit regulatory blind spots. 
    • Underground banking acts as a "hidden settlement layer" behind seemingly legitimate financial trails. 
    • Underground banking is increasingly used to launder proceeds from cybercrime, fraud, corruption, tax evasion, illegal gambling and human trafficking, beyond traditional crimes such as drug trafficking and smuggling.  
    • HOSSPs remain a core method for terrorist financing, particularly in conflict zones and cash-heavy economies, often co-mingled with genuine diaspora remittances. 
  • Regional Patterns and Transnational Hubs: 
    • Africa & Asia: There is deep integration with mobile-money ecosystems where customers use mobile wallets for deposits and withdrawals, while underlying settlements occur off-platform. 
    • Middle East: Criminals are adopting hybrid models that combine virtual assets with gold and precious metals. 
    • Latin America: There is a continued reliance on the traditional Black Market Peso Exchange (BMPE) for laundering narcotics proceeds through trade and currency arbitrage. 
    • Global Hubs: A functional division of labor exists across the global architecture, distinguishing "outgoing hubs" (cash-generation zones in Europe and West Africa) from "incoming hubs" (settlement and absorption zones in Asia and the Middle East)

India-Specific Case Studies 

  • Trade-Based Money Laundering via Shell Companies (2022): Indian authorities uncovered a scheme involving shell companies created using forged/stolen KYC documents, which used under-invoicing and circular trade through a third country to justify outward remittances and settle illicit funds. 
  • Online Gambling–Hawala Nexus: An illegal online gambling network used Unified Payments Interface (UPI), digital wallets and mule accounts to collect and move proceeds. The funds were then converted into cash, transferred abroad through hawala, and round-tripped into India as purported foreign investment from the UAE. 

Key Terminology 

  • Underground Banking: Informal arrangements operating outside, alongside or through the regulated financial system to transfer, settle or store value through intermediary networks.  
    • It is not inherently criminal, but unlicensed operations are criminalised in most countries. 
  • Hawala and Other Similar Service Providers (HOSSPs): A subset of Money or Value Transfer Services (MVTS) that transfer or receive funds and generally settle balances on a net basis, without physically moving money across borders. 
    • Licensed HOSSPs operate within the formal sector under FATF Recommendation 14, while unlicensed HOSSPs form part of underground banking. 
  • Digital Hawala: Represents the technological evolution of unlicensed underground banking and HOSSPs, using digital tools to coordinate, execute and conceal informal value transfers, thereby making existing settlement methods faster and more difficult to trace.  

What are the Challenges in Curbing Hawala and Underground Banking? 

  • Partial Currency Convertibility: Under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) 1999, the Indian Rupee has partial convertibility. It is freely convertible for current-account transactions such as trade and remittances, while capital-account transactions remain subject to regulatory restrictions and limits.  
    • This can incentivise some individuals and businesses seeking to bypass authorised channels or move undisclosed funds abroad to use hawala networks.  
  • Digital Hawala and Crypto Layering: Illicit finance in India is rapidly shifting from physical cash couriers to peer-to-peer (P2P) crypto networks and offshore over-the-counter (OTC) desks.  
    • While the government has brought Virtual Digital Asset Service Providers (VDASPs) under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) 2002, criminals continuously exploit decentralized finance platforms, privacy coins, and unhosted wallets to evade the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND).  
  • Illegal Online Gambling–Hawala Nexus: Illegal betting platforms use UPI, digital wallets and mule accounts to collect funds, which are then moved abroad via hawala and round-tripped as foreign investment.  
    • The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 bans online money games, their advertising and related financial transactions.  
  • High Remittance Flows: India's large inward remittance inflows, particularly from the Gulf and Western countries, can provide cover for illicit funds. This makes it challenging for enforcement agencies to distinguish hawala-linked transactions from genuine remittances without affecting legitimate users.  
  • Impact on Common People: Innocent bank accounts may be frozen when linked to suspected hawala transactions, causing temporary loss of access to funds and financial hardship. This highlights the need for targeted action and safeguards for genuine account holders.  

What Measures are Needed to Curb Hawala and Underground Banking? 

  • Clear Legal Framework: Governments should register HOSSPs, clearly criminalise unauthorised underground banking, extend AML/CFT obligations to vulnerable sectors such as real estate and precious-metal businesses, and publicise enforcement actions to strengthen deterrence. 
  • Disrupt the Entire Money-Laundering Chain: Enforcement agencies should target the entire PML chain, from client recruitment and cash collection to settlement and cash-out. Early measures such as rapid account freezing and asset recovery can prevent criminals from moving or dissipating illicit funds. 
  • Strengthen Inter-Agency Coordination: Countries should establish multi-agency task forces involving FIUs, police, ED, customs, tax authorities and central banks. 
    • Sharing and matching financial, tax, GST and customs data can help identify suspicious transactions and trade-based money laundering. 
  • Centralise Data and Risk Assessment: Authorities should maintain centralised databases covering suspicious transactions, investigations, prosecutions and asset seizures.  
    • Underground banking and HOSSPs should also be treated as a distinct and regularly updated risk category in National Risk Assessments. 
  • Leverage Technology and Digital Forensics: Law-enforcement agencies should use blockchain analytics, AI and machine learning to trace crypto-based settlements, detect mule-account networks and identify suspicious transaction patterns.  
    • Greater scrutiny is also needed at virtual IBANs, payment gateways and digital banking platforms. 
  • Deepen International Cooperation: Countries should harmonise legal definitions and strengthen real-time intelligence sharing through networks such as Egmont and INTERPOL.  
    • Joint cross-border investigations can help dismantle transnational PML networks and their settlement hubs. 
  • Protect Financial Inclusion: Governments should expand low-cost formal remittance services and adopt proportionate KYC requirements for low-value transfers.  
    • Banks should also avoid indiscriminate de-risking, as cutting off legitimate customers can push them towards informal hawala channels.

Money Laundering Issue in India

09-08-2025

For Prelims: Supreme Court, Money Laundering, Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, Enforcement Directorate, Enforcement Case Information Report 

For Mains: Legal and Regulatory framework in India to combat Money Laundering, Prevention of Money-Laundering Act (PMLA), Supreme Court Judgement and its Implications

Source: TH 

Why in News? 

A report submitted by the Finance Minister in the Rajya Sabha reveals that the Enforcement Directorate (ED) has taken up 5,892 cases under Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002 since 2015 but secured only 15 convictions. 

  • Also, in another development the Supreme Court held that ED must follow the rule of law, urged fast-track courts for PMLA cases, and called for cryptocurrency regulation to prevent misuse without banning it. 

What is Money Laundering? 

  • About: It is the process of disguising illegal proceeds from criminal activities (e.g., drug trafficking, smuggling, arms trade, embezzlement) to make them appear legitimate, enabling criminals to enjoy profits without detection.  
    • It involves changing the source, form, or location of funds to avoid scrutiny. 
  • 3 Key Stages: Money laundering involves placement (introducing illicit funds), layering (disguising their origin), and integration (making them appear legitimate). 
  • Key Methods: Key methods include structuring/ smurfing (splitting large cash into smaller), trade-based laundering, shell companies, and real estate investments. 

What are the Key Reasons for Rising Money Laundering Cases in India? 

  • Legal and Enforcement Gaps: Misuse of Section 5 of PMLA (property attachment without registered offence) and delays in prosecution reduce effectiveness. 
  • Complex Laundering Techniques: Use of digital currencies/ cryptocurrencies, fintech, and cross-border mechanisms make detection difficult. 
    • In the Rs 260 crore global cyber fraud case, the ED found that the funds were converted into cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin and USDT, which were then laundered through hawala operators and individuals in the UAE. 
  • Shadow Economy & Cash-Based Sectors: High informal sector presence and lax regulation in real estate, jewellery, and luxury goods. 
  • Weak Global Cooperation: Despite Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) with 85+ countries, limited information sharing hampers investigation. 

What are the Key Consequences of Money Laundering? 

  • Fueling Organised Crime & Terrorism: Laundered funds sustain criminal networks, drug/human trafficking and terror financing. 
  • Erosion of Public Trust: Encourages corruption and weakens democratic institutions. 
  • Impact on Vulnerable Groups: Diverts public funds meant for welfare, widening socio-economic inequality. 
  • Economic Distortion: Undermines monetary policy, creates capital flow volatility, inflates real estate prices, deters FDI, and causes financial instability.

Laundromat 

  • A “laundromat” refers to a complex web of banking channels, shell firms, and offshore accounts used to clean illicit funds. The term originated from US crime syndicates using laundromats as fronts for laundering. 

What is the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002? 

  • About: PMLA, 2002 (enforced in 2005) was enacted to prevent money laundering and provide for the confiscation of property derived from such activities.  
    • It targets laundering linked to crimes like drug trafficking, smuggling, and terror financing. 
    • Enacted under Article 253 of the Constitution to fulfill international commitments. 
  • Key Provisions of PMLA: 
    • Attachment, Seizure & Confiscation: The Act empowers authorities to attach, seize, and confiscate property derived from crime.  
      • These actions are overseen by an Adjudicating Authority and require the existence of a scheduled offence (offence against the state). 
    • Initiation of Proceedings & ECIR: Proceedings can be initiated based on an ECIR (Enforcement Case Information Report) filed by the ED & no FIR is required.  
      • The ECIR acts as the starting point for investigation under PMLA. 
      • The ED derives its powers under PMLA, 2002 from various sections (3, 4, 5, 16, 17 etc) to investigate, attach, seize, and prosecute offenses related to money laundering. 
    • Bail Conditions (Section 45): The accused must prima facie prove innocence and assure the court of no likelihood of reoffending to secure bail. 
    • Institutional Mechanisms: Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) receives and analyses suspicious transaction reports from banks and financial institutions. 
      • The Appellate Tribunal hears appeals against orders passed by the Adjudicating Authority. 
    • Recent Amendments Related to PMLA: 2019 amendment to PMLA, introduced of Rule 3A, allowing Special Courts to notify claimants for confiscated property after charges are framed.  
      • The 2023 amendment expanded disclosure obligations for NGOs and redefined Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) to include prominent foreign officials, aligning with FATF standards. 
    • Global Cooperation: India has signed DTAAs with over 85 countries for: 
      • Facilitating exchange of financial and tax-related information. 
      • Enabling tracing of offshore accounts and investments. 
      • Preventing tax evasion and illegal fund transfers. 

Key Judicial Cases Related ED 

  • In P. Chidambaram vs. ED, 2019, case Supreme Court (SC) held that concealing the illegal source of money impacts the financial system and threatens the sovereignty and integrity of the nation. 
  • In Vir Bhadra Singh vs. ED, 2017 case, SC reiterated that FIR is not essential as ECIR is enough to proceed. 
  • In Vijay Madanlal Chaudhury vs. Union of India, 2022 case, SC held that registration of a scheduled offence is mandatory for prosecution (Sec 3), but not for property attachment (Sec 5) 
    • This provision is often misused against dissenting voices or political opponents. 

What Measures Should be Taken to Tackle the Issue Money Laundering? 

  • Strict Compliance with FATF Norms: Authorities must align enforcement with Financial Action Task Force (FATF) recommendations, ensuring transparency, accountability, and checks against misuse. 
  • Independent &Fair Investigations: Enforcement agencies should act independently, free from political bias, ensuring genuine cases are prioritized and rights of individuals protected. 
  • Strengthening Evidence Collection & Coordination: Improve financial intelligence mechanisms, inter-agency coordination (ED, FIU, Income Tax Dept.), and use of digital forensics to increase conviction rates. 
  • Effective Use of DTAA Mechanisms: Enhance data exchange protocols and real-time cooperation with foreign counterparts to track illicit financial flows and plug treaty loopholes. 
  • Judicial Oversight & Safeguards: Ensure greater judicial scrutiny in attachment and prosecution processes under PMLA to prevent arbitrary action and uphold due process of law.

Conclusion 

Money laundering is a grave threat to India’s financial stability and national security. While the PMLA offers a strong legal framework, its implementation is marred by delays, misuse, and low conviction rates. Combating this menace requires legal reforms, enhanced international cooperation, and strengthened institutional accountability.

NCLAT on IBC-PMLA Conflict

06-07-2026

Source: TH 

Why in News?  

The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) has ruled that the moratorium under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 cannot shield assets alleged to be “proceeds of crime” from attachment under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002.  

  • The ruling came in the insolvency proceedings involving Siddhi Vinayak Logistics Ltd., where the Enforcement Directorate (ED) had attached assets despite the company undergoing insolvency proceedings. 
  • The liquidator relied on P. Mohanraj v. Shah Brothers Ispat (2021), where the Supreme Court held that coercive recovery proceedings are barred during the IBC moratorium. In contrast, the ED relied on Embassy Property Developments v. State of Karnataka (2019), where the Court held that National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and NCLAT cannot exercise judicial review over decisions taken by independent statutory authorities under other laws.   

What did the NCLAT Rule Regarding the IBC-PMLA Conflict? 

  • Distinct Statutory Domains: The NCLAT held that the IBC, 2016 and PMLA, 2002, operate in separate spheres.  
    • The IBC seeks insolvency resolution and creditor recovery, while the PMLA aims to trace, attach, and confiscate proceeds of crime. 
  • Moratorium Limited to Legitimate Assets: Section 14 of the IBC, 2016, imposes a moratorium that halts suits, execution proceedings, and other civil recovery actions against the corporate debtor during the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) to preserve its assets.  
    • However, the NCLAT clarified that this protection extends only to legitimately acquired assets and does not shield assets alleged to be “proceeds of crime” from attachment under the PMLA, 2002. 
  • Public Law Proceedings Remain Unaffected: Proceedings under penal statutes such as the PMLA, 2002 which do not create or recover civil debt, cannot be halted by the IBC moratorium, even if attachment reduces the assets available to creditors. 
    • Section 41 of the PMLA, 2002 bars civil courts from entertaining suits or proceedings regarding matters that PMLA authorities are empowered to determine, effectively walling off ED actions from standard civil scrutiny. 
  • National Interest Takes Precedence: While creditors may accept haircuts under the IBC, the NCLAT held that the national interest in tracing and confiscating proceeds of crime cannot be compromised. 
    • Insolvency tribunals lack jurisdiction to examine the validity of ED attachment orders. Such actions must be challenged before the authorities and courts designated under the PMLA, 2002.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What did the NCLAT rule regarding the conflict between the IBC and PMLA?
The NCLAT held that theSection 14 IBC moratorium cannot shield assets alleged to be proceeds of crime from attachment under the PMLA, as the two laws operate in distinct statutory spheres. 

2. What is the purpose of the moratorium under Section 14 of the IBC?
Section 14 temporarily haltscivil suits, execution proceedings, and debt recovery actions against the corporate debtor during the CIRP to preserve legitimate assets for collective insolvency resolution. 

3. What is the significance of Section 41 of the PMLA?
Section 41 bars civil courts from entertaining matters that PMLA authorities are empowered to determine, thereby requiring challenges to ED attachment actions to follow the adjudicatory mechanisms under the PMLA.

4. Why did the NCLAT describe the IBC as not being a 'Holy Ganges'?
The NCLAT emphasised that Parliament did not enact the IBC towash away corporate criminality, legitimise ill-gotten wealth, or protect proceeds of crime from attachment and confiscation under the PMLA. 

5. Can NCLT and NCLAT review attachment orders passed by the Enforcement Directorate?
No. The NCLAT held thatinsolvency tribunals lack jurisdiction to examine the validity of PMLA attachment orders, which must be challenged before the authorities and courts designated under the PMLA.

70 Years of the Directorate of Enforcement

04-05-2026

Source: TH 

The Directorate of Enforcement (ED), established on 1st May 1956, completed its 70 years on 1st May 2026. 

Directorate of Enforcement 

  • About: ED is a premier financial investigation agency in India. It operates under the Department of Revenue, Ministry of Finance, and is primarily tasked with enforcing economic laws and fighting financial crimes.  
    • It is an executive body (non-statutory and non-constitutional) with pan-India jurisdiction, deriving its powers primarily from the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) and the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), along with supplementary laws such as the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018. 
  • Core Mandates: Under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA), the ED has the authority to investigate the "proceeds of crime," attach and confiscate property believed to be derived from illegal activities, etc. 
    • Under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA), the ED investigates contraventions of foreign exchange laws and regulations, such as illegal foreign exchange transactions, hawala dealings, etc. 
    • Under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 (FEOA), the ED can seize the properties of economic offenders (like high-profile loan defaulters) who have fled the country to avoid prosecution. 
  • Organisational Structure: The ED is headed by a Director of Enforcement. It is headquartered in New Delhi. ED has 10 Zonal offices (e.g., Chennai, Kolkata, Chandigarh, etc.), each of which is headed by a Deputy Director and 11 sub-Zonal Offices (e.g., Jaipur, Jalandhar, Srinagar, etc.), each of which is headed by an Assistant Director.  
    • Officers are drawn from the Indian Revenue Service (IRS), Indian Police Service (IPS), and the agency's own cadre.
