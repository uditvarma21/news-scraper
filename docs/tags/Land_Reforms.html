Unlocking the Agricultural Potential of Forest Rights Act Lands

22-10-2026

For Prelims: Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006, Millets, Gram Sabha, Kisan Credit Card, PM-KISAN, TRIFED 

For Mains: Forest Rights Act, 2006, Tribal rights, Sustainable agriculture and climate resilience

Source:TH 

Why in News?  

Policy experts, civil society organizations, and the government have highlighted the urgent need to unlock the agricultural potential of lands distributed under the Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006.  

  • Despite millions of tribal and forest-dwelling families receiving land titles, a lack of convergence with mainstream agricultural schemes has kept these lands underutilized, hindering the true economic empowerment of marginalized communities.  

Summary 

  • Despite the distribution of over 24 lakh Individual Forest Rights (IFR) titles, the agricultural potential of FRA lands remains underutilised due to poor access to credit, irrigation, agricultural schemes and markets. 
  • Converging agricultural and rural development schemes with FRA lands, promoting region-specific crops and strengthening institutional support can convert legal land rights into tribal economic empowerment, sustainable agriculture and improved food security.  

What is the Agri-Potential of FRA Lands? 

  • IFR Titles: Since the inception of the FRA, over 24 lakh Individual Forest Rights (IFR) titles have been distributed, covering millions of acres of land. Unlocking this potential can yield massive dividends. 
  • Vast Ecological Diversity: FRA is being implemented in 20 States (including Himachal Pradesh) and 1 Union Territory, reflecting immense agro-ecological and socio-economic diversity.  
  • Hub for Organic Farming: FRA lands are traditionally untouched by chemical fertilizers and pesticides, making them naturally suited for high-value organic agriculture. 
  • Agroforestry and Non-Timber Forest Products (NTFPs): These lands can support a mix of timber, fruit-bearing trees, and medicinal plants, providing year-round income and enhancing carbon sequestration. 
  • Climate Resilience: Traditional tribal farming practices, specifically the cultivation of millets (Shree Anna) and indigenous drought-resistant seeds, offer solutions for climate-resilient agriculture. 
    • Promoting pulses and oilseeds on FRA lands can boost tribal incomes and productivity. These crops suit rain-fed conditions, require less water and improve soil health.  
    • Cultivating sesame, spices and horticultural crops based on local agro-ecology can further strengthen livelihoods and reduce India’s import dependence on edible oils and pulses.  
  • Food and Nutritional Security: Maximizing output on IFR lands directly addresses malnutrition in tribal belts (such as central India's tribal corridor).

What are the Individual Forest Rights (IFR) Titles under FRA, 2006? 

  • About: Under the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (commonly known as the Forest Rights Act (FRA)), Individual Forest Rights (IFR) titles grant legally recognized ownership and land-use rights to eligible forest dwellers for self-cultivation and habitation. 
    • These titles serve to correct historical colonial injustices by securing the livelihoods of marginalized communities who have lived in harmony with forests for generations. 
  • Core Features of IFR Titles: 
    • Scope & Limits: The title recognizes land actively farmed or occupied by a family up to a maximum of 4 hectares.  
      • It only recognizes pre-existing occupation; no new land is granted or cleared under this provision.  
    • Legal Status: IFR titles are heritable but non-transferable. The land cannot be sold, leased, or transferred to anyone outside the family. 
    • Joint Titling: Titles are typically registered jointly in the names of both spouses, protecting women's land security. 
    • Protection from Displacement: The Forest Rights Act strictly prohibits the eviction or displacement of forest dwellers until their claim verification process is fully completed.  
  • Eligibility Criteria: 
    • Dwellers & Dependency: Must be a member of a Scheduled Tribe (FDST) or an Other Traditional Forest Dweller (OTFD) who primarily resides in and depends on the forest for their livelihood. 
    • Scheduled Tribes: Must have occupied the forest land prior to 13th December 2005. 
    • Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (OTFD): Must prove continuous residence and dependency in the forest for at least three generations (75 years) prior to  13th December 2005. 
  • Three-Tier Approval Mechanism: 
    • Gram Sabha: The initiating authority that receives, verifies, and passes resolutions on claims. 
    • Sub-Divisional Level Committee (SDLC): Reviews and compiles the Gram Sabha's records. 
    • District Level Committee (DLC): The final appellate authority that approves or rejects claims and issues the legal titles. 

IFR vs. Community Rights 

While IFR secures the immediate homestead and farm plots of a specific household, the FRA also grants two types of collective rights: 

  • Community Rights (CR): Grants shared access to grazing lands, water bodies, and the collection of minor forest produce (like honey, bamboo, or leaves). 
  • Community Forest Resource Rights (CFRR): Empowers the village Gram Sabha to protect, manage, and regenerate the broader forest area sustainably. 

What are the Challenges in Unlocking Agri-Potential of FRA Lands? 

  • Lack of Institutional Credit: IFR titles are rights of use, not absolute ownership. Hence, banks often refuse to accept FRA titles as collateral, denying farmers access to the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) and institutional crop loans. 
  • Exclusion from Agricultural Schemes: Because IFR lands are recorded as "Forest Land" and not "Revenue Land," titleholders are frequently excluded from direct benefit transfers like PM-KISAN, PM Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY), and fertilizer subsidies. 
  • Irrigation Deficit: Most FRA lands are located in undulating, hilly, or remote terrains and remain entirely rainfed. Access to the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY) is highly limited in these zones. 
  • Inadequate Land Development: Forest lands often require leveling, bunding, and soil conditioning before intensive agriculture can begin, which requires heavy initial capital investment. 
  • Market Linkages: Absence of nearby APMC mandis and Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) leaves tribal farmers vulnerable to exploitation by middlemen. 

What Measures are Needed for Convergence of FRA Lands into Agriculture? 

  • Targeted Cluster Development: Out of 244 tribal districts, 60 have major oilseed potential, 31 for pulses, and 23 for both. Developing region-specific production clusters in these high-potential districts is critical.  
  • Mandatory Scheme Convergence: Converging FRA land development with schemes such as PMKSY, the National Food Security and Nutrition Mission, the National Mission on Edible Oils and Van Dhan Yojana can improve irrigation, crop productivity, tribal livelihoods and value addition of Non-Timber Forest Products (NTFPs).  
  • Leveraging DA-JGUA: The Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan can strengthen post-title livelihood support through sustainable agriculture, allied activities and coordinated development interventions in tribal villages. 
    • The scheme includes livelihood support for FRA patta holders and Gram Sabha-led Community Forest Resource Management Plans. 
  • RBI Directives for Credit: The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) must mandate scheduled commercial banks to recognize IFR titles as legitimate documents for extending institutional credit (KCC) without demanding collateral. 
  • Leveraging MGNREGA: Funds under the Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) (VB–G RAM G) Act, 2025 should be prioritized for land development on IFR lands (e.g., building farm ponds, contour trenching, and land leveling). 
  • Promoting Tribal FPOs: Empowering organizations like TRIFED to establish tribal-specific Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) can help aggregate organic produce, process NTFPs, and connect farmers directly to global markets via the Van Dhan Yojana. 
  • Updating Revenue Records: States must actively integrate FRA titles into their digital land revenue portals (like Bhoomi, Dharani) to ensure administrative seamlessness. 
  • Strengthening Gram Sabhas: Gram Sabhas should participate in planning land and water development, crop diversification and community-based resource management to ensure that agricultural expansion respects customary rights and ecological sustainability. 

Conclusion 

Distributing titles under the Forest Rights Act is only the first step toward tribal justice; it must be followed by robust post-claim support. By converging agricultural, financial, and rural development schemes with FRA lands, India can uplift millions from poverty while advancing sustainable, climate-resilient agriculture in its forested hinterlands.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. What is the Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006?
The FRA recognises the forest and forestland rights of Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers, addressing historical injustices and protecting their livelihoods. 

2. What are Individual Forest Rights (IFR) under the FRA, 2006?
IFR recognises eligible forest dwellers’ rights to occupy forest land for self-cultivation and habitation, subject to statutory conditions and a maximum area of 4 hectares. 

3. What is the eligibility criterion for Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (OTFDs)?
OTFDs must establish 75 years of residence and dependence on forests before 13 December 2005, subject to the requirements of the Act. 

4. How can agricultural schemes support FRA titleholders?
Convergence with PMKSY, MGNREGA, the National Food Security and Nutrition Mission, the National Mission on Edible Oils and Van Dhan Yojana can improve irrigation, land development, productivity and market access. 

5. What are the major challenges in unlocking the agricultural potential of FRA lands?
Key challenges include inadequate institutional credit, exclusion from agricultural schemes, irrigation deficits, high land-development costs and weak market linkages.
