India-Mongolia Strategic Partnership

18-10-2025

For Prelims: Mongolia, Critical Minerals, Rare Earths Elements, Coking Coal, Gobi Desert, Snow Leopard, Musk Deer, Nomadic Elephant, Khaan Quest

For Mains: Dynamics of India-Mongolia Strategic Partnership, Buddhist diplomacy, Role of soft power and development partnerships in India's engagement with resource-rich, landlocked nations. 

Source: IE

Why in News?

The President of Mongolia undertook a State Visit to India, commemorating the 70th anniversary of diplomatic relations and the 10th anniversary of the Strategic Partnership between the two nations.

What are the Key Outcomes of the President of Mongolia Visit to India? 

  • Defence & Security Cooperation: India launched a capacity-building programme for Mongolia’s border security forces and strengthened defence ties via training programmes and a Defence Attache at the Indian Embassy. 
  • Energy Security: The visit confirmed the USD 1.7 billion Oil Refinery Project in Mongolia, funded by an Indian Line of Credit, marking India’s largest global development partnership and pivotal for Mongolia’s energy security. 
  • Economic Partnership: Cooperation in critical minerals, rare earths elements, and coking coal was highlighted as a priority, with India considering logistical routes through Vladivostok (Russia) and Tianjin port (China) for importing Mongolian coking coal. 
  • Cultural Connectivity: India will send the holy relics of Lord Buddha's disciples, Sariputra and Maudgalyayana, to Mongolia, and an MoU was signed between the Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Council and Arkhangai Province to boost cultural ties. 
    • India will also send a Sanskrit teacher to the Gandan Monastery (Mongolia) and launch a project to digitize one million ancient Mongolian manuscripts. 
  • Development Cooperation & Skill Development: Both sides signed pacts on cultural exchange, immigration cooperation, disaster management, and Bogd Khan Palace (Mongolia) renovation, while India’s developmental role was highlighted via the Atal Bihari Vajpayee Centre of Excellence for IT and the India-Mongolia Friendship School.

Mongolia

  • Geography: Mongolia is a landlocked country in Asia,located between Russia to the north and China to the south. The country is extremely dry, receiving only 4 inches of rainfall annually. 
    • Southern Mongolia is dominated by the Gobi Desert (cold desert). 
    • The country is called the Land of the Eternal Blue Sky and the Land of the Horse.

  • People & Culture: Many Mongolians live in traditional yurts (gers), which are dome-shaped tents.  
    • The Naadam Festival, celebrated in summer, features sports, games, and food, with children participating in events like horse racing. 
    • Most of the population practices traditional nomadic herding, and Mongolia includes ethnic groups like Mongols, Kazakhs, and Tuvans. 
  • Nature & Wildlife: Mongolia is home to native species such as Bactrian camels with two humps and resilient Mongolian horses.Endangered species include snow leopard and musk deer.  
    • The first dinosaur egg ever discovered was found in the Gobi, along with many dinosaur remains and fossils from 100 million years ago, during the late Cretaceous period. 
  • History: The Mongol Empire rose to power under Genghis Khan and his sons in the 13th century, conquering much of Asia and Europe. Marco Polo and his family were the first Europeans to cross the Gobi around AD 1275.

What is the Status of India-Mongolia Relations? 

  • Historical Relations: Historical conjectures suggest migration from the Kangra kingdom 4,300 years ago under Mangaldev, though evidence is lacking.  
    • During the Hunnu State (3rd century BC in Mongolia) and the Great Mongol Empire, Buddhist monks and Indian traders visited Mongolia, and in 552 BC, Lama Narendrayash from Udayana (Northern India) visited Nirun state (Mongolia). 
  • Political Relations: Diplomatic relations between India and Mongolia were established in 1955, making India the first country outside the Socialist bloc to do so. India also supported Mongolia’s membership in the United Nations (UN) and Non-Aligned Movement (NAM).  
  • Trade Relations: In 2024, India-Mongolia bilateral trade stood at USD 111 million.  India’s exports to Mongolia include medicines, mining machinery, and auto parts, while imports consist mainly of raw cashmere wool. 
  • Defence Cooperation: The joint India-Mongolia exercise Nomadic Elephant is held annually, and India also participates in the week-long Khaan Quest training exercise hosted by Mongolia. 
  • Humanitarian Assistances: India provided humanitarian assistance worth USD 20,000 (beds, bedrolls, toys) to children of herders in Sukhbaatar Aimag affected by Zud (harsh winter), and USD 50,000 to flood-affected provinces Bayan Ulgii, Arhangai, and Huvsgul. 
  • Cultural Relations: The India-Mongolia Cultural Agreement of 1961 governs the Cultural Exchange Programme (CEP), facilitating educational cooperation through scholarships, expert exchanges, and conference participation.  
  • A pan-Mongolia Indian dance competition, Melody of Ganga, is being organized for school children.

What Factors are Impeding a Stronger India–Mongolia Partnership? 

  • Geographical & Logistical Chokepoint: Mongolia's landlocked status creates a fundamental logistical barrier for bilateral trade and investment.  
    • All movement of goods, including crucial mineral exports for India and Line of Credit (LoC) imports for Mongolia's oil refinery, must be routed through either Russia or China, driving up costs and reducing India's trade competitiveness. 
  • Disparity in Economic Scale and Trade Volume: Despite the Strategic Partnership since 2015, actual bilateral trade remains minuscule (around USD 110 million in 2024), especially compared with Mongolia’s economic dependence on China. 
  • Geopolitical Vulnerability to Neighbors: Mongolia’s "Third Neighbour" policy relies on India as a democratic and spiritual partner to balance China and Russia.  
    • However, any aggressive move by India to deepen this relationship could be perceived as geopolitical provocation by its powerful neighbors, complicating cooperation and potentially creating instability for Mongolia. 
  • Project Implementation and Political Volatility: The timeline and successful execution of India's flagship projects, particularly the USD 1.7 billion oil refinery (expected 2028), are susceptible to delays, cost overruns, and local political instability in Mongolia.  
    • Slow implementation or policy changes can invite skepticism and criticism, preventing the deepening of trust needed to translate MoUs into lasting strategic gains. 

What Measures can India Adopt to Deepen its Ties with Mongolia? 

  • Critical Minerals Partnership: Invest in coal gasification projects and joint ventures to extract Mongolia’s rare earth elements (e.g., copper, uranium), reducing India’s dependency on China for critical minerals. 
  • Strategic Connectivity: Launch direct flights (e.g., Delhi-Ulaanbaatar) and collaborate on digital infrastructure (e.g., India Stack for e-governance) to strengthen Mongolia’s “Third Neighbor” policy. 
  • Defense & Security Cooperation: Expand exercises on peacekeeping, counterterrorism, and cold‑desert warfare, leveraging India’s high‑altitude expertise. 
  • Promote Cultural Links: Fund restoration of Buddhist heritage sites (e.g., Gandan Monastery) and promote scholarly exchanges through Nalanda University, while organizing joint cultural events and cinema co-productions to leverage Mongolia’s interest in Indian media. 
  • Agriculture & Food Security: Share Indian expertise in cooperative dairy models (e.g., Amul) and modernize meat export infrastructure, while providing drought-resistant crop technologies to combat desertification.

Conclusion 

The President of Mongolia’s state visit to India reinforced a decade-long strategic partnership, advancing defence, energy, economic, cultural, and skill development cooperation. With landmark initiatives like the USD 1.7 billion Oil Refinery Project, capacity-building for border security, and cultural exchanges, the visit strengthened bilateral trust, regional engagement, and people-to-people ties.

India–UK CETA Comes into Effect

17-07-2026

For Prelims:  India-UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement, Double Contribution Convention (DCC), Make in India,  Global Capability Centres 

For Mains: India–UK CETA: Opportunities and challenges for India's trade policy, Free Trade Agreements (FTAs) and their role in achieving Viksit Bharat 2047, India's strategy to balance trade liberalisation with protection of domestic industries.

Source: PIB 

Why in News?

The India-UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), along with its companion social security pact known as the Double Contribution Convention (DCC), formally came into effect on 15th July 2026.

Summary

  • The India–UK CETA and Double Contribution Convention (DCC), effective from 15th  July 2026, significantly deepen bilateral trade by expanding market access, boosting services, investment, and professional mobility while safeguarding India's sensitive sectors. 
  • Its success will depend on addressing non-tariff barriers, CBAM-related challenges, MSME preparedness, and effective implementation to enhance India's competitiveness in global value chains.

What are the Key Highlights of India-UK CETA? 

  • UK Market Access: The UK has granted zero-duty access to nearly 99% of India's exports, covering almost 100% of the bilateral trade value, significantly enhancing India's export competitiveness and boosting trade and investment. 
  • India's Tariff Commitments: India has offered tariff concessions on 89.5% of its tariff lines, covering 91% of UK exports. While 24.5% of UK exports receive immediate duty-free access, concessions on the remaining products will be phased in gradually to safeguard sensitive domestic sectors. 
  • Protection for Sensitive Sectors: India has successfully ring-fenced its sensitive domestic sectors.  
    • No duty concessions have been granted to the UK on items like dairy, agriculture, millets, apples, edible oils, and gold. 
  • Services Trade Boost: The UK has offered its most comprehensive services commitments to date, opening up all 12 major service sectors and 137 sub-sectors. 
    • This creates unprecedented market access for Indian IT, healthcare, financial, and engineering professionals. 
  • Safeguards for Steel Trade: To protect Indian commercial interests against upcoming UK steel trade measures, both nations arrived at a landmark consensus to minimize market disruptions and protect Indian steel exporters. 
  • Professional Mobility: CETA creates clear rules for the temporary movement of professionals, explicitly preventing hidden numerical quotas or arbitrary "economic needs tests" that require proof of local worker unavailability before admitting a foreign professional. 
  • Protection for Strategic Sectors: Tariff concessions for sectors under Make in India and the PLI Scheme are phased over 5, 7, and 10 years, allowing domestic industries time to build capacity and remain competitive. 
  • Calibrated Automotive Liberalisation: India has adopted a quota-based, phased market opening for automobiles, while protecting sensitive domestic segments. 
    • Import concessions for affordable EVs begin only from Year 6, giving domestic manufacturers time to scale up technology, production, and competitiveness. 

Significance of India-UK CETA 

  • Textiles & Apparel: Zero-duty access removes India's tariff disadvantage against Bangladesh, Pakistan and Cambodia in the UK's USD 28.8 billion textile import market, boosting exports of garments, home textiles and handicrafts. 
  • Plantation Sector: Duty-free access for instant coffee boosts India's value-added coffee exports; the UK accounts for 5.6% of India's tea exports, 2.9% of spice exports, and 1.7% of coffee exports. 
  • Marine Products: Tariff-free access opens the UK's USD 4.9 billion marine products market, boosting value-added seafood exports and coastal livelihoods. 
  • Engineering & Electronics: Zero-duty access is expected to increase engineering exports to over USD 7.5 billion by 2029–30, while accelerating exports of smartphones, optical fibre cables, and IT-enabled services. 
  • Pharmaceuticals & Chemicals: Duty-free access enables India to tap the UK's USD 30 billion pharmaceutical and USD 35.8 billion chemical import markets, strengthening its position as a preferred supplier. 
  • Plastics: Duty-free access lowers landed costs, enhancing the competitiveness of Indian plastic products; India is already the 13th largest supplier of plastics to the UK. 
  • Sports Goods & Toys: Tariff elimination is expected to increase exports by 15%, with exports targeted to reach USD 186.97 million by 2030, improving competitiveness against China and Vietnam. 
  • Oilseeds: Reduced tariffs and streamlined procedures enhance export competitiveness, enabling Indian oilseed exporters to expand their presence in the UK market. 
  • Leather & Footwear: Duty-free access to the UK's USD 8.9 billion market strengthens India's competitiveness against Vietnam, Indonesia and Bangladesh while supporting labour-intensive manufacturing. 
  • Marine Products: Removal of UK tariffs on seafood improves price realisation, promotes value-added processing, and benefits coastal states such as Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Odisha and West Bengal. 
  • Promotes Inclusive Innovation: Encourages inclusive, technology-neutral innovation across sectors, including agriculture, while benefiting producers in Andhra Pradesh & Tamil Nadu (multiple crops), Punjab (agriculture), Maharashtra (grapes, onions), Gujarat (groundnut, cotton), Kerala (spices), and the North Eastern States (horticulture). 
  • Gems & Jewellery: Tariff concessions are expected to double India's jewellery exports within 2–3 years, generating employment in major jewellery clusters such as Surat, Jaipur and Mumbai. 
  • Steel Sector: Expanded tariff-free quotas and preferential access protect Indian steel exports from the UK's safeguard measures while ensuring continued market access.

What is the Double Contribution Convention (DCC)? 

  • About: The DCC is a landmark arrangement designed to eliminate dual social security contributions for defined categories of temporary workers moving between the UK and India. 
  • Extended Exemption Window: Traditionally, the UK exempts temporary foreign workers (termed "detached workers") from National Insurance (NI) contributions for the first 12 months.  
    • The DCC expands this exemption window to 60 months (5 years). 
  • The Non-Retrospective Clause: The pact does not apply retrospectively. 
    • According to UK guidance, Indian professionals already working in the UK immediately prior to 15thJuly 2026, are not considered detached workers under the DCC. They will be subject to UK social security laws and must pay UK National Insurance contributions. 
  • Certificate of Coverage (CoC): To claim the exemption, workers arriving under the new regime must secure a CoC from India’s Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) as evidence that they are contributing to the Indian social security system. 
  • Significance: The convention is projected to benefit over 75,000 Indian professionals and 900 companies, resulting in industry-estimated annual savings of more than USD 600 million while directly enhancing the take-home earnings of Indian talent. 
    • By facilitating seamless, cost-effective talent movement, the convention provides an incentive for UK corporations to upgrade their Indian operations from low-cost back-offices to strategic Global Capability Centres (GCCs) specializing in R&D, advanced analytics, and cybersecurity.  

Current Status of India-UK Bilateral Trade 

  • Economic Scale: As of 2025, India’s GDP reached USD 3.96 trillion, while the UK economy stood at USD 3.84 trillion, highlighting their massive footprint in the global economy. 
  • Merchandise Trade: In the global fiscal year 2025-26, bilateral merchandise trade stood at USD 25.12 billion. India’s exports were valued at USD 13.44 billion against imports of USD 11.68 billion, yielding a trade surplus of USD 1.76 billion for India. 
  • Services Trade: In 2024, total bilateral services trade reached USD 35.44 billion. India exported services worth USD 21.66 billion and imported USD 13.78 billion, resulting in a services trade surplus of USD 7.88 billion for India. 
  • Investment Footprint: The UK is India's 6th largest inward investor with cumulative equity investments of USD 35 billion (up to Sept 2024), while India's outward investment in the UK reached USD 19 billion (up to March 2024). Over 971 Indian companies operate in the UK, employing over 1 lakh individuals. 

What are the Key Challenges in India–UK CETA? 

  • Non-Tariff Barriers: Strict Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) norms, technical standards, and regulatory certifications may limit export gains, especially for MSMEs, agri-food, pharmaceutical, and engineering exporters. 
  • UK Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM): From 2027, carbon-intensive exports such as steel, aluminium, cement, and fertilisers may face carbon taxes, reducing India's export competitiveness. 
  • Import Circumvention: Reduced tariffs may enable third-country products to enter India via the UK, potentially undermining Make in India and PLI initiatives. 
  • Complex Rules of Origin: Stringent origin verification and documentation increase compliance costs, particularly for small exporters seeking preferential tariff benefits. 
  • Limited Mobility Gains: Professional mobility remains restricted, with limited visa liberalisation despite the Double Contribution Convention (DCC) and a small annual quota for select service providers. 
  • Low FTA Utilisation: Limited awareness of FTA provisions and compliance requirements could prevent Indian exporters from fully leveraging CETA's benefits.

What Measures can Strengthen India–UK CETA? 

  • Address Non-Tariff Barriers: Strengthen testing, certification, and traceability infrastructure to meet UK SPS and technical standards, while actively using the CETA Joint Committee to resolve trade barriers. 
  • Greening Supply Chains: Domestically, India must accelerate the decarbonization of its manufacturing sector.  
    • Aligning domestic industries with global green standards is the only long-term defense against emerging climate-based trade barriers.  
  • Enhance MSME FTA Utilisation: Establish MSME Trade Facilitation Centres and promote paperless trade and Authorised Economic Operator (AEO) mechanisms to simplify compliance. 
  • Expand Services & Mobility: Fast-track Mutual Recognition Agreements (MRAs) and leverage CETA to promote high-value sectors such as AI, cybersecurity, and advanced analytics. 
  • Protect Domestic Industry: Strictly enforce Rules of Origin to prevent trade circumvention and undertake periodic sectoral reviews, invoking safeguard measures where necessary.

Conclusion 

The India-UK CETA marks a definitive shift in India's trade strategy. It transitions the nation away from defensive protectionism and toward a confident, high-yield integration into Western supply chains. The ultimate success of the pact will be determined not by the elimination of tariffs, but by domestic regulatory reforms that neutralize Western non-tariff barriers and carbon taxation frameworks.

Seafarer Safety in Conflict Zones

17-06-2026

Source: TH  

Why in News?  

The death of three Indian seafarers aboard MT Settebello following a US military strike in the Gulf of Oman has brought maritime security and the safety of commercial shipping (seafarers) in conflict zones into focus.  

What is the Core Issue Affecting Seafarer Safety? 

  • Asymmetric Maritime Warfare: Modern maritime threats have evolved drastically from traditional piracy.  
    • Commercial vessels are now increasingly subjected to military-grade precision munitions, drone strikes, and loitering munitions by sovereign militaries and non-state actors alike. 
  • Geopolitical Crossfire and Blockades: Civilian merchant ships are frequently targeted to enforce geopolitical blockades (e.g., the US naval blockade on Iranian ports).  
    • Unarmed merchant ships carrying highly combustible cargo become vulnerable collateral damage in conflicts they have no part in. 
  • Sanctions-Related Vulnerabilities: Vessels accused of violating international sanctions often lose essential safeguards, including their Protection and Indemnity (P&I) insurance cover and port access, exponentially increasing operational and financial hazards for the crew onboard. 
  • Absence of Rigid Legal Safeguards in High-Seas Conflict: While international frameworks like UNCLOS guarantee the "freedom of navigation" and the Maritime Labour Convention (MLC) secures seafarer rights, these treaties lack hard enforcement mechanisms when non-state actors (like Houthis) or sovereign militaries engage in drone/missile strikes. 

Sanctioned Vessels  

  • Sanctioned Vessel: A sanctioned vessel is a ship that has been officially blacklisted or restricted under economic, trade, or security laws. 
  • These restrictions are put in place either globally by the UN Security Council (through binding resolutions) or unilaterally by individual countries and regional blocs (such as the US, UK, or EU). 
  • Triggers of Sanctions: Vessels are typically sanctioned due to direct or indirect ownership by blacklisted entities, links to transnational terrorism, or involvement in transporting prohibited cargo like weapons and illicit oil.  
    • Furthermore, ships face penalties for active sanctions evasion, which includes deceptive shipping practices like disabling Automatic Identification Systems (AIS) and conducting unauthorized ship-to-ship transfers in international waters. 
  • Applicability of Unilateral Sanctions: Unilateral sanctions apply strictly within the imposing nation's jurisdiction unless backed by a binding UN Security Council resolution.  
    • Consequently, a vessel blacklisted by external authorities like the US is not automatically illegal under Indian law, as India exclusively recognizes and enforces UN-mandated global sanctions. 

Why is Seafarer Safety a Major Strategic Concern for India? 

  • Massive Demographic and Labor Footprint: India has over 300,000 trained seafarers (2025), accounting for nearly 12% of the global seafaring workforce and ranking third worldwide after the Philippines and China.  
    • Under Maritime India Vision 2030, India aims to increase its global share to 20% by 2030 through expanded maritime training and skilling infrastructure.  
    • At any given time, thousands of Indian sailors (approximately 18,000 in the Persian Gulf/Gulf of Oman region alone) are navigating highly volatile chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab-el-Mandeb (Red Sea). 
  • Vulnerability of "Flags of Convenience": A vast majority of Indian seafarers are employed on commercial ships registered in foreign nations under Flags of Convenience (FoC) (e.g., Panama, Liberia, Palau). 
    • When global powers or rebel groups target a merchant vessel due to its cargo, ownership, or registration flag, Indian crew members become unintended collateral damage in conflicts they have no part in (as seen in the MT Settebello tragedy). 
    • This complicates legal jurisdiction and accountability, leaving the crew's home country with limited direct intervention power when a vessel is attacked. 
  • Economic and Remittance Security: Indian seafarers send back billions of dollars in foreign remittances annually, supporting the domestic economy and thousands of coastal families. 
    • Over 90% of India's trade by volume and 70% by value moves through maritime routes. Any threat to seafarer safety directly spikes maritime insurance premiums (War Risk Surcharges), inflating the cost of India's energy imports (crude oil and LNG).

Legal Provisions for Seafarers' Safety 

Global 

  • UNCLOS, 1982 (United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea): The foundational global treaty that guarantees "freedom of navigation" and "transit passage" through international straits. 
  • Maritime Labour Convention (MLC), 2006 (Ratified by India): Adopted and governed by the International Labour Organization (ILO).  
    • It is widely recognized as the "Seafarers' Bill of Rights," it mandates decent working conditions, comprehensive welfare, health protection, and guaranteed repatriation rights for sailors. 
  • Key IMO Conventions Related to Seafarers' and Maritime Safety: 
    • STCW (International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers), 1978 (Ratified by India): This is the foundational convention directly regulating seafarers.  
      • It establishes global minimum qualification standards for masters, officers, and watch personnel to ensure operational safety on board. 
    • International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS), 1974 (Ratified by India): Governed by the International Maritime Organization (IMO), it establishes the minimum safety standards for the construction, equipment, and operation of merchant ships. 
    • SAR (International Convention on Maritime Search and Rescue), 1979 (Ratified by India): Coordinates international efforts to ensure that any person or seafarer in distress at sea will be assisted and rescued regardless of geographic location.  

Indian 

  • Merchant Shipping Act, 1958: The primary domestic legislation governing Indian-registered vessels, setting stringent safety standards and employment conditions. 
  • Directorate General of Shipping (DGS): The apex regulatory authority under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways, responsible for overseeing merchant shipping, vessel certification, and seafarer employment agencies, with jurisdiction primarily over Indian-flagged vessels.  
  • Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR): Located in Gurugram, it acts as the nerve center for Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA), facilitating real-time threat tracking and multinational intelligence sharing.

What Measures can Strengthen Seafarer Safety? 

  • Strengthen Naval Deployments: India must continue to expand the scope and operational footprint of initiatives like the Indian Navy’s Operation Sankalp to provide enhanced surveillance, rapid-response Search and Rescue (SAR), and active deterrence in high-risk zones across the Arabian Sea. 
  • Adopt a Risk-Based Approach: Conduct regular risk assessments, issue clear advisories, ensure informed consent for deployment in high-risk zones, and strengthen security protocols instead of imposing blanket restrictions.  
  • Mandate Informed Consent and Accountability: The DGS must rigorously audit Recruitment and Placement Services License agencies to ensure mandatory risk disclosure before deployment.  
    • Furthermore, seafarers who refuse assignments in declared war-risk areas must be legally protected from professional penalties. 
  • Establish an Inter-Ministerial Framework: Create an integrated maritime security mechanism involving regulators, foreign affairs, defence, intelligence agencies, shipowners, and seafarers' unions for coordinated crisis response, real-time decision-making, and support through dedicated contact points, verified updates, and assistance to affected families.  
  • Leverage Multilateral Platforms: India must actively utilize its current chairmanship of the Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA), alongside its voice at the IMO, to forge binding, multinational protocols that explicitly protect civilian mariners from being targeted during sovereign geopolitical conflicts.

India-Qatar Trade and Economic Cooperation

09-10-2025

For Prelims: Qatar, Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement, Unified Payments Interface, LNG Supply Agreement, FDI, Za’ir-Al-Bahr, National Infrastructure Pipeline, ISRO.                                            

For Mains: India-Qatar Strategic Partnership: Analyzing Mutual Importance, Economic Synergy, and Future Trajectories in West Asian Geopolitics. 

Source: BS  

Why in News? 

The Union Minister of Commerce and Industry visited Qatar, to co-chair the India-Qatar Joint Commission on Economic and Commercial Cooperation, focusing on boosting bilateral trade and exploring new economic opportunities. 

What are the Key Outcomes of the India-Qatar Joint Commission Meeting? 

  • Ambitious Trade Target: Both countries recognized the untapped potential and emphasised the goal of doubling bilateral trade by 2030. Current trade stands at approximately USD 14 billion. 
    • Both nations reaffirmed their commitment to pursuing an ambitious India-Qatar Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA), a free-trade pact. 
  • Digital Integration: A key milestone was the launch of India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) in Qatar, facilitating seamless digital payments for the Indian diaspora and local consumers. 
  • Sectors Identified for Growth: The ministers highlighted sectors to boost Indian exports and collaborations: 
    • Traditional: Pharmaceuticals, textiles, gems & jewellery 
    • Manufacturing & Tech: Electronics, automobiles, processed foods 
    • Future-Focused: IT, emerging high-tech industries, solar energy.

Why in News?

The Prime Minister of India  concluded a State Visit to Indonesia,  marking a new chapter in the India-Indonesia Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (2018). What are the Key Outcomes of the PM Visit to Indonesia?

How are India-Indonesia Bilateral Relations? 

  • Civilizational Transmission: Historical footprints show that Hindu, Buddhist, and Islamic faiths transitioned to the Indonesian archipelago from the Indian coastline, anchoring a shared cultural heritage. 
  • Post-Colonial Solidarity: Both nations spearheaded Afro-Asian independence movements, laying the groundwork for the historic Bandung Conference of 1955 and the subsequent establishment of the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) in 1961. 
  • Policy Evolution: Institutional relations progressed under India’s 'Look East Policy' (1991) and were structurally upgraded to the 'Act East Policy' (2014), culminating in the celebration of 75 years of diplomatic ties in 2024. 
    • In 2018 the relationship was elevated to a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, institutionalizing a 'Shared Vision on Maritime Cooperation in the Indo-Pacific'. 
  • Economic & Commercial Dynamics: Indonesia has emerged as India’s second-largest trading partner in the ASEAN region, with bilateral trade reaching USD 28.15 billion in 2024-25. 
    • India stands as the largest global buyer of Indonesian crude palm oil and its second-largest buyer of coal, while exporting refined petroleum, vehicles, steel, and agricultural goods. 
  • Tourism Corridor: India stands as the fifth-largest source of tourists to Indonesia, driving high flight frequencies on sectors like Delhi-Bali and Mumbai-Jakarta. 
  • Defence & Maritime Security Cooperation: Formal defense cooperation monitored through mechanisms like the Joint Defence Cooperation Committee (JDCC) and regular Defence Ministers' Dialogues. 
    • Bilateral engagements feature prominent joint maneuvers including IND-INDO CORPAT (Coordinated Patrols), Multilateral Exercises Milan and Komodo, Army Exercise Garuda Shakti, and Naval Exercise Samudra Shakti.

    • Capacity Building via ITEC: Indonesia remains a primary beneficiary of India's ITEC (Indian Technical and Economic Cooperation) framework; notably, a 30-member delegation of Indonesian Judges traveled to India in April 2026 for advanced training at the National Judicial Academy in Bhopal. 
    • Vibrant Diaspora: The bilateral fabric is supported by a robust diaspora consisting of approximately 1,20,000 Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) and 15,000 Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) driving commercial operations in Indonesia.

Strategic Significance of India-Indonesia Ties

Dimension 

Significance 

Geopolitical Strategy 

Acts as the cornerstone of India’s 'Act East Policy' and SAGAR vision. Indonesia is India's closest maritime neighbor in Southeast Asia. 

Maritime Security 

Joint development of Sabang Port enhances the Indian Navy's logistical reach and interoperability to monitor the Strait of Malacca, ensuring a free, open, and rules-based Indo-Pacific. 

Countering Dominance 

Strengthening Indonesia's military capabilities (via BrahMos and Astra) and diversifying critical mineral supply chains reduces the region's asymmetric dependence on China. 

ASEAN Centrality 

Indonesia is the largest economy and a natural leader within ASEAN. Deepening ties with Jakarta automatically amplifies India's strategic footprint across the entire Southeast Asian bloc. 

What are the Challenges in India-Indonesia Relations? 

  • The "Quad" vs. ASEAN Centrality: Indonesia strictly adheres to its Bebas-Aktif (Independent and Active) foreign policy.  
    • While India views the Quad as a vital security architecture for a free Indo-Pacific, Indonesia views minilateral groupings with high caution, fearing they provoke China and undermine "ASEAN Centrality." 
  • Hesitance on Anti-China Balancing: Despite purchasing the BrahMos system, Indonesia meticulously avoids any overt military alignment that could be construed as an anti-China bloc.  
    • They prefer localized capacity building over India's broader strategic balancing efforts against Beijing. 
    • Indonesia’s deep economic integration with China, particularly in infrastructure and nickel-based EV supply chains, overshadows India’s investment footprint and limits New Delhi’s ability to emerge as a major economic alternative. 
  • Trade Imbalance and Palm Oil Vulnerability: Bilateral trade of around USD 28–30 billion remains heavily skewed in Indonesia’s favour, driven by India’s large imports of palm oil.  
    • Indonesia’s diversion of palm oil towards domestic biofuels could tighten global edible oil supplies and fuel food inflation in India.  
  • Trade Barriers: The absence of a bilateral Comprehensive Economic Cooperation Agreement (CECA), low utilisation of the ASEAN-India Trade in Goods Agreement (AITIGA) and Indonesian non-tariff barriers in sectors such as pharmaceuticals and agricultural products constrain trade expansion. 
  • Protectionist Frictions: Conversely, Indian domestic industries (especially textiles) frequently raise alarms over the influx and alleged dumping of Indonesian products like Viscose Staple Fibre (VSF), leading to defensive trade posturing.  

What Should be the Way Forward for India-Indonesia Relations? 

  • Restore Trade Parity: Fast-track the AITIGA review and explore a bilateral CECA to address non-tariff barriers and expand Indian exports in pharmaceuticals, dairy and other high-potential sectors. 
  • Build Critical Mineral Supply Chains: Develop nickel, rare-earth and EV battery value chains through joint ventures, equity investments and processing facilities in Indonesia, reducing excessive dependence on China-dominated supply chains. 
  • Operationalise Sabang Port: Establish a dedicated Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV), assured financing and time-bound implementation framework to accelerate development of the strategically located Sabang Port near the Malacca Strait. 
  • Respect Indonesia’s Strategic Autonomy: Align India’s SAGAR vision with Indonesia’s Global Maritime Fulcrum while respecting its Bebas-Aktif doctrine and commitment to ASEAN Centrality, focusing on maritime capacity-building, intelligence sharing and practical Indo-Pacific cooperation. 

Value Addition for Mains: Formation of the Indonesian Archipelago 

  • The Indonesian archipelago is a classic geomorphological example of a Volcanic Island Arc, created by intense tectonic activity along the Pacific Ring of Fire. 
  • Plate Convergence: The region is situated at a highly active triple junction where the Eurasian, Indo-Australian, and Pacific plates constantly collide. 
  • Subduction: The denser, heavier oceanic crust of the Indo-Australian plate is forced underneath the lighter Eurasian plate. This creates a deep underwater depression known as a subduction zone (e.g., the Sunda Trench). 
  • Magma Generation: As the subducting plate plunges deep into the mantle (asthenosphere), extreme friction, heat, and the release of trapped water cause the surrounding rock to undergo partial melting, creating highly pressurized magma. 
    • Because this newly formed magma is less dense than the surrounding rock, it rises through the fractures of the overriding Eurasian plate and erupts on the ocean floor as underwater volcanoes. 
  • Island Emergence: Over millions of years, continuous volcanic eruptions build massive submarine mountains.  
    • Once these peaks accumulate enough lava and ash to breach the ocean's surface, they form a continuous, curved chain of islands (an island arc) like Sumatra, Java, and Bali.

India–Canada Strategic Reset

Source: PIB

Why in News? 

The Prime Minister of Canada, Mark Carney, paid an official visit to India, marking what is widely viewed as a strategic reset in bilateral relations after the diplomatic tensions of 2023–24. 

  • The visit signals an effort by both countries to refocus on economic cooperation, defence dialogue, and strengthening the overall partnership.

Summary

  • The Prime Minister of Canada's visit to India marked a reset in India–Canada relations after the 2023–24 diplomatic tensions. Major outcomes included restarting CEPA trade negotiations, a USD 2.6 billion uranium supply deal with Cameco, cooperation on critical minerals and clean energy, the launch of the first India–Canada Defence Dialogue, and initiatives in innovation, research internships, and food technology.
  • India and Canada share strong economic and diaspora ties (1.8 million Indians in Canada) and aim to double trade to $50 billion by 2030. However, issues like Khalistani extremism, trade barriers, and visa delays remain challenges. Stronger security cooperation, early trade agreements, and Indo-Pacific collaboration can help rebuild trust and deepen the partnership.

What are the Key Outcomes of the Visit of the PM of Canada to India?

  • Relaunch of CEPA Negotiations: India and Canada signed the Terms of Reference (ToR) to resume talks on the Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA). 
    • Both nations have set an ambitious target to double bilateral trade to USD 50 billion by 2030.
  • Landmark Civil Nuclear Deal: The Department of Atomic Energy (India) signed a USD 2.6 billion long-term commercial contract with Canada's Cameco for the supply of Uranium Ore Concentrates. 
    • This is a major boost for India's Viksit Bharat vision to achieve 100 GW of nuclear power capacity by 2047.
  • Critical Minerals & Clean Energy: A Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) was signed to develop secure and resilient critical mineral supply chains, aligning with the G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan.
    • Canada announced it will officially join both the International Solar Alliance (ISA) and the Global Biofuels Alliance (GBA).
    • An MoU was established to share best practices and build capacity in energy storage, solar, wind, and biomass/bio-energy.
  • Defense and Security: The two nations announced the establishment of the first-ever India-Canada Defence Dialogue to foster strategic security discussions.
  • Political and Diplomatic Ties: The India-Canada Parliament Friendship Group was established to enhance political networking. 
    • Additionally, India formally announced its support for Canada to join the Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA) as a Dialogue Partner.
  • Private Sector Engagement: The India-Canada CEO Forum has been reconstituted to serve as a key platform for deepening private-sector collaboration across priority industries.
  • Innovation and Talent:  The India-Canada-Australia Trilateral MoU was signed to advance cooperation in emerging technologies and innovation under the Australia–Canada–India Technology and Innovation (ACITI) Partnership
    • An MoU between All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE)  and Canada's Mitacs was signed to provide 300 fully-funded research internships for Indian students. 
    • A Declaration of Intent was signed to establish a joint Joint Pulse Protein Centre of Excellence at National Institute of Food Technology, Entrepreneurship, and Management Kundli (NIFTEM-K). 
      • This center will focus on advanced protein extraction and fortified food products to address micronutrient deficiencies.

Importance  of Uranium for India’s Nuclear Energy Programme

  • Primary Nuclear Fuel: Uranium is the main fuel used in nuclear reactors for electricity generation.
    • India plans to increase nuclear power capacity from about 9 GW currently to 100 GW by 2047. 
    • Nuclear energy provides a reliable, low-carbon power source that supports India’s clean energy transition. Long-term uranium contracts ensure a stable supply for upcoming nuclear reactors.
  • Need for Uranium Imports: Indian uranium ore contains 0.02%–0.45% uranium, much lower than the global average of 1–2%. India consumes 1,500–2,000 tonnes of uranium per year. 
    • Annual demand may rise to about 5,400 tonnes with the expansion of nuclear power capacity.
    • Some Canadian mines have ore grades as high as 15%, making them far more efficient.
    • Due to lower ore quality, domestic uranium extraction is more expensive than imports.
    • Over 70% of India’s uranium requirements are currently met through imports.
  • Diversification of Uranium Imports:
    • International Suppliers: India is importing uranium from several countries to ensure fuel security, including Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Canada, and Russia.
    • Reactor-Linked Supply Arrangements: Russia provides a lifetime uranium fuel supply for reactors at the Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant in Tamil Nadu.
    • Exploring New Suppliers: India is also considering future supply agreements with countries such as Australia and the United States.
      • The Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India Act, 2025 enables greater private sector participation in India’s nuclear sector, facilitating collaboration with global partners including the US.
    • Overseas Mining Opportunities: Indian companies like Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL) are exploring the possibility of investing in or mining uranium deposits abroad to secure long-term fuel supplies.

What is the Significance of India-Canada Relations?

  • Strategic Partnership: The two nations share over 75 years of diplomatic relations, which were formally upgraded to a "Strategic Partnership" in 2018.
  • Economic Complementarity:  Canada aims to reduce dependence on the US market. India provides a vast, fast-growing market and a massive skilled talent pool, while Canada offers advanced technology, natural resources (potash, uranium, critical minerals), and capital. 
    • Canadian Pension Funds have cumulatively invested over USD 75 billion in India and are increasingly viewing India as a favourable destination for investments.
    • In 2024, India was Canada’s 7th largest trading partner, with two-way trade worth USD 30.9 billion, and India maintaining a trade surplus in goods. 
      • Top Canadian exports to India: Vegetables, mineral fuels and oils, wood pulp, fertilisers, paper and paperboard 
      • Top Indian exports to Canada: Pharmaceuticals, machinery, electronics, precious stones and metals, iron and steel products. 
  • Geopolitical Alignment: As two vibrant democracies, both nations share converging interests in maintaining a rules-based, free, and open Indo-Pacific. 
    • Canada’s Indo-Pacific Strategy specifically identifies India as a critical partner.
  • Diaspora and Soft Power: The Indian diaspora in Canada is over 1.8 million strong (around 4% of the population), acting as a vital bridge for people-to-people and economic ties. 
    • Furthermore, India remains the top source country for international students in Canada.
  • Multilateral Alignment: Both nations work closely together to address global challenges in major international forums, including the United Nations (UN), World Trade Organization (WTO), and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO).
  • Security Cooperation: It is anchored in the Joint Working Group on Counter Terrorism (1997) and the Framework for Cooperation on Countering Terrorism (2018), while legal collaboration is strengthened through the Extradition Treaty (1987) and the Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty (1994).

Measures to Strengthen India–Canada Relations

Khalistani Extremism: India remains concerned about anti-India separatist activities operating from Canadian soil and attacks on Indian consulates and diaspora members.

Diaspora to Shape India’s Next Growth Chapter

02-09-2026

For Prelims:  Remittances, Non-Resident Indians, Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs), Digital Public Infrastructure, Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs), Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act (FCRA), 2010

Why in News? 

India’s 35-million-plus global diaspora is increasingly emerging as a potential source of long-term investment and growth capital, beyond its traditional role as a major source of remittances.

  • India received USD 143.6 billion in remittances in FY26, making it the world’s largest recipient. With stronger economic fundamentals, expanding capital markets and improved digital infrastructure, Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) are increasingly exploring investment opportunities in India.

Summary

  • India’s 35-million-plus diaspora is increasingly shifting from being primarily a source of remittances to becoming a source of long-term investment, growth capital, entrepreneurship and technological expertise. Digital infrastructure such as UPI, GIFT IFSC, along with instruments such as AIFs, REITs and InvITs, is facilitating greater diaspora participation in India’s growth.
  • However, challenges such as brain drain, restrictive immigration policies, migrant-worker vulnerability, generational disconnect, regulatory hurdles, high remittance costs and limited political participation constrain its potential. India can address these through diaspora bonds, brain-circulation frameworks, stronger labour protections, sector-specific diaspora councils and deeper digital financial integration.

How the Diaspora is Shaping India’s Growth?

  • From Remittances to Growth Capital: India remains the world’s largest recipient of remittances. Remittances contribute over 10% of Gross Current Account Receipts, supporting the Current Account and foreign exchange reserves strengthening India’s external-sector resilience. 

    • The diaspora is increasingly moving beyond family support towards structured investment and growth capital.

    • This reflects growing confidence in India as a long-term investment destination, with the diaspora increasingly contributing to investment, entrepreneurship and economic growth.
  • Shift to Advanced Economies: The US has overtaken the Gulf as the single largest source (accounting for ~27.7%) of remittances. 
    • Advanced economies now contribute over 51% of total inflows, reflecting the higher earning capacity of the knowledge-based diaspora. 
  • Diversification Across Modern Asset Classes: Previously, NRI wealth was disproportionately locked in assets like real estate, gold, or traditional bank deposits.
    • Today, diaspora capital is actively deepening India's capital markets. They are channeling funds into advanced financial vehicles like Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs), directly funding infrastructure, renewable energy, and the manufacturing ecosystem.
    • Their growing participation has contributed to India's booming equity market (reaching a USD 5.18 trillion capitalization in July 2026).
  • Capitalizing on India's Macroeconomic Rebound: They are capitalizing on the formalization driven by the GST, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), and manufacturing boosts via PLI schemes.
    • The influx of global Indian capital is accelerated by a cleaned-up, well-capitalized banking sector and robust macroeconomic indicators, including inflation control and forex reserves exceeding $700 billion.
  • Digital & Institutional Infrastructure: The deployment of India's Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) specifically Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, and the Account Aggregator framework has eliminated the geographical friction of cross-border investing. 
    • Electronic KYC (e-KYC) and digital onboarding have made it seamless for NRIs to deploy capital.
    • Regulatory upgrades by the RBI and SEBI have strengthened market integrity. Concurrently, the operationalization of the GIFT IFSC (Gujarat International Finance Tec-City) is providing the diaspora with a globally competitive, dollar-denominated financial gateway directly within India.
  • Brain Gain and Technological Transfer: Indian professionals leading global tech giants and R&D labs are instrumental in facilitating knowledge transfer. This is pivotal for sunrise initiatives like the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) and the National Quantum Mission. 
  • Strategic and Diplomatic Leverage: The diaspora serves as an organic soft-power asset. Their growing political and corporate representation in countries like the US, UK, and Canada helps shape favorable foreign policies, secure strategic partnerships, and influence bilateral trade agreements.

    What are the Key Challenges in Harnessing Diaspora for Growth?

    • Economic Vulnerability of Low-Skilled Workers: A significant portion of the Indian diaspora consists of semi-skilled and low-skilled workers in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries. 
      • They face severe job insecurity tied to volatile oil prices and remain subjected to exploitative labor practices, such as the restrictive "Kafala" system, which limits their mobility and wage security.
    • Restrictive Immigration and Visa Policies: Advanced economies are increasingly adopting protectionist measures. 
      • Tightened visa norms and rising costs (such as the steep hikes in H-1B visa fees in the US) directly restrict the mobility, earnings, and professional growth of highly skilled Indian talent in the West.
    • Brain Drain vs. Brain Circulation: India continues to lose top-tier scientific, medical, and engineering talent to countries offering better research ecosystems and remuneration. 
      • Domestic institutions lack the absorptive capacity, funding, and infrastructure required to incentivize these professionals to return or meaningfully contribute to India's domestic R&D.
    • Generational Disconnect: Second and third-generation Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) often possess weaker cultural and economic ties to India compared to first-generation migrants. 
      • Without highly targeted engagement models, their venture capital and intellectual expertise do not flow back to India.
    • Limited Political Participation: Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) face a democratic deficit because they are required to be physically present in India to vote. 
      • The lack of postal or online voting alternatives restricts their ability to influence policy formulation, leading to a sense of political exclusion.
    • Philanthropic and Regulatory Hurdles: Despite a strong willingness among the diaspora to invest in India's social development, their efforts are often frustrated by bureaucratic red tape and stringent compliance requirements under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act (FCRA), 2010.
    • Data and Policy Fragmentation: India lacks a comprehensive, unified, and continuously updated database of its overseas citizens. 
      • While grievance mechanisms like the e-Migrate and Madad portals exist, their scattered nature often delays rapid response during geopolitical crises or for individual legal aid.
    • Racism and Xenophobia: A rising tide of anti-immigrant sentiment and targeted hate crimes in destination countries (such as recent incidents involving students in Australia, the US, and Canada) creates an atmosphere of insecurity, dampening diaspora morale and straining consular resources.
    • Asymmetric Remittance Dependence: Relying heavily on remittances to manage the CAD masks structural weaknesses in domestic export competitiveness.
      • Remittances are essentially private consumption transfers that boost local demand, but they do not substitute for long-term capital investments (FDI) required for industrial capacity building. 
    • Cost of Remittance: Despite digital advancements, the global average cost of sending money via traditional banking channels remains higher than the UN Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 10.c) target of 3%. 
    • Informal Channels: The parallel existence of informal channels (such as Hawala) continues to pose a challenge. They are utilized to circumvent KYC documentation and formal forex channels, posing a risk of money laundering. 
    • Illegal Recruitment and Human Trafficking: Fraudulent recruitment agents and touts may lure Indians with false promises of jobs abroad, leading to exploitation, wage theft, forced labour and unsafe working conditions. 
      • Cases of Indians being wrongly recruited or coerced into supporting the Russia–Ukraine war effort highlight the risks associated with fraudulent overseas recruitment. 
      • Irregular migration through ‘donkey routes’ exposes Indians to trafficking, exploitation, detention and loss of life, while harming India’s international image. 

    India’s Initiatives to Boost Diaspora Engagement

    • Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) Scheme: Provides lifelong, multiple-entry visas to eligible overseas Indians and grants parity with NRIs in specified economic, financial and educational matters, while excluding rights such as voting and agricultural land ownership. 
    • Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (PBD): A biennial diaspora convention held on 9 January that provides a platform for diaspora engagement, networking and diplomacy and includes the Pravasi Bharatiya Samman Award. 
    • VAJRA (Visiting Advanced Joint Research) Scheme: Enables overseas Indian scientists to conduct high-end collaborative research in Indian public-funded institutions. 
    • PRABHASS (Pravasi Bharatiya Academic and Scientific Sampark): Connects the global Indian Science and Technology (S&T) community with Indian ministries to help address domestic technological challenges. 

    Boost Remittances

    • FCNR(B) Swap Facility (2025–26): RBI provides banks with a USD/INR swap facility against fresh FCNR(B) deposits, reducing banks’ hedging costs and encouraging mobilisation of NRI foreign-currency deposits.
    • UPI Internationalisation: India is expanding UPI-based cross-border payment connectivity with partner countries, allowing Indians abroad and foreign users to make/receive payments through linked payment systems.
    • FDI Parity: Investments by Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs) on a non-repatriable basis are treated on par with domestic investments, exempting them from stringent Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) caps. 
    • India–UAE Local Currency Settlement (LCS) Framework (2023): Enables INR–AED settlement for cross-border transactions, reducing dependence on intermediary currencies and lowering conversion costs.
    • Foreign Exchange Management (Manner of Receipt and Payment) Regulations, 2023: Expands the permissible modes of cross-border receipts and payments in INR and foreign currencies, facilitating local-currency transactions.

    How Can India Better Leverage its Diaspora for Growth?

    • Globalizing the 'India Stack': Expanding the UPI-RuPay footprint to the top remittance corridors (including the US, UK, and Canada) is imperative to sustainably democratize cross-border transactions and eliminate hidden intermediary fees.
    • Reviving Diaspora Bonds: India has previously mobilised overseas savings through instruments such as India Development Bonds (1991), Resurgent India Bonds (1998) and India Millennium Deposits (2000). 
      • The government can consider reviving a modern diaspora bond to channel overseas savings into infrastructure, green energy and other long-term development projects. 
    • Institutionalizing 'Brain Circulation': Instead of insisting on permanent return migration, India should create dynamic, flexible frameworks that integrate overseas academics into Indian universities and R&D ecosystems through visiting fellowships, joint patents, and remote mentorships.
    • Establishing Sector-Specific Diaspora Advisory Councils: Create formal advisory boards comprising global Indian corporate leaders to guide policy formulation in highly specialized sectors such as Artificial Intelligence, green hydrogen, and biotechnology.
    • Diaspora for Economic Diplomacy: Engage influential Indian-origin professionals and business leaders for policy advocacy, trade and strategic partnerships. The Indian diaspora in the US played a vital role in lobbying for the Indo-US Civil Nuclear Agreement, finalised in 2008.
    • Strengthening Blue-Collar Protections: Aggressively pursue comprehensive Social Security Agreements (SSAs) and transparent bilateral labor mobility pacts with the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) nations to safeguard the rights and financial security of semi-skilled workers.
    • Revamping the OCI Framework: Transition from a purely cultural engagement model to a transactional and opportunity-driven investment framework. Offering streamlined regulatory clearances for diaspora-led venture funds will help attract the capital of the younger, second-generation demographic.
    • Targeted Financial Literacy: Enhancing digital financial literacy among low-skilled migrant workers in the Gulf is vital to transition them from expensive physical money transfer operators to low-cost digital platforms.

    Conclusion

    The diaspora is no longer just a demographic dividend outside India's borders; it is a critical instrument for capital formation. By combining their global wealth with India’s mature digital public infrastructure (DPI) and strong macroeconomic fundamentals, the diaspora is directly financing India's transition to a developed economy.

       VAJRA (Visiting Advanced Joint Research) Scheme enables overseas Indian scientists to undertake high-end collaborative research in Indian public-funded institutions.
