Issues with River-Linking Projects

26-09-2026

Source: TH 

Why in News? 

At the Southern Zonal Council meeting in Mamallapuram, the Union Home Minister emphasised the need to resolve water disputes in southern India and proposed river interlinking from the Brahmaputra to the Godavari and Cauvery as a long-term water security measure. 

  • The issue gains importance amid disputes like Pennaiyar and Mekedatu, highlighting challenges in inter-State water management and the debate between large-scale water projects and demand-side conservation measures. 

Summary 

  • River interlinking aims to transfer water from supposedly surplus river basins to water-deficient regions through canals, reservoirs and diversion structures. 
  • While projects such as the Mullaperiyar Dam, Parambikulam-Aliyar Project, Krishna Water Supply Project and Indira Gandhi Canal Project demonstrate benefits of inter-basin transfers, large-scale river linking faces complex environmental, social and federal challenges. 
  • Experts argue that India’s water crisis cannot be solved only through supply augmentation. Greater emphasis is required on water conservation, demand-side management, groundwater regulation and efficient agricultural practices.

What are the Key Facts About Interlinking of Rivers (ILR)?

  • About: The Interlinking of Rivers (ILR) programme is a large-scale water management initiative aimed at transferring water from water-surplus basins to water-deficit regions through a network of reservoirs, canals and diversion structures. 
  • Objectives: To improve water security, expand irrigation, reduce flood and drought risks, generate hydropower and support balanced regional development. 
  • Historical Background: The idea of linking rivers dates back to the 19th century, with Sir Arthur Cotton proposing river-based irrigation and navigation schemes. The concept gained momentum after independence due to concerns over uneven rainfall distribution and recurring floods and droughts. 
  • National Perspective Plan (NPP): Formulated in 1980 by the Ministry of Water Resources, it identified 30 major links (14 in the Himalayan component and 16 in the Peninsular component). 
  • Executing Agency: The National Water Development Agency (NWDA) was established in 1982 to study the feasibility of river-linking projects.

What are the Key Arguments in Favour and Against River Interlinking (ILR)? 

Arguments in Favour 

  • Enhancing Water Security: River interlinking can help transfer water from surplus basins to water-deficit regions, reducing regional disparities in water availability. For example, the Ken–Betwa Link Project aims to provide irrigation and drinking water benefits to drought-prone areas of Bundelkhand (Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh). 
  • Improving Agricultural Productivity: Additional irrigation potential created through inter-basin water transfer can increase agricultural output, support crop diversification and improve farmers’ incomes. For example, the Indira Gandhi Canal Project transformed parts of the arid Rajasthan region by providing irrigation facilities. 
  • Mitigating Flood and Drought Risks: River linking can help manage excess water during floods by diverting water to water-scarce regions, thereby reducing the severity of both floods and droughts. Proponents argue that linking major rivers could improve India’s long-term water resilience. 
  • Supporting Drinking Water and Industrial Needs: Interlinking projects can enhance the availability of water for urban centres, industries and rural communities facing water shortages. The Krishna Water Supply Project is an example of transferring river water to meet the drinking water requirements of Hyderabad. 
  • Generating Economic Benefits: Large water infrastructure projects can create employment opportunities, improve rural infrastructure and support regional economic development through better irrigation, navigation and allied activities. 
  • Promoting Integrated River Basin Management: River interlinking can encourage coordinated planning of water resources across political boundaries and support a more integrated approach towards managing India’s rivers. 

Arguments Against   

  • Ecological Concerns: River ecosystems are highly interconnected, and altering natural river flows can affect aquatic biodiversity, wetlands, sediment transport and downstream ecosystems. 
    • Changes in water flow may disturb fish migration patterns, groundwater recharge processes and ecological balance in river basins. 
    • For example, Kerala has opposed the Pamba–Achankovil–Vaippar Link Project, arguing that it could adversely affect the Vembanad wetland ecosystem. 
  • Inter-State Water Conflicts: River waters are shared resources among multiple States, making allocation and usage a sensitive issue. 
    • The Pennaiyar River dispute highlights these challenges, where Tamil Nadu demanded a tribunal over alleged violation of the 1892 inter-State agreement by Karnataka. 
    • Similarly, the Mekedatu dam project proposed by Karnataka has faced strong opposition from Tamil Nadu due to concerns over downstream water availability. 
  • Federal and Governance Challenges: Water management requires strong cooperation between the Centre and States, as disputes often involve competing regional interests. 
    • Although the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 provides a mechanism for resolving disputes through tribunals, delays in tribunal formation and implementation of decisions remain major concerns. 
    • Lack of consensus among States can delay projects and affect their timely completion. 
  • Social and Rehabilitation Issues: Large-scale river-linking projects require significant land acquisition, which may lead to displacement of local communities, particularly tribal populations. Ensuring fair rehabilitation and protecting the livelihood rights of affected communities remain major challenges. 
    • The Ken–Betwa Link Project has faced resistance from tribal communities due to concerns regarding displacement and environmental impacts. 
  • Economic and Technical Challenges: River interlinking projects involve high financial costs, complex engineering requirements and long gestation periods. Construction of large dams, canals and reservoirs requires extensive investment and continuous maintenance. 
    • Uncertainty regarding future rainfall patterns due to climate change also raises questions about the availability of “surplus” water for transfer. 
  • Climate Change and Sustainability Concerns: Climate change is altering rainfall patterns, glacier behaviour and river flows, making assumptions about permanent water surplus or deficit increasingly uncertain. 
    • Overdependence on large-scale engineering solutions may divert attention from sustainable approaches such as water conservation, watershed management and demand-side management. 

Way Forward 

  • Strengthen institutional mechanisms for water governance: The National River Linking Project (NRLP) and Interlinking of Rivers (ILR) Programme should be implemented through a scientific, transparent and participatory approach. The National Water Development Agency (NWDA) should undertake detailed basin-level studies to ensure that water transfers are ecologically and socially sustainable. 
  • Ensure effective dispute resolution and cooperative federalism: The Centre and States should strengthen mechanisms under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 and ensure timely resolution of disputes. The constitutional framework under Article 262 should be effectively utilised to promote equitable and cooperative management of inter-State rivers. 
  • Adopt a balanced approach towards river-linking projects: Projects such as the Ken–Betwa Link Project should integrate environmental impact assessments, climate risk analysis and rehabilitation measures to balance developmental objectives with ecological sustainability. 
  • Prioritise demand-side water management: India should shift from excessive dependence on large supply-side projects towards efficient water utilisation through micro-irrigation, groundwater conservation, rainwater harvesting, watershed management and restoration of traditional water bodies. 
  • Promote community participation and integrated river basin management: Local communities, farmers and civil society should be actively involved in water conservation efforts. A basin-based approach involving States, experts and stakeholders can ensure sustainable management of rivers while reducing conflicts over water sharing. 

Conclusion 

River interlinking can contribute to water security in selected cases, but it cannot be viewed as a universal solution to India’s water challenges. A sustainable water strategy must move beyond large supply-side interventions towards conservation, efficient utilisation, groundwater management and cooperative federalism. Ensuring water security requires protecting both rivers and the communities that depend on them.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

Q1. What is Interlinking of Rivers (ILR)? 
Interlinking of Rivers (ILR) refers to the transfer of water from water-surplus river basins to water-deficit regions through reservoirs, canals and pipelines to address regional water imbalances. 

Q2. What are the objectives of River Interlinking? 
River interlinking aims to reduce regional water scarcity, enhance irrigation potential, mitigate floods and droughts, and improve water availability for domestic, agricultural and industrial purposes. 

Q3. What is the National River Linking Project (NRLP)? 

The National River Linking Project (NRLP) is a government initiative aimed at connecting river basins through canals and reservoirs to transfer water from surplus regions to deficit areas. 

Q4. How does River Interlinking affect ecology? 
River interlinking may disturb natural river systems by altering water flow, sediment transport, aquatic habitats and wetland ecosystems. 

Q5. How is River Interlinking related to cooperative federalism? 
Since rivers are shared among multiple States, successful implementation of ILR requires cooperation between the Centre and States through mechanisms under Article 262 and the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956.

Mekedatu Dam

25-11-2025

Source: TH

Karnataka has announced that it would submit a revised Detailed Project Report (DPR) to the Centre for the Mekedatu dam across the Cauvery river.

  • This comes after Tamil Nadu raised concerns over Karnataka's Mekedatu dam, citing Cauvery water disputes, excessive water storage, and irregular water release.

Mekedatu Dam

  • About: It involves constructing a balancing reservoir near Kanakapura, Karnataka, at the deep gorge of Mekedatu where the Cauvery and Arkavathi rivers meet. 
    • It aims to provide drinking water to Bengaluru and nearby areas and generate 400 MW of hydroelectric power.
  • Controversy: Tamil Nadu, the lower riparian state, strongly opposes the project, fearing reduced downstream water flow and is central to the Cauvery River water sharing dispute.

Cauvery River Water Dispute

  • About the Dispute: The dispute involves Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Puducherry. The Cauvery river originates in Karnataka, flows through Tamil Nadu, with major tributaries from Kerala.
  • Historical Genesis: The dispute is over 150 years old, originating from 1892 and 1924 agreements between the Madras Presidency and Mysore, that required the upper riparian state (Karnataka) to obtain consent from the lower riparian state (Tamil Nadu) for construction projects.
  • Renewed Conflict: A fresh dispute began in 1974 when Karnataka started diverting water without Tamil Nadu's consent, violating previous agreements.
  • Resolution Mechanism: The Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT), established in 1990, delivered its Final Order in 2007, specifying water-sharing among all riparian states. 
    • In the State of Tamil Nadu vs. State of Karnataka (2018), the SC declared the Cauvery river a national asset and largely upheld the CWDT award.
    • The Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA) was created after the Supreme Court’s 2018 verdict to ensure the implementation of the CWDT’s final order. 

Note: Water is a State subject under Entry 17 of the State List, but the regulation and development of inter-state rivers come under Entry 56 of the Union List.

Mekedatu Project

23-06-2026

Source: TH

Recently, the Tamil Nadu Assembly unanimously adopted a resolution opposing Karnataka’s Mekedatu dam proposal across the Cauvery. Karnataka, however, has said it is open to talks and maintains that the project will not affect Tamil Nadu’s allocated Cauvery water share. 

  • About: The Mekedatu project is proposed by Karnataka across the Cauvery river, within Karnataka’s territory, near Mekedatu in Ramanagara district. It is planned as a balancing reservoir to regulate Cauvery river flows and improve water management. 
  • Main Objectives: The project aims to meet drinking water requirements of Bengaluru and surrounding areas, regulate release to Tamil Nadu at Biligundlu, and generate 400 MW of hydroelectric power.  
  • Concerns: Tamil Nadu argues that no new dam or storage project should be constructed in the Cauvery basin without the consent of other basin States(Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Puducherry) and approval from the Union government and competent authorities. 
  • Supreme Court Ruling: In November 2025, the Supreme Court rejected Tamil Nadu’s challenge at that stage, observing that the issue was still at the Detailed Project Report (DPR) stage and had to be examined by expert bodies such as the Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA) and Central Water Commission (CWC). 

Cauvery (Kaveri) River

  • About: Kaveri River is one of South India's most significant rivers, often referred to as the "Ganga of the South" (Dakshin Bharat ki Ganga). 
  • Origin: It rises at Talakaveri in the Brahmagiri Range of the Western Ghats (Kodagu district, Karnataka).
  • Course: It flows for about 800 km in a southeasterly direction through Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, ultimately emptying into the Bay of Bengal near Poompuhar.
  • Key Tributaries:
    • Left Bank: Harangi, Hemavati, Shimsha, Arkavati.
    • Right Bank: Lakshmana Tirtha, Kabini, Suvarnavathi, Bhavani, Noyyal, Amaravati.
  • Key Dams: Krishna Raja Sagara (KRS) in Karnataka and Mettur Dam (Stanley Reservoir) in Tamil Nadu.

Polavaram- Banakacherla Project Dispute

02-08-2025

For Prelims: Krishna-Godavari water-sharing issues, Inter-State River Water Disputes, Sarkaria Commission,  

For Mains: Inter State Relations, Inter-State River Water Disputes, issues related to Sharing of water Resources. 

Source: IE 

Why in News? 

The Central Government has decided to constitute a high-level committee to resolve the conflict between Andhra Pradesh (AP) and Telangana over the proposed Polavaram–Banakacherla Link Project (PBLP) and other Krishna-Godavari water-sharing issues. 

What is Polavaram-Banakacherla Link Project? 

Polavaram-Banakacherla Link Project: 

  • Objective: The project aims to address water scarcity in the drought-prone Rayalaseema region by diverting 200 TMC of surplus Godavari floodwaters to the Krishna and Penna river basins. 
  • Water Transfer Mechanism: Water will be drawn from the Polavaram Dam, pass through the Prakasam Barrage, lifted to the Bollapalli reservoir, and tunneled under the Nallamala forest to the Banakacherla reservoir in Rayalaseema. 
  • Significance: The PBLP will enhance irrigation, ensure drinking water supply, and promote agricultural sustainability, improving water security and livelihoods in southern Andhra Pradesh. 

Key Challenges Related to the Project 

  • Alleged Violation of 2014 Act: Telangana claims Andhra Pradesh violated the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014, which mandates prior approval from the Apex Council, Krishna River Management Board (KRMB), and Central Water Commission (CWC) for any new inter-state river projects. 
  • Disputed Surplus Water Claims: Telangana disputes Andhra Pradesh’s claim of 200 TMC “surplus” Godavari floodwaters, stating that the allocation has not been adjudicated or approved by any competent authority or tribunal. 
  • Environmental Concerns & Pending Clearances: While the Polavaram Project received an Environmental Clearance in 2005, the Expert Appraisal Committee (EAC) noted the need for fresh environmental scrutiny and Central Water Commission (CWC) consultation due to submergence disputes with Odisha and Chhattisgarh. 
  • Unauthorised Inter-Basin Diversion: Telangana objects to the diversion of Godavari waters into the Krishna basin without mutual consent, warning it could reduce water availability for its projects. 
  • Breach of Cooperative Federalism: Telangana views Andhra Pradesh’s unilateral actions as a breach of cooperative federalism, bypassing essential consensus mechanisms for managing shared river resources. 

What is the Mechanism for Inter-State River Water Disputes Resolution in India? 

  • Constitutional Provisions:  
    • Article 262 empowers Parliament to enact laws for the adjudication of disputes related to the use, distribution, or control of inter-state river waters. 
      • Article 262(1): Allows Parliament to provide a legal mechanism for such adjudication. 
      • Article 262(2): Permits Parliament to bar the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other courts over these disputes. 
    • Entry 17, State List (List II): Grants states control over water-related subjects like irrigation, canals, drainage, water supply, and hydropower. 
    • Entry 56, Union List (List I): Empowers the Union Government to regulate and develop inter-state rivers and river valleys in the public interest, as declared by Parliament. 
  • Statutory Provisions: 
    • River Boards Act, 1956: Empowers the Central Government to establish River Boards, in consultation with states, for the coordinated development and management of inter-state rivers and valleys. However, no River Board has been constituted under this Act so far. 
    • Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956: Provides a legal framework for resolving disputes between states over inter-state river waters. The process includes: 
      • Initial negotiation by the Centre with concerned states. 
      • If unresolved, a Water Disputes Tribunal is constituted, whose award is final and binding. 
      • The tribunal's decision cannot be challenged in courts, though procedural or functional lapses may be reviewed judicially. 
      • The act was amended in 2002, to include the major recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission. 
        • The amendments mandated a one-year time frame to set up the water disputes tribunal and also a 3-year time frame to give a decision. 
  • Role of Supreme Court: Though Article 262(2) bars the Supreme Court and other courts’ jurisdiction over inter-state water disputes, the Supreme Court has intervened in related matters (under Article 136), such as interpreting or implementing tribunal awards. 
    • Eg: Mahadayi Water Dispute (2018), SC settled water allocation between Karnataka, Goa, and Maharashtra, and directed implementation of the tribunal award. 

What are the Key Issues Related to Inter-State Water Disputes? 

  • Delayed Tribunal Awards: Tribunals often take decades to resolve disputes (e.g., Cauvery, Krishna). Also absence of time-bound mechanisms for adjudication and implementation hampers early resolution. Eg: Cauvery Tribunal (1990–2007): 17-year delay in final verdict. 
  • Lack of Credible and Real-Time Data: Disputes often rest on conflicting data from states, as there is no independent basin authority to validate claims. Eg: Mahanadi dispute (Odisha vs. Chhattisgarh) over unverified water flow data. 
  • Judicial Overlap Despite Article 262: Despite the bar on Supreme Court jurisdiction under Article 262, states often approach the Court under Articles 131/136, leading to legal ambiguity and parallel proceedings. 
  • Weak enforcement: Tribunal awards need central notification to become binding, but enforcement is often delayed or politically stalled. 

What Measures Should be Taken to Effectively Resolve Inter-State Water Disputes? 

  • Time-Bound Adjudication: Amend Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 to fix  timelines for adjudication of disputes and establish a permanent tribunal with technical experts. 
  • Reliable Data & River Basin Authority: Set up an independent river basin organization for transparent, real-time water data to prevent disputes based on conflicting claims. 
  • Promote Cooperative Federalism: Encourage pre-litigation inter-state negotiations and utilize platforms like the Inter-State Council to build trust and consensus.
