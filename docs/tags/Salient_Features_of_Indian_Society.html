Salient Features of Indian Society

Six-Pocket Syndrome

22-10-2025

For Prelims: Six-Pocket Syndrome, United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, National Commission for Protection of Child Rights 

For Mains: Changing family dynamics, Role of schools, parents, and society in building emotional intelligence

Source:IE 

Why in News? 

A Kaun Banega Crorepati episode featuring a child’s overconfident behaviour towards Amitabh Bachchan has sparked debate on overindulgent parenting among Generation Alpha (people born from 2010-2024/25), which experts link to the “Six-Pocket Syndrome.” 

What is Six-Pocket Syndrome? 

  • About:  The Six-Pocket Syndrome, a term from China’s one-child policy era (1979-2015), describes how one child was doted on by six adults (two parents and four grandparents) who poured all their affection and resources into them. 
    • This overindulgence fostered entitlement and a sense that every wish deserves to be fulfilled, leaving little room for discipline, patience, or empathy. 
  • Indian Context:  In India, the Six-Pocket Syndrome is common in urban middle-class families, where working parents and indulgent grandparents shower children with attention, gifts, and leniency.  
    • This overcompensation fosters a belief in the right to demand and be rewarded instantly, without understanding effort, boundaries, or consequences.  
  • Sociological Perspective: Reflects the shift from a responsibility-based society (where children contribute and adapt) to a right-based society (where children feel entitled to comfort and attention). 
    • Erving Goffman’s “Dramaturgical Perspective”:  Explains how modern children, shaped by family and media, learn to perform roles that attract validation rather than build character.  
      • Childhood becomes a stage for social approval rather than moral learning. 
    • Zygmunt Bauman’s “Liquid Modernity”: States that society now prioritises instant gratification, fluid identities, and social visibility over long-term emotional grounding. 
  • Psychological and Behavioural Effects: 
    • Low frustration tolerance: The child cannot handle rejection, denial, or criticism. 
    • Reward-demanding behaviour: They associate love with material rewards and expect instant gratification. 
    • Emotional immaturity: Limited capacity for patience, empathy, or self-control. 
    • Dependency and fragility: The child struggles with autonomy and resilience when facing failure or peer pressure. 
      • In severe cases, such children may exhibit tantrums, aggression, or anxiety when denied their wishes. 
    • Long-term Consequences: Children raised under the Six-Pocket Syndrome often develop ego-centric behaviour, which can strain relationships.  
      • Over time, this may increase their vulnerability to aggression, substance abuse, and mental health challenges. 

What are the Challenges Facing Generation Alpha? 

  • Impatience and Instant Gratification: Growing up in a digital world of one-click solutions and fast content, Generation Alpha struggles with delayed gratification. 
    • They expect quick results and often lack patience, persistence, and tolerance for failure. 
  • Overconfidence and Entitlement: Constant validation both at home and online breed excessive self-assurance without competence. 
    • This results in low humility, difficulty accepting criticism, and unrealistic self-perception. 
  • Erosion of Behavioural Norms: With social interactions increasingly mediated through screens, children show declining respect for elders, authority, and social etiquette. 
    • Exposure to unfiltered online content normalises informality and irreverence in language and conduct. 
  • Emotional Fragility and Mental Health Issues: Overprotection, combined with constant comparison on social media, weakens emotional resilience. 
    • Rising levels of anxiety, irritability, and self-esteem issues are evident among school-age children. 
  • Declining Social and Empathic Skills: Reduced face-to-face interaction limits their ability to read emotions, negotiate differences, or handle conflict. 
    • Virtual connections often replace real friendships, creating emotional isolation. 
  • Consumerism: Influencer culture promotes materialism and a performative lifestyle, where self-worth depends on visibility and possessions. 
  • Overdependence on Technology: Early digital exposure leads to reduced creativity, shorter attention spans, and addictive behaviour. 
    • The ability to think critically or solve problems independently is gradually diminishing. 
  • Data and Privacy Risks: Generation Alpha is the first fully datafied generation, with their information collected from birth. 
    • This raises legal and ethical concerns under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) provisions on child rights. 

What can be a Corrective Model of Parenting and Society for Generation Alpha? 

  • Link Rewards to Effort, Not Entitlement: Children should earn privileges through achievements or responsibilities, preventing the “right-to-reward” mindset seen in the Six-Pocket Syndrome. 
    • Supported by UNCRC article 5, which emphasises guidance and direction in line with the child’s evolving capacities. 
  • Instil Emotional Resilience: Exposure to controlled failure, constructive criticism, and problem-solving builds patience, adaptability, and emotional regulation. 
    • India’s National Policy for Children (2013) encourages resilience-building programmes in schools and communities. 
  • Promote Responsibility: Assign chores, peer group activities, and community engagement to teach empathy, cooperation, and civic responsibility which aids in development of social responsibility and respect for societal values. 
  • Balance Affection with Boundaries: Families must prevent overindulgence and impulsive entitlement. Extended family support should reinforce discipline rather than compensate for indulgence. 
    • Indian legal frameworks like the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, stress the role of family in guiding children’s moral development. 
  • Regulate Digital Exposure: Monitor online activity, social media use, and reality TV exposure. 
    • Information Technology Rules, 2021 and National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) guidelines mandate protection of children from cyberbullying, exploitative content, and online harassment. 
    • International practices, like UNICEF’s Guidelines for Industry on Child Online Protection, encourage age-appropriate exposure and digital literacy. 
    • Schools, communities, and digital platforms must work collaboratively to create safe, nurturing, and disciplined environments. 

Conclusion 

Generation Alpha is technologically advanced but emotionally underprepared. The challenge for society is to raise not just smart children, but responsible, empathetic, and grounded citizens balancing digital fluency with emotional intelligence and respect for shared values.

Advancing Women’s Employment and Economic Empowerment in India

16-10-2026

Source: PIB 

Why in News?

The rising female labour force participation in India has shifted attention towards improving the quality of women’s employment, with greater emphasis on job security, social protection, safer workplaces, financial independence and economic empowerment. 

What are the Key Trends About Women’s Employment in India? 

  • Rising Labour Force Participation: According to  the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) the female LFPR for women aged 15 years and above increased from 23.3% in 2017–18 to 40% in 2025. 
    • The Labour Force Participation Rate(LFPR) measures the proportion of the population that is either working or seeking or available for work. 
  • Increasing Employment: The female Worker Population Ratio (WPR) rose from 22% in 2017–18 to 38.8% in 2025, indicating an increase in the proportion of women actually employed. 
    • The WPR measures the proportion of employed persons in the total population. 
  • Large Employment Base: An estimated 20 crore women aged 15 years and above were employed in India during 2025. 
  • Shift Towards Quality Employment: Women's economic empowerment increasingly depends on access to adequate income, job security, social protection, formalisation, financial opportunities and greater control over economic decisions. 

How are Women Contributing to Entrepreneurship and Emerging Sectors? 

  • Women’s Entrepreneurship: Women are increasingly participating in entrepreneurship, strengthening financial independence, asset creation and access to economic opportunities. 
    • Women-Owned MSMEs: As of October 2026, approximately 3.69 crore registered MSMEs were women-owned, accounting for 38% of total MSME registrations. 
    • Women in Startups: By March 2026, over 1.07 lakh recognised startups (48%) had at least one woman director or partner. 
  • Electronics Manufacturing: As of July 2026, women constituted nearly 70% of the direct workforce in mobile phone manufacturing and approximately 30% of the workforce created in the broader electronics manufacturing sector over the preceding decade. 
  • Semiconductor Industry: As of March 2025, women accounted for approximately 25% of the semiconductor workforce, including nearly one-fourth of India's chip design and engineering workforce. 
  • Civil Aviation: As of June 2025, women constituted 15% of pilots, one of the highest proportions globally, alongside 15% of air traffic controllers, 11% of flight dispatchers and 33% of Flight Operations Inspectors in the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA). 
  • STEM Research: According to the Research & Development Statistics 2025–26, women's participation in Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) research careers increased from 13% in 2000–01 to 18.6% in 2020–21 and 29% in 2023–24, reflecting their growing presence in research and innovation. 

How is the Government Supporting Women-Led Enterprises Through Formalisation? 

  • Swavalambini Scheme (2025): Launched as a pilot in February 2025 across six higher education institutions in Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Uttar Pradesh and Telangana, the scheme supports young women from business ideation to enterprise creation through entrepreneurship training, financial and market guidance, legal support and 21 weeks of mentorship. 
    • The pilot trained 1,276 participants under the Entrepreneurship Awareness Programme against a target of 1,200 and 602 participants under the Entrepreneurship Development Programme against a target of 600. 
  • Yashasvini Campaign (2024): Aims to promote women's entrepreneurship and gender parity on the Udyam Registration Portal and Udyam Assist Platform by 2029. 
    • Provides awareness, handholding and capacity-building support while promoting the mandated 3% public procurement target for women-owned Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs). 
    • By July 2026, it had facilitated over 22 lakh women-owned MSME registrations. In FY 2025–26, procurement from women-owned MSEs reached 3.8%, exceeding the 3% target.

How are Credit Schemes Improving Women’s Access to Finance? 

Financial Support for Women-Led MSMEs 

  • Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana (PMMY), 2015: PMMY provides collateral-free loans of up to ₹20 lakh to eligible micro-enterprises engaged in non-corporate, non-farm income-generating activities. 
    • By April 2026, over 57.79 crore loans had been sanctioned since inception, with women accounting for nearly two-thirds of the total. 
  • Credit Guarantee Scheme for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE): It facilitates collateral-free institutional credit to Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs), offering 90% guarantee coverage for women-led MSEs and a 10% concession on guarantee fees. 
    • As of February 2026, approximately 30.75 lakh guarantees worth ₹2.07 lakh crore had been approved for women-owned MSEs since the scheme's inception. 
  • Prime Minister's Employment Generation Programme (PMEGP): A credit-linked subsidy scheme promoting new non-farm micro-enterprises, with women eligible for 25% subsidy in urban areas and 35% in rural areas. 
    • By May 2026, women accounted for nearly 40% of micro-enterprises assisted and received approximately 45% of the total margin-money subsidy disbursed. 

Financial Support for Women-Led Startups 

  • Fund of Funds for Startups (FFS), 2016: Provides capital to SEBI-registered Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), which subsequently invest in startups through equity and equity-linked instruments. 
    • As of January 2026, supported AIFs had invested approximately ₹2,995 crore in women-led startups from 2025 onwards. 
  • Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS), 2021: SISFS provides early-stage funding through approved incubators to support proof of concept, prototype development, product trials and market entry. 
    • As of January 2026, supported incubators had approved approximately ₹294 crore for women-led startups. 
  • Credit Guarantee Scheme for Startups (CGSS), 2023: CGSS facilitates collateral-free debt financing for eligible startups through credit guarantees on loans extended by banks, financial institutions, NBFCs and SEBI-registered AIFs. 
    • As of January 2026, loans amounting to approximately ₹39 crore had been guaranteed for women-led startups. 

How are Government Initiatives Expanding Market Access for Women Entrepreneurs? 

  • Womaniya Initiative: Launched on the Government e-Marketplace (GeM), it facilitates market access for women-owned Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs) and Self-Help Groups (SHGs) by connecting them with government buyers. 
    • It enables women entrepreneurs to register and sell products such as handloom, jute, coir and home décor items through a dedicated digital interface. 
    • By July 2026, over 2.17 lakh women-owned MSEs were registered on GeM. In FY 2025–26, their total contract value exceeded ₹28,000 crore, reflecting 27.6% annual growth. 
    • Women-owned MSEs received 5.6% of the total order value on GeM, exceeding the mandated 3% public procurement target. 
  • Procurement and Marketing Support (PMS) Scheme: Supports registered MSEs in accessing markets through trade fairs, exhibitions, vendor-development programmes, e-commerce and packaging support. 
    • Women-owned MSEs are eligible for a 100% subsidy on built-up space rent for participation in eligible trade fairs. 
  • MSME Trade Enablement and Marketing (TEAM) Initiative (2024–27): Launched under the Raising and Accelerating MSME Performance (RAMP) Programme, it helps MSEs access digital markets through the Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC). 
    • Up to FY 2025–26, approximately 1,700 women-owned MSEs had registered under the initiative. 
  • Significance: These initiatives strengthen women's entrepreneurship by connecting formalisation, access to credit and market opportunities, enabling women-led enterprises to expand their customer base, increase income and achieve sustainable business growth. 

How are Skill Development Initiatives Preparing Women for Emerging Sectors? 

  • Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana 4.0 (PMKVY 4.0): The scheme emphasises training in futuristic and new-age technologies.  
    • Between 2021–22 and 2025–26, 20,393 women candidates trained in artificial intelligence, 5,927 in semiconductors and 76,671 in renewable energy. 
  • National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme (NAPS): The scheme promotes paid on-the-job apprenticeship training.  
    • During 2021–22 to 2025–26, the reported number of women candidates trained was 1,534 in artificial intelligence and 19,899 in renewable energy. 
  • Craftsman Training Scheme (CTS): Delivered through Industrial Training Institutes (ITIs), the scheme provides vocational and technical training.  
    • 512 women trained in artificial intelligence, 31 in semiconductors and 5,753 in renewable energy during 2021–22 to 2025–26. 
  • NAVYA Initiative: Launched in 2025, Nurturing Aspirations through Vocational Training for Young Adolescent Girls aims to skill 3,850 adolescent girls under PMKVY 4.0 for non-traditional and future-oriented jobs.  
  • AI Careers for Women: Launched in 2025, the programme is establishing 30 Centres of Excellence in women’s colleges across six states, supported by 150 spoke centres in Tier-II and Tier-III towns. It aims to provide 20,000 learners with practical AI training, certifications, internships and career guidance. 
  • WISE-KIRAN: The Women in Science and Engineering–Knowledge Involvement in Research Advancement through Nurturing scheme promotes women’s participation in science and technology research through fellowships and institutional programmes. 
    • During 2025–26, 180 women pursued PhDs, 606 women scientists received post-doctoral or translational research support, 26 senior scientists received R&D support and 29,443 schoolgirls received STEM support.  

How are Labour Reforms Promoting Safer Employment Opportunities for Women? 

  • The Government implemented four Labour Codes in November 2025, consolidating 29 existing labour laws to strengthen workers' rights, workplace safety, social security and employment opportunities. 
  • The four codes include the Code on Wages, 2019; Industrial Relations Code, 2020; Code on Social Security, 2020; and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020. 

Key Provisions for Women Workers 

  • Grievance Redressal: Ensures proportionate representation of women in Grievance Redressal Committees, strengthening their participation in resolving workplace disputes. 
  • Maternity Benefits: Eligible women employees receive up to 26 weeks of paid maternity leave, while eligible adoptive and commissioning mothers are entitled to 12 weeks. 
    • Medical Bonus: ₹3,500 where free prenatal and postnatal care is not provided by the employer. 
    • Nursing Breaks: Two nursing breaks during working hours until the child reaches 15 months of age. 
    • Crèche Facilities: Establishments with 50 or more employees must provide crèche facilities, allowing women four visits daily, including rest intervals. 
    • Work-from-Home Provision: Women may work from home after maternity leave where the nature of work permits, subject to mutual agreement with the employer. 
  • Expanded Employment Opportunities: Women can work in all establishments and sectors, including night shifts, subject to their consent and prescribed safety measures. 
  • Prohibition of Gender Discrimination: The Code on Wages, 2019 prohibits gender-based discrimination in recruitment, wages and employment conditions for the same or similar work, promoting equal pay for equal work. 
  • Representation in Policy-Making: Women must constitute at least one-third of the members of Central and State Advisory Boards, which advise on minimum wages and measures to enhance women's employment opportunities. 
  • Significance: These reforms aim to strengthen workplace equality, maternity protection, employment security and women's participation across sectors, supporting safer and more inclusive labour markets. 

Conclusion 

India's transition from increasing women's workforce participation to ensuring quality employment reflects a shift towards inclusive economic empowerment. Strengthening access to skills, finance, markets, social security and safe workplaces will enhance women's economic independence and entrepreneurship, advancing women-led development and the vision of Viksit Bharat 2047.

Dowry Deaths in India

16-07-2025

For Prelims: National Crime Records Bureau, Dowry Death, Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana 

For Mains: Dowry Deaths in India, Legal provisions under Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita related to dowry 

Source: TH 

Why in News? 

A surge in dowry-related deaths across states like Uttar Pradesh, Chandigarh, and Tamil Nadu shows the persistent grip of this illegal practice. Women continue to face harassment, assault, and suicide over dowry, while investigations drag on and convictions remain rare.

Dowry Death and Cruelty  

  • Dowry Death: According to the Section 80 of Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) A woman's death by burns, injury, or in unnatural circumstances within 7 years of marriage is considered dowry death if she faced cruelty or harassment over dowry soon before her death. 
    • Punishment: Minimum 7 years imprisonment, extendable to life. 
  • Cruelty: Under Section 86 of the BNS, cruelty is defined as any willful conduct likely to drive a woman to commit suicide or cause serious harm to her physical or mental health.  
    • It also includes harassment intended to force her or her family to meet unlawful demands for money or property, or harassment due to their failure to meet such demands. 

What are the Reasons for the Persistence of Dowry-related Deaths in India? 

  • Cultural Entitlement and Deep-rooted Traditions: Dowry, though illegal since 1961 under the Dowry Prohibition Act, continues to be treated as a social practice.  
    • Many families still see it as an essential part of marriage, often disguised as "gifts." In some sections of society, a girl’s worth is judged not by the life she builds, but by the dowry she brings.  
    • Cultural and societal pressures push families to meet rising dowry demands, which often leads to harassment, abuse, and even deaths. 
  • Extent of the Dowry Problem in India: Between 2017 and 2022, India saw an average of 7,000 dowry deaths annually, based on reports from the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB). 
    • The NCRB data is conservative, as many dowry deaths go unreported, underscoring the extent of the issue. 
    • A World Bank study covering 40,000 marriages in rural India from 1960 to 2008 revealed that dowry was paid in 95% of marriages. This shows how deep-rooted the practice remains. 
  • Gender Discrimination and Patriarchal Practices: Dowry is often used to assert control over women, who are perceived as a burden upon marriage. The demands from husbands and in-laws for additional dowries are rooted in patriarchal attitudes that value women as commodities. 
  • Normalizing Abuse: Laws like Section 80 and 85 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 make dowry-related deaths and harassment criminal offenses. Yet, the practice of dowry has normalized abuse in many households. Such violence often goes unreported, dismissed as a private family matter. 
    • Between 2017 and 2022, dowry was the main motive in over 6,100 murders across India.  
    • Over 60% occurred in West Bengal, Odisha, and Bihar. Together with Jharkhand, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Haryana, these States accounted for 80% of all dowry death cases during this period. 
    • This lack of awareness and ineffective implementation of laws makes it harder for women to break free from abusive relationships. 
  • Role of Economic Factors: Rising economic pressures contribute to the demand for higher dowries. The growing consumerism, social media influence, and the desire for a 'grand wedding' create an environment conducive to dowry expectations. 

Judicial Interventions 

  • Sanjay Kumar Jain v. State of Delhi (2011): The Supreme Court condemned the dowry system as a curse on Indian society and called for strong efforts to eliminate the rising menace of dowry deaths. 
  • K. Prema S. Rao v. Yadla Srinivasa Rao (2003): The Supreme Court stressed that stricter laws would only be effective if implemented seriously. It urged courts and enforcement agencies to act firmly to meet the intent of anti-dowry legislation. 
  • Satvir Singh & Ors v. State of Punjab (1998): The Punjab & Haryana High Court called dowry a stigma and social evil, stressing the urgent need for a shift in societal thinking and stronger laws to prevent dowry-related deaths. 
  • S. Gopal Reddy v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1996): The Supreme Court emphasized that the Dowry Prohibition Act targets both receiving and demanding dowry. Dowry, as a quid pro quo for marriage, is illegal and reflects deep-rooted gender bias.  

What are the Key Legal and Judicial Challenges in Dowry-related Cases in India? 

  • Inadequate Investigation and Slow Response: Only around 4,500 out of 7,000 dowry deaths reported each year lead to charge sheets.  
    • The rest remain stuck due to investigations being delayed, mishandled, or disposed of due to lack of evidence or false complaints. 
    • More than 67% of dowry deaths in 2022 had investigations pending for over six months, indicating systemic delays. 
  • Judicial Bottlenecks: Even after charge sheets are filed, over 90% of cases face delays in courts. With limited convictions (around 100 annually), cases often remain unresolved for years, leading to a sense of impunity among perpetrators. 
  • Lack of Coordination Between Police and Judiciary: Police often try to “resolve” dowry complaints through mediation, especially in small towns or villages. 
    • Additionally, the slow pace of charge sheet filing (70% after two months or more) and delays in court proceedings create a system where justice is not only delayed but sometimes denied. 
  • Underreporting and Victim-Blaming: Many dowry-related deaths go unreported due to fear of stigma, lack of legal awareness, and pressure from both society and family.  
    • Many women fear being disowned, and victims' families often hesitate to pursue justice in close-knit communities. 

 

What can be Done to Break the Cycle of Dowry-related Violence? 

  • Economic Empowerment: Empowering women financially is key to breaking the cycle of dowry violence. Laws like the Prevention of Child Marriage Act, 2006 and Right to Education Act, 2009 must be strictly enforced to keep girls in school and delay early marriage.  
    • Schemes like Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana, Mudra Yojana, and the National Skill Development Mission can give women the tools, savings, and skills needed for independence.  
      • When women earn and control resources, they’re better able to resist abuse and build their own future. 
  • Make Dowry Reporting Easier and Safer: Use tech-based platforms (apps, WhatsApp helplines) to allow anonymous complaints and track police response. Provide legal anonymity and protection for whistleblowers within families or communities. Safer reporting helps more women come forward. 
  • Strengthening Law Enforcement: Police must be trained to handle dowry-related cases with greater sensitivity and urgency. Timely investigation and stringent action against the accused must be prioritized. 
    • Dowry complaints must be registered and investigated as criminal offenses from the start. Treating these cases seriously sends a strong signal that dowry abuse isn’t negotiable, it's a crime. 
  • Create Real Exit Pathways for Women: Leaving abuse isn’t an option without support. Every district needs proper shelters with counseling, legal aid, and job help. Direct cash or income support must back women who walk out, so survival isn’t the next struggle. 
  • Judicial Reforms and Fast-Tracking Cases: Setting up fast-track courts dedicated to dowry death cases would expedite trials and reduce the delay in the justice system.  
  • Increase Awareness and Legal Support: Victims of dowry harassment should be provided with easier access to legal aid and counseling. 
    • Social awareness campaigns targeting the negative impacts of dowry could help change societal attitudes over time, reducing dowry-related violence.
